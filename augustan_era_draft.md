# AUGUSTAN ERA (44 BC -- 14 AD)

The men who stabbed Caesar in 44 BC believed they were saving the Republic. Instead they handed Rome to a sickly nineteen-year-old nobody had taken seriously. Octavian, Caesar's adopted son, avenged him at Philippi, shared the world with Mark Antony for a decade, and beat Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BC. In 27 BC he announced, with a perfectly straight face, that he was giving the Republic back; the grateful Senate gave him the name *Augustus* and, in practice, everything else. He ruled as *princeps*, "first citizen", for forty-one years, offering an exhausted Rome peace and a program to go with it: old gods, old morals, laws against adultery, and a city he claimed to have found in brick and left in marble. On his deathbed, says Suetonius, he asked his friends whether he had played his part in the comedy of life well, and if so, would they kindly applaud.

This is the **Golden Age** of Latin literature, the pinnacle of stylistic perfection. The neoterics had taught Roman poets to polish every line; the Augustans kept the polish and aimed it at the greatest Greek models. Virgil takes on Homer, Horace takes on Alcaeus, Sappho and Pindar. Rome no longer wants to translate Greece. It wants to equal it, and for once it arguably does.

The price is that the greatest poets of the era are **poets at the service of the state**. Maecenas, Augustus's friend and unofficial minister of culture, gathered Virgil, Horace and Propertius into his circle and gave them money and land. In return the regime got the *Aeneid*, an epic whose whole plot points towards Augustus, and Horace's *Carmen Saeculare*, the official hymn of the festival of 17 BC. Propaganda, in some sense, although nobody handed out scripts: the poets were friends of the regime and knew who was paying for the farm. When they did not want to write something, they used the *recusatio*, explaining in exquisite verse that they would love to sing Augustus's wars but their talent was, sadly, far too small. It is the most elegant way ever invented of saying no to an emperor.

Not everyone sang in the official key. The era's own Roman genre, **love elegy** (Gallus, Tibullus, Propertius, Ovid), was its counter-culture: the elegists refuse careers, war and marriage, and live as "slaves" to a mistress usually married to someone else. In a city passing laws against adultery, this was not a harmless hobby.

And that is the darker side of the Golden Age: Rome discovered **censorship**. In AD 8 Ovid, the most fashionable poet in the city, was banished to the Black Sea for *carmen et error*, "a poem and a mistake". The poem was the *Ars Amatoria*, a manual of seduction, and his books were removed from Rome's public libraries; he spent his last ten years begging to come home and never did. The historian Titus Labienus, so savage that people called him *Rabienus*, "the rabid one", had his books burned by order of the Senate, the first time Rome punished literature that way; he shut himself in his family tomb rather than outlive them. To be fair, Augustus censored himself too: he wrote a tragedy on Ajax, judged it bad and wiped it out with a sponge, joking that his Ajax had fallen on his sponge instead of his sword.

For a translator, the battlefield changes. In Caesar's era the enemy was Cicero's periodic sentence and Sallust's archaism; here it is mostly **verse**: the hexameter (Virgil, Ovid's *Metamorphoses*), the elegiac couplet (Tibullus, Sulpicia, Propertius) and the Greek lyric metres Horace brought to Rome. The archaisms are gone; in their place come free word order, poetic plurals and unexplained mythology. In Horace's *Quis multa gracilis te puer in rosa*, *multa* waits four words for *rosa*, and *te*, the girl, sits right between *gracilis* and *puer*, so the boy is literally wrapped around her. The result is a sharp spike in difficulty compared with Caesar's era: with so much of the literature written in verse, many of these authors are hard going, and Virgil and Horace above all. The prose is gentler, from Augustus's plain *Res Gestae* and Vitruvius's honest technical Latin to Livy's long, flowing periods.

The corpus is in excellent shape where it matters most. Virgil, Horace and Ovid survive complete, and Virgil only because Augustus refused his dying wish to burn the unfinished *Aeneid*. Livy survives in 35 of 142 books, the *Res Gestae* in a copy carved on a temple wall in Ankara. At the other extreme, Gallus, the founder of elegy, is down to about ten lines on a papyrus scrap found in Egypt in 1978.

# AUGUSTUS (63 BC -- 14 AD)

## Biography

Gaius Octavius was born in Rome on 23 September 63 BC, the year of Cicero's consulship and the Catilinarian conspiracy, into a respectable but unremarkable equestrian family from Velitrae, in the hills south of Rome. His father died when he was four. What made him remarkable was his mother, Atia, who was the niece of Julius Caesar. Caesar had no legitimate son, took an interest in the clever, sickly boy, and brought him along on campaign in Spain. In March 44 BC the eighteen-year-old Octavius was studying at Apollonia, across the Adriatic, when the news arrived: Caesar had been murdered, and his will adopted Octavius as his son and heir. Against the advice of his family, he went to Rome to claim the inheritance and, above all, the name. From now on he was Gaius Julius Caesar, and that name was worth more than any army.

**The boy who owed everything to his name.** Nobody took him seriously at first. Mark Antony, Caesar's right-hand man, called him "a boy who owes everything to his name". Cicero thought he could use him against Antony, and summed up his plan in a pun that somebody, inevitably, repeated to Octavian: the young man was to be *laudandum, ornandum, tollendum*, "praised, honoured, and lifted up", where *tollere* also means "got rid of". Octavian raised a private army out of Caesar's veterans, fought for the Senate against Antony at Mutina in 43 BC, and watched both consuls of the year, Hirtius and Pansa, die in the campaign (the same Hirtius who finished Caesar's *Gallic War*; ancient gossip, needless to say, blamed Octavian). Then he marched on Rome, had himself made consul at nineteen, and changed sides: he formed the Second Triumvirate with Antony and Lepidus, and the three drew up the proscription lists. Cicero's name was on them. So much for *tollendum*.

**Philippi, Actium, and a broken nose.** In 42 BC the triumvirs destroyed Brutus and Cassius at Philippi. Antony did the fighting; Octavian, who was ill, spent part of the battle hiding in a marsh for three days. For the next decade the Roman world was split: Antony took the East, and Cleopatra; Octavian took Italy and the West, the unpopular confiscations of land for veterans, and the war against Sextus Pompeius, which was won for him in 36 BC by his best friend and admiral, Marcus Agrippa. Lepidus was quietly pushed aside. The final showdown came at Actium on 2 September 31 BC, and again it was Agrippa who won it. The following year, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra killed themselves. Octavian went to see the embalmed body of Alexander the Great, touched it, and (says Cassius Dio) accidentally broke off a piece of the nose. Asked if he would also like to see the tombs of the Ptolemies, he said he had come to see a king, not a row of corpses.

**The restored Republic.** At thirty-three he was master of the world, and he had learned from Caesar's mistakes: Caesar had ruled openly as dictator and had been stabbed for it. So in January 27 BC Octavian solemnly gave the Republic back to the Senate and the People, and the Senate, which had no illusions about who was in charge, gave him the new name *Augustus*, "the revered one". Over the following decades he collected the powers of the old magistrates one by one (the powers of a tribune from 23 BC, the office of *pontifex maximus* in 12 BC, the title of *pater patriae* in 2 BC) without ever holding a title that sounded like "king". He called himself *princeps*, first among citizens, and the system he built, the Principate, lasted three centuries.

**The man.** Suetonius, who had access to the imperial archives, gives us a portrait that is wonderfully unheroic. Augustus was short and wore shoes with thick soles to look taller. His teeth were small, few and decaying. He was so afraid of thunderstorms that he carried a piece of sealskin as a lightning charm, and hid in an underground room when a storm got bad. He loved gambling with dice and wrote cheerful letters about losing. He was also capable of real despair: in AD 9, when Varus lost three legions in the forests of Germany, Augustus went for months without cutting his hair or beard, beating his head against the doors and shouting *Quintili Vare, legiones redde!*, "Quintilius Varus, give me back my legions!"

**The succession problem.** The one thing he could not arrange was an heir. He had a single child, Julia, and a long and genuinely devoted marriage to Livia, whom he had taken from her first husband while she was pregnant. One by one, every successor he chose died before him: his nephew Marcellus in 23 BC, Agrippa in 12 BC, his grandsons Lucius and Gaius in AD 2 and AD 4. He ended up adopting Livia's son Tiberius, whom he never much liked. Julia was exiled for adultery in 2 BC and her daughter, also Julia, in AD 8; Augustus used to call his daughter, his granddaughter and his last grandson "my three boils", and quoted Homer: "I wish I had never married, and died without children."

**The death, and the figs.** He died at Nola on 19 August AD 14, in the month that had already been renamed after him, a few weeks short of his seventy-sixth birthday. Suetonius gives him a perfect theatrical exit: he asked for a mirror, had his hair combed, asked his friends whether he had played his part in the comedy of life well, and quoted the Greek line that ends a play, "if the show has pleased you, give it your applause". His last words were to Livia: "Livia, live mindful of our marriage, and farewell." Cassius Dio reports a darker version. Augustus, who was careful about what he ate, liked to pick figs with his own hands straight from the trees in his garden. Livia, so the rumour went, smeared poison on the figs still hanging there, ate the clean ones herself, and let her husband eat the rest, to make sure Tiberius succeeded before Augustus could change his mind. Nobody can prove it, and Dio himself only reports it as something "some suspected", but it has been too good a story to die. The Senate made him a god, helped by a former praetor who swore he had seen Augustus's spirit rising from the funeral pyre into the sky; Dio adds that Livia rewarded him with a million sesterces for his excellent eyesight.

## Most Important Works

- **Res Gestae Divi Augusti** (*The Achievements of the Divine Augustus*, finished AD 13-14) -- Augustus's own account of his career in thirty-five short chapters, written in the first person in his seventy-sixth year and left with his will, with orders to have it inscribed on two bronze pillars in front of his mausoleum. Modern editors divide it into three parts: the honours he received (chapters 1-14), the money he spent on the Roman people (15-24), and his deeds in war and peace (25-35), with a short appendix added after his death. The bronze originals are lost; the text survives because copies were carved on temples in the province of Galatia, above all the Temple of Rome and Augustus at Ankara. [Complete]
- **De Vita Sua** (*On His Own Life*) -- an autobiography in thirteen books, taking the story up to the Cantabrian war in Spain (around 25 BC). [Lost]
- **Letters** -- Augustus was a lively and prolific letter-writer, and Suetonius and Aulus Gellius quote him often, which is how we know about his spelling habits and his dice games. [Fragmentary]
- **Literary experiments** -- a hexameter poem called *Sicilia*, a book of epigrams written mostly in the bath, a reply to Brutus's praise of Cato, and a tragedy on Ajax, which he destroyed himself: when asked how his Ajax was doing, he said it had "fallen on its sponge". [Lost]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Augustus had firm opinions about Latin style, and Suetonius records them gleefully. He wanted above all to be clear. He avoided what he called "the stench of far-fetched words", mocked Maecenas for his perfumed, curling prose, mocked Tiberius for digging up obsolete words, and mocked Antony for writing in a style that people admired rather than understood. He repeated prepositions rather than leave any doubt about the construction. His everyday speech was full of homely expressions: someone who would never pay would pay "on the Greek Kalends", a quick job was done "faster than asparagus cooks".

The *Res Gestae* is that taste for clarity carved in stone. It is official, inscriptional prose: short sentences, almost always in the first person, almost always ending on a perfect-tense verb (*comparavi*, *vindicavi*, *feci*, *refeci*, *dedi*). The syntax is the gentlest of any author so far after Nepos. The main constructions are the ablative absolute, used constantly for dates (*C. Pansa et A. Hirtio consulibus*, "when Pansa and Hirtius were consuls"), relative clauses, a few subordinate clauses with *cum* and *ut*, and lists, lists, lists. **Syntactic complexity: LOW; Stylistic figures: LOW.**

The real difficulty is elsewhere. The first is **lexicon**: the text is full of the technical vocabulary of Roman institutions (*tribunicia potestas*, *imperium*, *ovatio*, *supplicatio*, *lustrum*, *congiarium*, *aurum coronarium*) and of Roman numbers, written the Roman way: distributives like *trecenos*, "three hundred each", multiplicative adverbs like *quinquiens* and *viciens et semel*, and sums in sesterces such as *HS milliens*, which means a hundred million. A student who has never met these will spend more time with the dictionary than with the grammar. **Lexicon: MEDIUM.** The second is **context**. The *Res Gestae* is propaganda by omission, and it is a masterpiece of the genre. Antony, Brutus and Cassius, Lepidus and Cleopatra are never named; the proscriptions are never mentioned; civil wars become "wars against the enemies of the Republic", and Sextus Pompeius becomes a pirate. To translate it is easy. To understand what it is not saying, you need to know the history of the previous thirty years. **Allusion density: MEDIUM to HIGH, but historical rather than literary.**

This is exactly why the *Res Gestae* is the best possible way into the Augustan Era. In thirty-five short chapters it gives you the whole political frame that the poets of the period take for granted: the civil wars, the victory, the "restored Republic", the buildings, the games, the conquests, the peace, and the man at the centre of it all. Virgil, Horace, Livy and Ovid are much easier to understand once you have read the official version they are writing with, around or against. It is also an ideal text for brushing up on prose before the era's poetry hits: clean classical Latin, regular word order, standard constructions, and a sense of moving quickly through a real text. Read it first.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Res Gestae 1-3 -- Part I: the Rise to Power

**Source:** Augustus, *Res Gestae Divi Augusti*, chapters 1-3, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/augustus/resgestae.html)

**Context:** The opening of the *Res Gestae* and of its first part, on the honours Augustus received. In three chapters he covers the years from 44 to about 30 BC: the private army he raised after Caesar's murder, the war of Mutina, his first consulship, the triumvirate, Philippi and the civil wars. That is, the most violent fourteen years of his life. Read it with the biography above open beside it, and count how many of those events are actually named.

**Latin Text:**

[1] Annos undeviginti natus exercitum privato consilio et privata impensa comparavi, per quem rem publicam a dominatione factionis oppressam in libertatem vindicavi. [Ob quae] senatus decretis honorificis in ordinem suum me adlegit, C. Pansa et A. Hirtio consulibus, consularem locum sententiae dicendae tribuens, et imperium mihi dedit. Res publica ne quid detrimenti caperet, me propraetore simul cum consulibus providere iussit. Populus autem eodem anno me consulem, cum cos. uterque bello cecidisset, et triumvirum rei publicae constituendae creavit.

[2] Qui parentem meum trucidaverunt, eos in exilium expuli iudiciis legitimis ultus eorum facinus, et postea bellum inferentis rei publicae vici bis acie.

[3] Bella terra et mari civilia externaque toto in orbe terrarum saepe gessi, victorque omnibus veniam petentibus civibus peperci. Externas gentes, quibus tuto ignosci potuit, conservare quam excidere malui. Millia civium Romanorum sub sacramento meo fuerunt circiter quingenta. Ex quibus deduxi in colonias aut remisi in municipia sua stipendis emeritis millia aliquanto plura quam trecenta, et iis omnibus agros adsignavi aut pecuniam pro praemiis militiae dedi. Naves cepi sescentas praeter eas, si quae minores quam triremes fuerunt.

**English Translation:**

[1] At the age of nineteen, on my own initiative and at my own expense, I raised an army, with which I set free the Republic, oppressed by the tyranny of a faction. For this the Senate, with honorific decrees, enrolled me in its order in the consulship of Gaius Pansa and Aulus Hirtius, giving me the right to speak among the former consuls, and it granted me military command. It ordered me, as propraetor, to see to it together with the consuls that the Republic should suffer no harm. And in the same year, since both consuls had fallen in war, the people made me consul and triumvir for the restoration of the Republic.

[2] Those who butchered my father I drove into exile, avenging their crime through lawful courts; and afterwards, when they made war on the Republic, I defeated them twice in battle.

[3] I waged wars, civil and foreign, by land and sea, all over the world, many times, and as victor I spared all citizens who asked for mercy. Foreign peoples who could safely be pardoned I preferred to preserve rather than destroy. About five hundred thousand Roman citizens were bound to me by the military oath. Of these I settled in colonies, or sent back to their own towns when their service was over, somewhat more than three hundred thousand, and to all of them I assigned land or gave money as a reward for their service. I captured six hundred ships, not counting any that were smaller than triremes.

**Analysis:**

*Annos undeviginti natus*, "at the age of nineteen": the first words of the text are not a name or a title but an age, and they set the tone of everything that follows. The boy that Antony and Cicero underestimated becomes the young saviour who acted when nobody else would. Look at how he presents the facts. A nineteen-year-old raising an army of his own, *privato consilio et privata impensa*, was flatly illegal: no magistrate had authorised it. Augustus turns that illegality into a virtue, repeating *privato... privata* so that it sounds like personal generosity. The "faction" whose tyranny oppressed the Republic is Antony's, but Antony is never named, here or anywhere else in the text. Nor are the consuls' deaths explained (*cum cos. uterque bello cecidisset*, a simple fact of war), nor what the *triumvir rei publicae constituendae* actually did in his first months in office: the proscriptions, and the murder of Cicero, simply do not exist.

The phrase *Res publica ne quid detrimenti caperet*, "that the Republic should suffer no harm", is a legal formula: the wording of the Senate's emergency decree, the *senatus consultum ultimum*. It is the same decree that Caesar complains about in *Bellum Civile* I.7, used this time in favour of a Caesar rather than against one. Augustus quotes it because it makes his private army the Senate's army.

Chapter 2 is a small masterpiece of silence. *Qui parentem meum trucidaverunt*, "those who butchered my father": Brutus and Cassius go unnamed, and the verb *trucidare*, a violent, butcher's word, is the only emotional word in the passage. *Iudiciis legitimis* refers to the special court set up in 43 BC that condemned the assassins in their absence; *bis acie* is the two battles of Philippi, one of which Augustus spent in a marsh.

Chapter 3 introduces the great theme of the reign, *clementia*: he spared the citizens who asked, and preferred to preserve foreign peoples "who could safely be pardoned", a very large qualification hidden in a relative clause. Then come the numbers, which are the real challenge for a translator: *millia... circiter quingenta*, where *millia* is separated from its numeral by four words, and *millia aliquanto plura quam trecenta*, "somewhat more than three hundred thousand".

Grammatically, the passage is a good revision test. Note the long line of first-person perfects (*comparavi, vindicavi, expuli, vici, gessi, peperci, malui, deduxi, remisi, adsignavi, dedi, cepi*); the ablatives absolute (*C. Pansa et A. Hirtio consulibus*, *stipendis emeritis*); the deponent participle *ultus*, "having avenged"; the old accusative plural *inferentis* for *inferentes*; *si quae* for *si aliquae*; and above all *quibus tuto ignosci potuit*. Since *ignosco* takes the dative, its passive is impersonal: literally "to whom it was possible to be forgiven safely", that is, "who could safely be pardoned". That one clause will catch more students than the rest of the passage put together.

### Excerpt 2: Res Gestae 20-21 -- Part II: the Builder of Rome

**Source:** Augustus, *Res Gestae Divi Augusti*, chapters 20-21, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/augustus/resgestae.html)

**Context:** From the second part of the *Res Gestae*, the *impensae*: the money Augustus spent on the Roman people out of his own pocket. Chapters 15-18 list cash handouts and grain, chapter 19 lists new buildings, and chapters 20-21 cover restorations and the great monuments paid for out of war booty. This is the Augustus who boasted, according to Suetonius, that he had found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble, and many of the buildings named here can still be visited in Rome today.

**Latin Text:**

[20] Capitolium et Pompeium theatrum utrumque opus impensa grandi refeci sine ulla inscriptione nominis mei. Rivos aquarum compluribus locis vetustate labentes refeci, et aquam quae Marcia appellatur duplicavi fonte novo in rivum eius inmisso. Forum Iulium et basilicam quae fuit inter aedem Castoris et aedem Saturni, coepta profligataque opera a patre meo, perfeci et eandem basilicam consumptam incendio, ampliato eius solo, sub titulo nominis filiorum meorum incohavi, et, si vivus non perfecissem, perfici ab heredibus meis iussi. Duo et octoginta templa deum in urbe consul sextum ex auctoritate senatus refeci nullo praetermisso quod eo tempore refici debebat. Consul septimum viam Flaminiam ab urbe Ariminum refeci pontesque omnes praeter Mulvium et Minucium.

[21] In privato solo Martis Ultoris templum forumque Augustum ex manibiis feci. Theatrum ad aedem Apollinis in solo magna ex parte a privatis empto feci, quod sub nomine M. Marcelli generi mei esset. Dona ex manibiis in Capitolio et in aede divi Iuli et in aede Apollinis et in aede Vestae et in templo Martis Ultoris consacravi, quae mihi constiterunt HS circiter milliens. Auri coronari pondo triginta et quinque millia municipiis et colonis Italiae conferentibus ad triumphos meos quintum consul remisi, et postea, quotienscumque imperator appellatus sum, aurum coronarium non accepi decernentibus municipiis et colonis aeque benigne adque antea decreverant.

**English Translation:**

[20] The Capitol and the theatre of Pompey, both of them, I restored at great expense without any inscription of my own name. I restored the channels of the aqueducts, which in many places were collapsing with age, and I doubled the aqueduct called the Marcia by bringing a new spring into its channel. The Forum of Julius and the basilica that stood between the temple of Castor and the temple of Saturn, works begun and nearly finished by my father, I completed; and when that same basilica was destroyed by fire, I began to rebuild it on an enlarged site, under the name of my sons, and gave orders that, if I did not finish it in my lifetime, it should be finished by my heirs. In my sixth consulship, on the authority of the Senate, I restored eighty-two temples of the gods in the city, leaving out none that needed restoring at the time. In my seventh consulship I restored the Via Flaminia from Rome to Ariminum, and all its bridges except the Mulvian and the Minucian.

[21] On land I owned privately, and with the spoils of war, I built the temple of Mars the Avenger and the Forum of Augustus. Next to the temple of Apollo, on ground bought for the most part from private owners, I built a theatre, to bear the name of my son-in-law Marcus Marcellus. With the spoils of war I dedicated gifts on the Capitol and in the temple of the Deified Julius, in the temple of Apollo, in the temple of Vesta and in the temple of Mars the Avenger, which cost me about a hundred million sesterces. Thirty-five thousand pounds of crown gold, which the towns and colonies of Italy were contributing for my triumphs, I remitted in my fifth consulship; and afterwards, whenever I was hailed as *imperator*, I did not accept the crown gold, although the towns and colonies voted it just as generously as they had before.

**Analysis:**

The first sentence is the best joke in the *Res Gestae*, although Augustus did not intend it as one. He restored the Capitol and Pompey's theatre "without any inscription of my own name", and he tells us so in an inscription of his own name, set up in front of his own mausoleum. Modesty, in Augustan Rome, was something you advertised. The choice of buildings is not innocent either: the Capitol was the religious heart of the state, and the theatre of Pompey had been built by the man his adoptive father defeated. Restoring it without his name was a public gesture of reconciliation.

The passage works like a rhythmic inventory. Almost every clause ends on the same verb or one of its family, *refeci... refeci... perfeci... incohavi... refeci... refeci... feci... feci*, so that the city seems to rise again sentence by sentence. Behind the inventory there is a family history. *A patre meo* is Caesar again, whose unfinished projects Augustus completes as a son should. *Sub titulo nominis filiorum meorum*, "under the name of my sons", refers to Gaius and Lucius, his grandsons and adopted sons, both dead by the time he wrote this. The theatre in the name of Marcellus, the nephew and son-in-law who died at nineteen in 23 BC, is the same young man Virgil mourns at the end of *Aeneid* VI. And the temple of *Mars Ultor*, Mars the Avenger, was vowed at Philippi: it is chapter 2's revenge for the "butchered father", turned into marble.

The last sentence is about refusing money, which is the most Augustan kind of boast. *Aurum coronarium*, "crown gold", was the gold that towns traditionally sent a victorious general to make his triumphal crowns. Augustus refused thirty-five thousand pounds of it, and the ablative absolute at the end, *decernentibus municipiis et colonis aeque benigne adque antea decreverant*, makes sure we know that the towns kept offering it.

For the translator, the passage is a lesson in Latin word order and in compact constructions rather than in hard grammar. Watch for the objects placed first, before the verb they depend on (*Capitolium et Pompeium theatrum... refeci*, *Forum Iulium et basilicam... perfeci*), with *utrumque opus* in apposition; the participles *coepta profligataque*, "begun and nearly finished" (*profligare* here means "to bring almost to completion", a sense the dictionary gives second); the passive infinitive after *iussi*, *perfici ab heredibus meis iussi*, "I ordered it to be finished by my heirs"; the pluperfect subjunctive *si vivus non perfecissem*, a condition in reported form; the relative clause of purpose *quod sub nomine M. Marcelli... esset*; *Ariminum* without a preposition, because names of towns take a plain accusative of motion; the adverbial numerals *consul sextum*, *consul septimum*, *quintum consul*; and the old spelling *adque* for *atque*. Then the numbers again: *HS circiter milliens* is about a hundred million sesterces, and *pondo triginta et quinque millia* is thirty-five thousand pounds by weight.

## Legacy and Impact

Augustus's name is still on every calendar: in 8 BC the Senate renamed the month of Sextilis after him, and it has been August ever since. His title became a rank. Every Roman emperor after him was an *Augustus*, just as every one of them was a *Caesar*, and the model of power he invented, a monarchy that insists it is not one, has been imitated by rulers ever since.

The *Res Gestae* itself had an unusual afterlife. The bronze pillars in Rome disappeared, and the text survived only because the province of Galatia, in modern Turkey, had copies carved on its temples. The most complete, in Latin with a Greek translation, is on the walls of the Temple of Rome and Augustus at Ankara, the *Monumentum Ancyranum*, which was copied by a Western embassy to the Ottoman court in 1555. Two other copies, at Antioch in Pisidia and Apollonia, have filled most of the gaps. The great German historian Theodor Mommsen called it the *regina inscriptionum*, the queen of Latin inscriptions.

In 1938, for the two-thousandth anniversary of Augustus's birth, Mussolini had the whole text carved on the wall of the pavilion built to house the reconstructed Ara Pacis, beside the Mausoleum of Augustus in Rome. It is still there, on the side of the modern museum facing Via di Ripetta: the propaganda of one regime, reused by another. Few texts have been used so successfully, twice, for exactly the purpose they were written for.

## Final Rating

**Tier: D (Good Exercise)**

The *Res Gestae* is short, complete, and written in clean, regular classical prose: first-person statements, standard constructions, almost no subordination, almost no figures of speech. Its only real obstacles are institutional vocabulary and Roman numerals, both solved with a good dictionary and a little patience, and its political silences, which make it harder to *understand* than to translate. It is the right text to open the Augustan Era with: it gives the reader the historical background that every other author of the period assumes, and it is a comfortable prose warm-up before the era's poetry, which is a very different matter. A clear D, and one of the most useful texts in the whole project.

# MARCUS VITRUVIUS POLLIO -- VITRUVIUS (c. 80 -- c. 15 BC)

## Biography

For the man who wrote the most influential book on architecture in Western history, we know remarkably little about Vitruvius, and almost all of it comes from his own prefaces. Even his name is uncertain: *Vitruvius* is secure, but *Marcus* rests on weak evidence and *Pollio* comes from a later writer who summarised him. He was probably born in the 80s or 70s BC, possibly in Campania or in central Italy, and he tells us that his parents gave him a broad education in the liberal arts, for which he is still grateful.

**Caesar's artillery man.** As a young man he served Julius Caesar as a military engineer. Together with three colleagues, whom he names, he was in charge of building and repairing *ballistae*, *scorpiones* and the other siege engines of the Roman army, which makes him the Roman equivalent of an artillery officer. Where exactly he served we do not know, though Gaul and Africa are the usual guesses. A German scholar once tried to identify him with Mamurra, Caesar's chief engineer, whom Catullus mocks in some of his crudest poems as a corrupt, bankrupt playboy. Almost nobody accepts the theory, which is a pity, because it would be the best-documented career change in Latin literature.

**A pension from Octavia.** After Caesar's death he passed into the service of Octavian, and here comes the most charming detail of his life. In the dedication of his book he thanks the young Caesar for a pension that allowed him to work without worrying about poverty in his old age, and he adds that the pension was kept up "on the recommendation of your sister", Octavia. The great theorist of Roman architecture, in other words, owed his comfortable retirement to a reference letter from the emperor's sister.

**The ugly architect.** Vitruvius is also the only Roman author who opens a book by apologising for his face. In the preface to Book II he tells the story of Dinocrates, a tall, handsome Greek architect who, unable to get an audience with Alexander the Great, oiled himself, put on a lion skin, crowned himself with poplar leaves, took a club and walked into the king's assembly dressed as Hercules. It worked: Alexander noticed him, and Dinocrates went on to plan the city of Alexandria (after first proposing to carve Mount Athos into a giant man holding a city in his hand, which Alexander politely declined). Vitruvius then turns to Augustus and admits: "nature has not given me height, age has disfigured my face, and illness has taken away my strength". Since he cannot win the emperor over with his looks, he will have to do it with his knowledge. It is the most endearing self-portrait in Latin prose.

**One building.** For all his theory, Vitruvius mentions only one building of his own: a basilica at Fanum Fortunae, modern Fano on the Adriatic coast, which he describes in detail in Book V. For centuries architects tried to reconstruct it from his description, and nobody knew where it stood. In recent years excavations in the centre of Fano have uncovered remains that some archaeologists believe may finally be his basilica, although the identification is still debated. He wrote the *De Architectura* in the 20s BC, as an old man, and dedicated it to Augustus. After that he disappears, and we do not know when he died.

## Most Important Works

- **De Architectura** (*On Architecture*, c. 30-20 BC) -- ten books, dedicated to Augustus, covering everything a Roman architect was expected to know. Book I deals with the education of the architect and the planning of cities; Book II with building materials (and the origins of human housing); Books III and IV with temples and the architectural orders; Book V with public buildings, including the acoustics of theatres; Book VI with private houses; Book VII with plaster, stucco, floors and pigments; Book VIII with water and aqueducts (where he warns that lead pipes are bad for your health, two thousand years ahead of schedule); Book IX with astronomy and sundials; and Book X with machines, from water wheels to siege engines. The text survives complete, but the illustrations he refers to throughout (*subscripta forma*, "a diagram is drawn below") were lost, which has kept readers guessing for two millennia. [Complete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Vitruvius knew perfectly well that he was not a great writer, and he said so. At the end of the first chapter of Book I he asks Caesar and his readers to forgive him if anything is "not quite according to the rules of grammar", because he has written "not as a great philosopher, nor as an eloquent rhetorician, nor as a grammarian trained in the highest principles of the art, but as an architect who has had a taste of letters". This is not false modesty. His Latin is the Latin of a practical man with a technical subject and no literary training: honest, functional and frequently clumsy.

The first difficulty is the **lexicon**, which is enormous and highly technical. Vitruvius needs words for column bases, capitals, cornices, mouldings, types of brick and sand, kinds of lime, the parts of catapults and water organs, and Latin does not have most of them. So he borrows from Greek wholesale, sometimes translating, sometimes not: *symmetria*, *analogia*, *eurythmia*, *taxis*, *gnomonice*, *teleon*. A student meets a new term in almost every line, and many of them are not in a school dictionary. **Lexicon: HIGH.**

The second is **syntax**, which is difficult in a different way from Cicero's or Sallust's. Their sentences are hard because they are built with great art; Vitruvius's sentences are hard because they are not quite built at all. They run on, pile up clauses, change construction halfway through, and occasionally lose track of their subject. The great Renaissance architect Leon Battista Alberti, who owed him almost everything, still complained that Vitruvius wrote in such a way that the Latins thought he wanted to sound Greek and the Greeks thought he was speaking Latin. Add the lost diagrams, which leave several descriptions of machines nearly impossible to visualise, and you get a text that is easy to parse sentence by sentence and surprisingly hard to understand paragraph by paragraph. **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM.**

In everything else he is gentle. He uses almost no rhetorical figures, his allusions are to Greek scientists and mathematicians rather than to myth or poetry, and he likes to break the technical material with good stories: Dinocrates dressed as Hercules, Archimedes running naked through Syracuse, the origins of the Corinthian capital in a basket left on a young girl's grave, around which an acanthus plant grew. **Stylistic figures: LOW; Allusion density: LOW to MEDIUM.** And he writes prose, which in the Augustan Era is already a gift.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: De Architectura I.3.1-2 -- Firmitas, Utilitas, Venustas

**Source:** Vitruvius, *De Architectura*, Book I, chapter 3, sections 1-2, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vitruvius/vitruvius1.html)

**Context:** After two chapters on what an architect must study and on the basic concepts of the discipline, Vitruvius divides architecture into its parts, and then sets out the three requirements that every building must meet. Those three words, *firmitas, utilitas, venustas*, are the most famous sentence ever written about architecture. In the seventeenth century the English diplomat Sir Henry Wotton translated them as "commodity, firmness and delight", and they are still taught in every school of architecture today.

**Latin Text:**

[1] Partes ipsius architecturae sunt tres: aedificatio, gnomonice, machinatio. Aedificatio autem divisa est bipertito, e quibus una est moenium et communium operum in publicis locis conlocatio, altera est privatorum aedificiorum explicatio. Publicorum autem distributiones sunt tres, e quibus est una defensionis, altera religionis, tertia opportunitatis. Defensionis est murorum turriumque et portarum ratio ad hostium impetus perpetuo repellendos excogitata, religionis deorum immortalium fanorum aediumque sacrarum conlocatio, opportunitatis communium locorum ad usum publicum dispositio, uti portus, fora, porticus, balinea, theatra, inambulationes ceteraque, quae isdem rationibus in publicis locis designantur.

[2] Haec autem ita fieri debent, ut habeatur ratio firmitatis, utilitatis, venustatis. Firmitatis erit habita ratio, cum fuerit fundamentorum ad solidum depressio, quaque e materia, copiarum sine avaritia diligens electio; utilitatis autem, [cum fuerit] emendata et sine inpeditione usus locorum dispositio et ad regiones sui cuiusque generis apta et conmoda distributio; venustatis vero, cum fuerit operis species grata et elegans membrorumque commensus iustas habeat symmetriarum ratiocinationes.

**English Translation:**

[1] Architecture itself has three parts: building, gnomonics (the making of sundials) and mechanics. Building in turn is divided into two: one part is the placing of city walls and public works in public spaces, the other is the construction of private buildings. Public buildings are of three kinds: one for defence, another for religion, the third for convenience. Defence means the planning of walls, towers and gates, designed to drive back enemy attacks for all time; religion, the siting of the shrines and sacred temples of the immortal gods; convenience, the layout of common spaces for public use, such as harbours, forums, porticoes, baths, theatres, promenades and all the rest that are designed on the same principles in public places.

[2] All of these must be built in such a way that account is taken of strength, utility and beauty. Account will have been taken of strength when the foundations have been dug down to solid ground and the materials, whatever they are, have been chosen with care and without stinginess; of utility, when the arrangement of the spaces is faultless and allows them to be used without obstruction, and each type of building is placed suitably and conveniently with respect to its orientation; and of beauty, when the appearance of the work is pleasing and elegant, and the proportions of its parts follow the correct calculations of symmetry.

**Analysis:**

This is Vitruvius at his most organised, and it shows how his mind works: by division. Architecture has three parts; building has two; public buildings have three kinds; each kind gets a definition in the genitive (*defensionis est... religionis... opportunitatis...*), with the verb *est* understood after the first. It reads less like an essay than like a table of contents spoken aloud, which is exactly what it is.

Then comes the famous sentence: *ut habeatur ratio firmitatis, utilitatis, venustatis*. Three abstract nouns in *-tas*, in asyndeton, all depending on *ratio*, "account, consideration". Notice that Vitruvius does not say that a building must *be* strong, useful and beautiful, but that the builder must *take account* of strength, utility and beauty. Architecture, for him, is a matter of method. Each quality is then defined with a future perfect (*erit habita ratio, cum fuerit...*), "account will have been taken when there will have been...", the tense of the instruction manual: when you have done this, you will have achieved that. The definition of *venustas* is the one that matters most for the rest of the book: beauty is not taste, it is proportion, *symmetriarum ratiocinationes*, a matter of calculation. That idea leads straight to Excerpt 2.

This passage also shows why Vitruvius needs a careful translator. *Gnomonice* is a Greek word for the science of sundials; *machinatio* covers everything from cranes to catapults; *ad regiones* refers to the orientation of a building towards the points of the compass and the winds, a subject he has just discussed in chapter 2. And *quaque e materia* ("from whatever material") is dropped into the middle of the clause with no grammatical connection, a typical Vitruvian shortcut.

A note on the text: The Latin Library prints *turrimque* for *turriumque*, *distrtbutio* for *distributio*, and leaves out two pieces of punctuation (the comma after *aedificatio* and the break before *venustatis*). All four are plain printing errors and have been corrected here, checked against Krohn's edition on LacusCurtius. The square brackets around *cum fuerit* are the editor's: the words are not in the manuscripts but are needed for the sense.

### Excerpt 2: De Architectura III.1.2-3 -- The Vitruvian Man

**Source:** Vitruvius, *De Architectura*, Book III, chapter 1, sections 2-3, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vitruvius/vitruvius3.html)

**Context:** Book III is about temples, and Vitruvius opens it by explaining that a temple must be built with *symmetria*, the correct proportion of every part to the whole. To show what he means, he turns to the most perfectly proportioned thing in nature: the human body. Around 1490 Leonardo da Vinci read this passage, took a pen, and drew a man inside a circle and a square, with a note in his mirror-writing that opens by naming "Vetruvio architecto" as his source. The drawing, now in Venice, is probably the most reproduced image in the history of art, and it is on the Italian one-euro coin.

**Latin Text:**

[2] Corpus enim hominis ita natura composuit, uti os capitis a mento ad frontem summam et radices imas capilli esset decimae partis, item manus palma ab articulo ad extremum medium digitum tantundem, caput a mento ad summum verticem octavae, cum cervicibus imis ab summo pectore ad imas radices capillorum sextae, <a medio pectore> ad summum verticem quartae. Ipsius autem oris altitudinis tertia est pars ab imo mento ad imas nares, nasum ab imis naribus ad finem medium superciliorum tantundem, ab ea fine ad imas radices capilli frons efficitur item tertiae partis. Pes vero altitudinis corporis sextae, cubitum quartae, pectus item quartae. Reliqua quoque membra suas habent commensus proportiones, quibus etiam antiqui pictores et statuarii nobiles usi magnas et infinitas laudes sunt adsecuti.

[3] Similiter vero sacrarum aedium membra ad universam totius magnitudinis summam ex partibus singulis convenientissimum debent habere commensus responsum. Item corporis centrum medium naturaliter est umbilicus. Namque si homo conlocatus fuerit supinus manibus et pedibus pansis circinique conlocatum centrum in umbilico eius, circumagendo rotundationem utrarumque manuum et pedum digiti linea tangentur. Non minus quemadmodum schema rotundationis in corpore efficitur, item quadrata designatio in eo invenietur. Nam si a pedibus imis ad summum caput mensum erit eaque mensura relata fuerit ad manus pansas, invenietur eadem latitudo uti altitudo, quemadmodum areae quae ad normam sunt quadratae.

**English Translation:**

[2] For nature has composed the human body in such a way that the face, from the chin to the top of the forehead and the lowest roots of the hair, is a tenth of the whole height; the palm of the hand, from the wrist to the tip of the middle finger, the same; the head, from the chin to the crown, an eighth; from the top of the chest, including the base of the neck, to the lowest roots of the hair, a sixth; from the middle of the chest to the crown, a quarter. Of the height of the face itself, one third runs from the bottom of the chin to the bottom of the nostrils; the nose, from the bottom of the nostrils to the point between the eyebrows, is the same; and from that point to the lowest roots of the hair, the forehead also makes up a third. The foot is a sixth of the height of the body, the forearm a quarter, the chest also a quarter. The other limbs, too, have their own proportions, and it was by using them that the famous painters and sculptors of antiquity won great and boundless praise.

[3] In the same way, the parts of sacred temples must have, from each single part up to the total size of the whole, the most harmonious correspondence of measurements. Likewise, the natural centre of the body is the navel. For if a man is laid on his back with his hands and feet spread out, and the point of a compass is placed on his navel, then by drawing a circle around him the fingers of both his hands and the toes of both his feet will touch the line. And just as a circle can be traced on the body, a square will also be found in it. For if you measure from the soles of the feet to the top of the head, and set that measure against the outstretched hands, the width will be found to be the same as the height, just as in surfaces squared off with a set square.

**Analysis:**

The first section is a list of fractions, and it is a fine exercise in the genitive of quality and measure: *esset decimae partis*, "was of a tenth part", then *octavae*, *sextae*, *quartae*, with *partis* understood each time and the verb *esset* carried silently through the whole series. Vitruvius never repeats what he can leave out, which is the main reason the passage looks harder than it is. Read *est* or *esset* and *partis* into every clause and it falls into place.

The second section contains the image that made the passage immortal. A man lying on his back, arms and legs spread out, a compass on his navel: the circle touches his fingers and toes. Then, measured from head to foot and from fingertip to fingertip, the same body fits into a square. The circle and the square were, for ancient and Renaissance thinkers alike, the two perfect geometrical figures, so the human body turns out to be the meeting point of geometry and nature. And since temples must be proportioned like bodies, a well-built temple is a kind of portrait of man. Leonardo's famous drawing is, quite literally, an illustration of these two sentences, and a correction of them: Leonardo noticed that if the man is to fit both figures, the circle and the square cannot share a centre, so he moved the square down and put its centre at the groin.

Grammatically, watch the future perfects again (*conlocatus fuerit*, *mensum erit*, *relata fuerit*), the tense of instructions; the gerund *circumagendo*, "by turning (the compass) round", which has no expressed object; *pansis*, from *pando*, a rarer participle form than *passis*; and the impersonal *mensum erit*, "it will have been measured", which Italian and English both turn into "if you measure".

A note on the text: The Latin Library has three misprints here: *ReIiqua* (with a capital I) for *Reliqua*, *vertices* for *verticem* in the first sentence, and *conlocantum*, which is not a word, for *conlocatum*. All three are corrected, as confirmed by Krohn's edition on LacusCurtius. The angle brackets around *a medio pectore* are the editor's: the words are restored to fill a gap in the manuscripts.

### Excerpt 3: De Architectura IX, Praefatio 9-10 -- Eureka!

**Source:** Vitruvius, *De Architectura*, Book IX, preface, sections 9-10, from *LacusCurtius* (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/9*.html), which reproduces F. Krohn's Teubner edition (1912). The Latin Library's text of this passage is unusually corrupt (it prints *Nimium* for *nimirum* and *verius* for *versus*, and omits Archimedes' Greek words altogether), so LacusCurtius is used instead. Sentence-initial capitals have been added, since Krohn prints the text in lower case throughout.

**Context:** Each book of the *De Architectura* has a preface, and Vitruvius uses them for his best stories. Book IX, on astronomy and sundials, opens with a reflection on how athletes are showered with honours while the thinkers who truly benefit humanity are forgotten. To prove the point, he tells stories about great thinkers. The first two are about Plato and Pythagoras; the third is this one, the oldest surviving version of the most famous story in the history of science.

**Latin Text:**

[9] Archimedis vero cum multa miranda inventa et varia fuerint, ex omnibus etiam infinita sollertia id quod exponam videtur esse expressum. Nimirum Hiero Syracusis auctus regia potestate, rebus bene gestis cum auream coronam votivam diis inmortalibus in quodam fano constituisset ponendam, manupretio locavit faciendam et aurum ad sacoma adpendit redemptori. Is ad tempus opus manu factum subtiliter regi adprobavit et ad sacoma pondus coronae visus est praestitisse.

[10] Posteaquam indicium est factum dempto auro tantundem argenti in id coronarium opus admixtum esse, indignatus Hiero se contemptum esse neque inveniens qua ratione id furtum deprehenderet, rogavit Archimeden uti insumeret sibi de eo cogitationem. Tunc is cum haberet eius rei curam, casu venit in balineum ibique cum in solium descenderet, animadvertit quantum corporis sui in eo insideret tantum aquae extra solium effluere. Idque cum eius rei rationem explicationis ostendisset, non est moratus sed exsiluit gaudio motus de solio et nudus vadens domum versus significabat clara voce invenisse quod quaereret. Nam currens identidem graece clamabat ευρηκα ευρηκα.

**English Translation:**

[9] Of the many and varied marvellous discoveries of Archimedes, the one I am about to describe seems to show, above all the others, an ingenuity without limits. Hiero, you see, having risen to royal power in Syracuse, decided after his successes to place a golden crown in a certain temple as a votive offering to the immortal gods. He contracted out the work for a fee, and weighed out the gold for the contractor on the scales. The contractor delivered the work on time, finely crafted, to the king's satisfaction, and on the scales the crown seemed to match the weight of the gold.

[10] Afterwards, word came that some of the gold had been taken out and the same weight of silver mixed into the crown. Hiero was furious at having been cheated, but since he could find no way to prove the theft, he asked Archimedes to take the problem on. While Archimedes had the matter on his mind, he happened to go to the baths, and as he got down into the tub he noticed that the more of his body sank into it, the more water overflowed from the tub. Since this showed him how the problem could be explained, he did not wait a moment: overjoyed, he leapt out of the tub and ran home naked, announcing at the top of his voice that he had found what he was looking for. For as he ran he kept shouting in Greek: *heureka, heureka*, "I have found it, I have found it!"

**Analysis:**

This is Vitruvius in storytelling mode, and his Latin relaxes accordingly. The construction is mostly the narrative staple of every Latin textbook: *cum* with the subjunctive (*cum... constituisset*, *cum haberet*, *cum... descenderet*, *cum... ostendisset*), the ablative absolute (*rebus bene gestis*, *dempto auro*), indirect statement after verbs of perceiving and saying (*animadvertit... effluere*, *significabat... invenisse*), and an indirect question (*quod quaereret*). A student who has read Caesar will feel at home.

The technical touches are where the care is needed. *Manupretio locavit faciendam* is legal-commercial language: *locare* with a gerundive means "to put out to contract", and *manupretium* is the price of the labour, so Hiero hired a goldsmith for a fee. *Ad sacoma* is a Greek loan-word for the counterweight on a scale. *Redemptor* is not a "redeemer" but the contractor who has undertaken the job. And the central observation, *quantum corporis sui in eo insideret tantum aquae extra solium effluere*, is a correlative pair, *quantum... tantum*, "as much... so much", which sets out in one line what we now call the displacement of water. Notice that Vitruvius does not mention a law of buoyancy, or anything about the weight of displaced water: that is Archimedes' later work *On Floating Bodies*. What he describes is volume. Gold is denser than silver, so a crown with silver in it takes up more space than the same weight of pure gold, and pushes out more water. The rest of the preface (sections 11-12) describes the experiment, which proves the theft.

And then the punchline: *nudus vadens domum versus*. The greatest mathematician of antiquity running naked through the streets of Syracuse. Vitruvius ends the section on Archimedes' words in Greek, *heureka*, which every modern language has borrowed. For an author who apologised for his writing, it is a perfectly told anecdote.

## Legacy and Impact

The *De Architectura* is the only treatise on architecture to survive from the ancient world. Greek architects wrote many books on their buildings, and Vitruvius quotes them; Romans after him wrote more; every one of them is lost. Everything we know at first hand about how a Roman architect was trained, how he thought about proportion, how he chose his sand or sited his city, comes from this one book. That alone would make him important. What made him decisive was the Renaissance.

The text never quite disappeared: it was copied in Carolingian monasteries, and dozens of medieval manuscripts survive. But it was the humanists who turned it into a sacred text. In 1416 Poggio Bracciolini, the great book-hunter of the age, found a copy at the monastery of St Gall and helped bring it back into circulation. From then on, anyone in Italy who wanted to build *all'antica*, in the manner of the ancients, read Vitruvius. When humanist princes and popes rebuilt their cities and Renaissance architects designed their churches, palaces and squares, Vitruvius was the manual open on the table, and the ruins of Rome were the examples they measured him against. Brunelleschi studied the ruins; Alberti studied Vitruvius; Bramante, Raphael and Michelangelo all worked in a world shaped by him. In the 1540s a group of humanists in Rome even founded an *Accademia della Virtù*, nicknamed the Vitruvian Academy, with the aim of producing a complete edition and commentary of the text.

His influence was just as great on the books that came after him, because every major architectural treatise of the Renaissance was built on Vitruvius, whether it followed him, corrected him or argued with him. Leon Battista Alberti's *De re aedificatoria* (1452), the first great architectural treatise of the modern age, is written in Latin, deliberately divided into ten books like Vitruvius's, and built on his ideas from start to finish, even where it corrects him. It was followed by the first printed edition of Vitruvius (Rome, c. 1486), the first illustrated edition by Fra Giocondo (Venice, 1511), the first Italian translation by Cesare Cesariano (Como, 1521), and the great annotated edition of Daniele Barbaro (Venice, 1556), with illustrations by Andrea Palladio. Then came the manuals that taught architecture to the whole of Europe: Sebastiano Serlio's books on architecture, Vignola's *Regola delli cinque ordini* (1562), and Palladio's *I quattro libri dell'architettura* (1570), whose villas and churches were copied from England to Virginia. The five classical orders of columns, the idea that a building should be proportioned like a human body, and the triad of *firmitas, utilitas, venustas* all reached the modern world through these books, and all of them go back to Vitruvius. When Thomas Jefferson designed his house at Monticello and the University of Virginia, he worked from Palladio, which means that, at second hand, he was working from a retired Roman artillery engineer with a pension from Octavia.

And then, of course, there is Leonardo's *Vitruvian Man*, which made Vitruvius's name famous even among people who have never opened his book.

## Final Rating

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

Vitruvius is prose, and in the Augustan Era that already puts him on the easier side. His grammar is standard, his stories are told in plain narrative Latin, and he uses almost no rhetorical figures or literary allusion. What lifts him above D is his lexicon: a dense, technical vocabulary full of Greek loan-words, many of them missing from school dictionaries, together with a loose and sometimes awkward sentence structure that makes his descriptions harder to follow than to parse, especially without the lost diagrams. The narrative prefaces, like the Archimedes story, sit close to D; the technical chapters on temples or machines can touch B. On balance, a solid C: approachable for any student who has read Caesar and Cicero, provided they keep a good dictionary and a lot of patience within reach.

# GAIUS CORNELIUS GALLUS (70/69 -- 26 BC)

## Biography

Gaius Cornelius Gallus was the most dangerous kind of man to be in Augustan Rome: brilliant at two things at once. He invented a whole genre of Latin poetry, and he governed Egypt. The second of these killed him, and in killing him it took most of the first one with it.

**A provincial from Fréjus.** He was born in 70 or 69 BC at Forum Iulii, modern Fréjus on the coast of Provence, in the province of Gallia Narbonensis, probably into a family of no particular distinction; his name may even suggest Gallic origins. He was almost exactly the same age as Virgil, and like Virgil he came to Rome from the provinces with nothing but talent. The first time he appears in history is in a letter of 43 BC from Asinius Pollio to Cicero, where he is mentioned in passing as "my friend Cornelius Gallus". Within a few years he was one of the most admired young poets in the city.

**Virgil's friend.** Gallus and Virgil were close friends, and the friendship is written into the *Eclogues*. According to the ancient commentators, when Virgil's family farm near Mantua was confiscated for veterans after Philippi, Gallus was one of the influential men who helped him. Virgil paid him back in the only currency a poet has. In *Eclogue* 6 he imagines Gallus being led up Mount Helicon by one of the Muses, where the whole chorus of Apollo rises to greet him and he is handed the pipes of Hesiod: a solemn consecration as a poet. And the whole of *Eclogue* 10, the last poem of the book, is dedicated to him: *pauca meo Gallo*, "a few verses for my Gallus". In it Gallus is lying under a rock in Arcadia, dying of love, while shepherds, gods and even the sheep gather round to ask what is wrong. It is Gallus who speaks the most famous line of the poem, *omnia vincit Amor: et nos cedamus Amori*, "Love conquers all: let us too give in to Love". Ancient commentators believed that some of the lines Virgil gives him there were adapted from Gallus's own poems, which would make *Eclogue* 10 the best surviving witness to what they sounded like.

**Lycoris.** The woman Gallus suffers for in *Eclogue* 10, and the heroine of his four books of love elegies, is called Lycoris. Her real name, according to the commentators, was Volumnia Cytheris, and she was the most famous actress in Rome: a freedwoman who performed in mime, the scandalous, semi-improvised popular theatre of the day, and who had previously been the mistress of Mark Antony, with whom she toured Italy in a litter, to the horror of respectable society. Cicero once found himself at a dinner party next to her and wrote to a friend, plainly embarrassed, that he had had no idea she would be there. In *Eclogue* 10 Lycoris has left Gallus to follow a soldier to the snows of the Alps and the Rhine. The pattern of Roman love elegy, the poet devoted to a beautiful, unfaithful woman under a Greek pseudonym, begins here.

**The general.** Unlike almost every other Roman poet, Gallus was also a successful soldier. In the final campaign against Antony and Cleopatra in 30 BC he commanded Octavian's legions in the west of Egypt, captured the town of Paraetonium and drove back Antony's attack on it. He then played a part in the capture of Cleopatra: according to Plutarch, Gallus kept the queen talking at the barred door of her mausoleum while his colleague Proculeius climbed in through a window behind her. Octavian rewarded him with the greatest prize available to a man of his rank: in 30 BC Gallus became the first Roman prefect of Egypt.

**Too much glory.** Egypt was not an ordinary province. It was the richest territory in the empire and the granary of Rome, and Augustus governed it as his personal possession, through a man of equestrian rank, not a senator, so that nobody powerful enough to rebel could ever control it. Gallus did his job extremely well. He crushed a revolt in Upper Egypt, took five cities in fifteen days, pushed beyond the First Cataract of the Nile, and received ambassadors from the king of Ethiopia. Then he made the fatal mistake of telling everybody. In April 29 BC he set up at Philae a stone inscription in three languages (Latin, Greek and Egyptian hieroglyphs) in which he boasted that he had led an army "where neither the Roman people nor the kings of Egypt had ever carried arms", and mentioned Octavian only briefly. According to Cassius Dio, he also put up statues of himself all over Egypt, had his achievements carved on the pyramids, and, worst of all, said disrespectful things about Augustus, probably after a few drinks.

**The fall.** In 27 or 26 BC he was recalled and denounced by a former friend. Augustus formally renounced his friendship (*renuntiatio amicitiae*) and banned him from his house and his provinces, which in Augustan Rome was the end of a career and very nearly the end of a life. The Senate, eager to show its loyalty, piled on: it voted to have him tried, exiled, and his property handed over to Augustus. Gallus did not wait for the verdict. He killed himself in 26 BC, at the age of forty-three. Suetonius says that Augustus wept when he heard the news, and complained that he alone was not allowed to be angry with his friends only as much as he wanted. Ovid, who knew something about displeasing Augustus, later summed up the case in two lines: it was not writing about Lycoris that ruined Gallus, but not holding his tongue when he had had too much wine.

**Erased.** What followed is often called a *damnatio memoriae*, and although the term is modern and there is no record of a formal decree under that name, the effect was very much the same: Gallus was removed from the record. The Philae inscription was smashed and its pieces used as building material for a later temple, where archaeologists found it in 1896. In Alexandria he had built a new forum for Octavian and dedicated an obelisk there in his own name; his inscription was removed and replaced. That obelisk was later brought to Rome by Caligula and now stands in the middle of St Peter's Square, and in the 1960s the archaeologist Filippo Magi realised that the rows of small holes on its base were the fixing holes of bronze letters from the original dedication. When he joined the dots, they spelled out the name of Gallus. Every pilgrim who has ever crossed St Peter's Square has walked past the one monument in Rome that still, invisibly, carries his name. The fourth-century commentator Servius adds a more famous and more doubtful story: that the fourth book of Virgil's *Georgics* originally ended with a long praise of Gallus, and that after his fall Augustus ordered Virgil to replace it, which is how we got the story of Aristaeus and Orpheus instead. Most modern scholars think this is a legend, but it says a great deal that ancient readers found it believable.

His poetry did not vanish immediately. Ovid could still promise that "Gallus will be known in the West, Gallus in the East, and his Lycoris with him", and Quintilian read and ranked him at the end of the first century AD. But no manuscript of his four books survived the end of antiquity. Until 1978, the founder of Latin love elegy was represented by exactly one line.

## Most Important Works

- **Amores** (*Love Poems*, probably in the 40s and 30s BC) -- four books of love elegies addressed to Lycoris, the work that founded Roman love elegy as a genre. Before 1978 only one pentameter survived, quoted by a late geographer for its description of a river: *uno tellures dividit amne duas*, "with a single stream it divides two lands". In 1978 a papyrus found at Qasr Ibrim in Egyptian Nubia gave us about ten more lines (see the excerpt below). [Fragmentary]
- **Translations or adaptations of Euphorion** -- Virgil's *Eclogues* hint that Gallus adapted the obscure, learned Greek poet Euphorion of Chalcis, the kind of Alexandrian model the neoterics loved, possibly including a poem on the origin of the Grynean grove of Apollo. [Lost]
- **The Philae inscription** (29 BC) -- not literature, but the only surviving text certainly written in Gallus's name: his official account of his victories in Egypt, in Latin and Greek, with an Egyptian version in hieroglyphs. It is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. [Survives, damaged]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

With about ten lines of verse, there is very little to say about Gallus's style from his own words, and anything we say rests largely on what others said about him. Quintilian, ranking the Latin elegists, called Tibullus the most polished and elegant, said some preferred Propertius, found Ovid too playful, and described Gallus as *durior*, "rather harsh". That fits what the papyrus shows. His couplets are self-contained, each making a single point and stopping. He ends a pentameter on a five-syllable word, *historiae*, which Ovid, a generation later, would almost never do. And his spelling is old-fashioned: *quom* for *cum*, *spolieis* and *deiuitiora* with *ei* for long *i*, *Kato* with a K. These may be the habits of the copyist rather than of the poet, but they give the lines an archaic look that fits an author writing at the very beginning of the Augustan age, closer to Catullus than to Ovid.

The papyrus also confirms, at least in miniature, what the ancients said of his range: in nine lines he moves from his mistress, to Caesar's glory, to the Muses, to the literary critics whose judgement he fears. Love, politics and poetry in one breath. That combination, the love poet who also writes for the regime and keeps one eye on his critics, is exactly the tension that runs through the whole of Augustan elegy.

## Latin Excerpt

### Excerpt: Gallus fr. 2 (the Qasr Ibrim papyrus)

**Source:** Gallus, fragment 2 Courtney, from *Living Poets*, Durham University (livingpoets.awh.durham.ac.uk/w/index.php/Gallus_fr._2), following the text first published by R. D. Anderson, P. J. Parsons and R. G. M. Nisbet in the *Journal of Roman Studies* (1979). The Latin Library and Splash Latino do not carry Gallus. Square brackets mark letters lost on the papyrus and restored by editors; dots mark letters that are lost or illegible.

**Context:** In 1978 an expedition of the Egypt Exploration Society was excavating Qasr Ibrim, a fortress on a cliff above the Nile in Nubia, far to the south of Egypt, where a Roman garrison was stationed around 25-21 BC. In a heap of rubbish from the Roman occupation they found a scrap of papyrus with Latin elegiac couplets written on it, with the name *Lycoris* in the first surviving line. Since Lycoris was Gallus's mistress, and since no other poet is known to have used the name, the lines were identified as his. The papyrus was probably copied within a few decades of the poems being written, possibly in the poet's own lifetime, which makes it the oldest surviving manuscript of Latin poetry. That Gallus's poems should reappear in Egypt, the province that ruined him, carried there perhaps by one of the soldiers who served under his successor, is an irony he would have appreciated.

**Latin Text:**

> ] tristia nequit[ia fact]a, Lycori, tua.
>
> fata mihi, Caesar, tum erunt mea dulcia quom tu
> maxima Romanae pars eris historiae,
> postque tuum reditum multorum templa deorum
> fixa legam spolieis deiuitiora tueis.
>
> . . . . . ] . . . . . tandem fecerunt c[ar]mina Musae
> quae possem domina deicere digna mea.
> . . . . . . . . . . . ] . atur idem tibi, non ego, Visce,
> . . ] . . . . . . . . l . Kato, iudice te uereor.

**English Translation:**

> ... made sad, Lycoris, by your wickedness.
>
> My fate will be sweet to me, Caesar, at the moment when you
> become the greatest part of Roman history,
> and when, after your return, I read that the temples of many gods
> are the richer for your spoils hung up in them.
>
> ... at last the Muses have made poems
> that I could recite, worthy of my mistress.
> ... the same to you, I do not, Viscus,
> ... Cato, with you as my judge, fear.

**Analysis:**

These nine lines, some of them broken, are almost everything we have of the man who invented Latin love elegy, so every word has been argued over. The first line, of which only the end survives, is a pentameter addressed to Lycoris, accusing her of *nequitia*, a word that means anything from naughtiness to sheer wickedness and will become a key word of later elegy, where the lover's *nequitia* and the mistress's are both on display. That single half-line already contains the whole elegiac situation: the poet, the woman, and the hurt.

The next four lines are a complete epigram of a completely different kind: a political compliment. "My fate will be sweet to me, Caesar, when you become the greatest part of Roman history." Which Caesar is meant is debated. Many scholars think it is Julius Caesar, about to leave for his planned war against Parthia in 44 BC, which would date the poem before the Ides of March; others think it is Octavian. Either way, the lines show that the founder of love elegy was also, already, writing praise of the ruler, the same mix that Propertius and Ovid would handle so uneasily. The grammar is clean: a future *erunt* answered by *quom tu... eris* (with *quom*, the old spelling of *cum*), then *legam*, "I shall read", governing an indirect statement with *esse* understood, *templa... deiuitiora*, "that the temples are richer". *Fixa... spolieis... tueis* refers to the Roman custom of nailing captured arms and trophies to the walls of temples.

The last four lines are literary: at last the Muses have produced poems "worthy of my mistress", and the poet wonders how they will be received by two critics, Viscus and Cato. Viscus is probably one of the Visci brothers whom Horace lists among the readers whose approval he wants; Cato is almost certainly Valerius Cato, the grammarian and poet who was the great authority of the neoteric generation. So in nine lines Gallus places himself exactly: between his mistress, the most powerful man in Rome, and the literary establishment.

Formally, each couplet stands alone, and the pentameters end on a two-syllable word (*tua*, *mea*) or, unusually, on a longer one (*historiae*, *uereor*). Later elegists, above all Ovid, made the two-syllable ending almost compulsory, so the papyrus gives us a glimpse of the genre before its rules were fixed. Notice also the old-fashioned spellings, *spolieis*, *deiuitiora*, *deicere* (for *dicere*), *quom*, *Kato*, and the use of *u* for consonantal *v* (*deiuitiora*, *uereor*), which is how the Romans themselves wrote it.

A word of caution: a minority of scholars have doubted that these lines are by Gallus at all, or have found them disappointingly flat for such a famous poet. Most accept them. Whether they are his best lines is another question, and an unfair one: they are simply the lines that happened to end up on a rubbish heap in Nubia.

---

## Legacy and Impact

Gallus is the clearest case in the whole project of an author whose importance has nothing to do with how much of him survives. He is the founder of Latin love elegy as a genre. Catullus had written love poems in elegiac couplets, but Gallus seems to have been the first to make an entire book, and then four, out of elegies about a single mistress under a Greek pseudonym, and in doing so he fixed the shape of the genre that Tibullus, Propertius and Ovid would bring to its peak. The ancients were in no doubt about this. When Ovid lists the line of Roman elegists, Gallus comes first; when Quintilian claims that in elegy "we challenge the Greeks", Gallus is one of the four names he gives; and Virgil's *Eclogue* 10 turns him into the very image of the elegiac lover, suffering in Arcadia, defeated by Love.

Many of the conventions of Roman elegy probably go back to him: the poet as the slave of his mistress (*servitium amoris*), love as a kind of military service (*militia amoris*), the beloved under a pseudonym that hides a real woman, the poet who refuses war and career in favour of love and then writes about Caesar's victories anyway. We cannot prove most of this from his own lines, because the lines are gone. But the genre he started is one of the great Roman contributions to European literature, and it runs from the Augustan elegists to the troubadours, Petrarch, and every love poet who has ever complained in verse about a cruel beloved.

His other legacy is a warning. Gallus was the first great writer of the Augustan age to discover that the regime gave a great deal and could take it all back. His fall in 26 BC came only a year after the "restored Republic" of 27 BC, and it showed everyone, poets included, where the limits were. Ovid would learn the same lesson thirty-four years later.

## Final Rating

**Tier: NC (Not Comparable)**

About ten lines of verse, several of them broken, cannot support a reliable rating. What survives is not especially hard: the syntax of the papyrus is clear, the vocabulary standard, and the main obstacles are the archaic spelling, the gaps in the text and the elegiac metre itself. But nine lines cannot tell us how Gallus handled the long, dense, allusive elegies his contemporaries admired, and Quintilian's *durior* suggests that he was not an easy read. So NC, as with Livius Andronicus. What NC does not measure is importance, and here Gallus ranks with anyone in the era: without him there would be no Tibullus, no Propertius, and no Ovid as we know them.

# PUBLIUS VERGILIUS MARO -- VIRGIL (70 -- 19 BC)

## Biography

Virgil is the most famous Latin poet who ever lived, and possibly the most famous Latin author of any kind. He is also, by every ancient account, one of the shyest, most awkward and most anxious men ever to become a celebrity. Most of what we know about his life comes from a biography by Suetonius that survives in a version by the fourth-century grammarian Donatus, and from the commentary of Servius. Some of it is legend. All of it is worth telling.

**A laurel in a ditch.** Publius Vergilius Maro was born on 15 October 70 BC in the village of Andes, near Mantua, in what was then Cisalpine Gaul. His father was a man of modest origins: according to Donatus, either a potter or the hired hand of a minor official called Magius, who did well enough to marry his employer's daughter, Magia Polla, and to buy land and bees of his own. Before Virgil was born, his mother dreamed that she gave birth to a laurel branch, which took root and grew at once into a fully grown tree covered in fruit and flowers. The next day she gave birth in a ditch by the side of the road, on the way to the country. The baby, we are told, did not cry, and had such a gentle expression that everyone took it as a sign of good fortune. A poplar planted on the spot grew so fast that local women started praying to it. Every great Roman got a few omens; Virgil got a botanical garden.

**The maiden.** He was educated at Cremona and Milan, then in Rome, where he studied rhetoric to become a lawyer. It did not go well. Donatus says he pleaded exactly one case in court, and spoke so slowly that he seemed almost uneducated. He then turned to medicine and, above all, mathematics, before ending up at Naples in the Epicurean school of Siro. He was tall, dark-skinned, with the face of a countryman, and in poor health all his life: he suffered from his stomach, his throat and headaches, and often coughed up blood. In Naples people called him *Parthenias*, "the maiden", because of the modesty of his life. When he did become famous, on the rare occasions he visited Rome and people recognised him in the street and pointed him out, he would duck into the nearest house to escape them.

**The farm.** In 41 BC, after Philippi, the triumvirs confiscated land all over northern Italy to settle their veterans, and the Virgil family farm was among them. According to the ancient commentators, Virgil was nearly killed by the veteran who took it, and only got it back (if he ever did) thanks to influential friends, among them Asinius Pollio and Cornelius Gallus. The experience went straight into his first book. The opening poem of the *Eclogues* is a dialogue between a shepherd who has kept his land and one who has been thrown off it, and the dispossessed shepherd's question to the new soldier-owner, *impius haec tam culta novalia miles habebit?*, "is some godless soldier going to have these well-tended fields?", is the civil wars seen from the farmyard.

**Maecenas, and a bear licking her cubs.** The *Eclogues* made him famous almost overnight: they were even performed on stage by actors, to wild applause. They also brought him into the circle of Maecenas, who became his patron and friend, and who asked him for a poem on farming. Virgil spent seven years on the four books of the *Georgics*, about 2,200 lines, which works out at less than a line a day. His method, as Donatus describes it, explains why. Every morning he dictated a large number of verses; then he spent the rest of the day reworking them and cutting them down to a very few, saying that he produced his poems the way a she-bear produces her cubs: by licking them into shape. In 29 BC he read the finished *Georgics* to Octavian, just back from Actium, at Atella in Campania. The reading took four days, and whenever Virgil's voice gave out, Maecenas took over.

**The Aeneid, and an emperor who would not wait.** For the last eleven years of his life he worked on the *Aeneid*. He first wrote the whole story out in prose and divided it into twelve books, then turned it into verse piece by piece, in no particular order, as the mood took him. Where he could not yet find the right line, he left a half-line and moved on, calling these unfinished verses "props" (*tibicines*), put in to hold up the building until the real columns were ready. Fifty-eight of those half-lines are still in the poem, and one of them appears in the excerpt below. Augustus could hardly bear the wait. While fighting in Spain in 26-25 BC he wrote to Virgil begging, half jokingly and half threateningly, for "even a first sketch or any section at all" of the poem. Virgil replied that he had nothing worthy to send, and that he had taken on something so enormous that he sometimes thought he must have been out of his mind to start it.

**Octavia faints.** Eventually he gave in, and read three books to Augustus and his family: Books II, IV and VI. Book VI ends with Aeneas in the underworld, where his father Anchises shows him the souls of the great Romans of the future waiting to be born. Last of all comes a young man of extraordinary promise, surrounded by mourning, and Anchises, weeping, reveals who he is: *tu Marcellus eris*, "you will be Marcellus". This was Augustus's nephew, son-in-law and intended heir, who had died in 23 BC at the age of nineteen, and his mother, Augustus's sister Octavia, was in the room. Donatus says that when Virgil reached those lines she fainted, and was revived only with difficulty; later tradition adds that Augustus and Octavia both wept, and that Octavia rewarded the poet with ten thousand sesterces for every line. It is the best evidence we have of what Virgil's poetry could do to an audience. These were not readers studying a set text. They were a grieving family hearing their dead boy turned into the last and saddest of Rome's heroes, in lines so beautiful that they could not bear them.

**Best or bust.** Virgil was, in the most literal sense, a perfectionist: he would rather have no poem than an imperfect one. In 19 BC, with the *Aeneid* complete in draft, he set out for Greece and Asia, planning to spend three more years revising it and then devote the rest of his life to philosophy. In Athens he met Augustus, who persuaded him to come home. On a visit to Megara in the burning sun he fell ill; the illness worsened on the voyage, and he died at Brundisium on 21 September 19 BC, a few weeks short of his fifty-first birthday. Before leaving Italy he had asked his friend Varius to burn the *Aeneid* if anything happened to him, and on his deathbed he called for the manuscript boxes so that he could burn it himself. Nobody brought them. In his will he left the manuscript to Varius and Plotius Tucca, on condition that they publish nothing he had not already published. Augustus overruled the will, and ordered them to publish the poem exactly as it was, adding nothing and correcting only what was obviously wrong: which is why the half-lines are still there. We have the most important poem in Latin literature because the emperor refused to respect a dead man's wishes. It is very nearly a matter of luck.

**Fame, fortune and thieves.** He died rich, with a fortune of almost ten million sesterces from the generosity of his friends, and a house on the Esquiline next to the gardens of Maecenas, which he hardly used. He was buried at Naples, under an epitaph that sums up his whole career in two lines: *Mantua me genuit, Calabri rapuere, tenet nunc / Parthenope; cecini pascua, rura, duces*, "Mantua bore me, Calabria took me, now Naples holds me; I sang of pastures, fields and leaders". He also had his critics, who accused him of stealing whole lines from Homer. His answer, according to Donatus, was the best literary comeback in antiquity: "Why don't they try the same thefts themselves? They will find it is easier to steal the club from Hercules than a line from Homer."

## Most Important Works

- **Bucolica** or **Eclogae** (*Eclogues*, c. 42-38 BC) -- ten short pastoral poems, modelled on the Greek idylls of Theocritus, set in an imaginary countryside of singing shepherds that later centuries would call Arcadia. The themes alternate between love (Eclogues 2, 8 and 10, the last one for Gallus), singing contests and poetry (3, 5, 7), the land confiscations and the intrusion of history into the countryside (1 and 9), and grand visions of the cosmos and the future (4 and 6). The fourth, which announces the birth of a child who will bring back the Golden Age, was later read by Christians as a prophecy of Christ. [Complete]
- **Georgica** (*Georgics*, c. 37-29 BC) -- a didactic poem on farming in four books, dedicated to Maecenas. Book I is about crops and the weather, Book II about trees and vines (with the famous praise of Italy), Book III about cattle, horses and sheep (ending in a terrible plague), Book IV about bees. On the surface it is a manual; underneath, it is a poem about work (*labor omnia vicit / improbus*, "relentless work conquered everything"), about Italy, and about how order can be rebuilt after the chaos of civil war. It ends with the myth of Aristaeus, who loses his bees and gets them back, and of Orpheus, who loses Eurydice and does not. [Complete]
- **Aeneis** (*Aeneid*, c. 29-19 BC) -- the national epic of Rome, in twelve books, unfinished at the poet's death. The first six books, the wanderings of Aeneas from burning Troy to Italy, are Virgil's *Odyssey*; the last six, the war in Latium, are his *Iliad*. Its hero is defined not by anger, like Achilles, or cunning, like Ulysses, but by *pietas*, duty to the gods, to his family and to his mission. Its great theme is the price of that mission: Rome is founded, but at the cost of Troy, of Dido, of Turnus and of countless young men, and the poem ends abruptly, with Aeneas killing Turnus in a moment of rage. It is a celebration of Augustan Rome and, at the same time, one of the saddest poems ever written about what empires cost. [Complete, but unrevised]
- **Appendix Vergiliana** -- a collection of minor poems attributed to Virgil in antiquity (the *Culex*, the *Ciris*, the *Moretum* and others), almost all of them written by other people and later attached to his name, which is what happens when you become the most famous poet in the world. [Mostly spurious]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Virgil is the point at which Latin poetry reaches stylistic perfection, and he made the dactylic hexameter what it would be for the rest of antiquity. Ennius had brought the hexameter into Latin two centuries earlier, and Lucretius and Catullus had refined it; but it was Virgil who fixed its rules, its rhythms, its pauses and its endings, so completely that after him every Latin epic poet wrote Virgil's hexameter, and every poet who wrote in hexameters in the genres he touched, pastoral and didactic as well as epic, wrote in his shadow. Every line is weighed. The sound imitates the sense: slow, heavy spondees for effort, grief or great size, quick dactyls for speed. Words are placed for emphasis at the start of the line or at its main pause. And there is not a word too many. For a translator, this perfection is precisely the problem.

The difficulties come in layers. The first is **poetic word order**. Adjectives and nouns are separated across the line, often framing it, and the most famous device of all is the transferred epithet (*hypallage*), where an adjective is attached grammatically to one noun while it really describes another, for the sake of the meaning and of the metre. The seventh line of the *Aeneid* is the classic example: *altae moenia Romae*, literally "the walls of high Rome", where we would expect "the high walls of Rome". Then come **poetic plurals** (*colla* for a single neck, *silentia* for silence), Greek forms and case endings for Greek names, archaisms (*olli* for *illi*, *aulai* for *aulae*), and **elision**, where a final vowel or *-m* before another vowel disappears in pronunciation, sometimes several times in one line, so that words have to be mentally pulled apart before they can be read. **Lexicon: MEDIUM to HIGH; Syntactic complexity: HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH.**

The second layer is **allusion**. Virgil writes with the whole of Greek and Latin literature open on the desk: Homer above all, but also Hesiod, Theocritus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Ennius, Lucretius and Catullus. Myths are referred to, not told; a single epithet can stand for a whole story. And compared with Horace, the Latin is honest: there is none of Horace's wilful game-playing with word order. But the density of everything at once makes Virgil hard all the time. **Allusion density: HIGH.**

Difficulty also varies a great deal across his works, and here a widespread school habit is simply wrong. Students are very often started on the *Aeneid*, as if Virgil's most famous poem were the natural way in. It is not. The **Eclogues** are the right place to begin: they are short, the vocabulary is limited, the situations are simple, and a student can translate a whole poem from beginning to end and enjoy the rare satisfaction of having actually finished a work of Virgil. They range from B to low A. The **Aeneid** is a step up: the narrative helps, but the speeches, the similes and the sheer density of the verse put it in the middle to upper range of A. The **Georgics** are the hardest of the three, and probably the hardest Latin poetry of the whole era outside Horace's lyrics: technical farming vocabulary, rare plant and animal names, Greek words, mythological allusions and a compressed didactic syntax all at once. At their most difficult they push into S territory.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Bucolica (Eclogue) 2.6-18 -- Corydon's Complaint

**Source:** Virgil, *Bucolica*, Eclogue 2, lines 6-18, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vergil/ec2.shtml)

**Context:** Eclogue 2 is a love complaint. The shepherd Corydon is in love with Alexis, a beautiful young slave who is his master's favourite, and who does not care for him at all. The first five lines, spoken by the narrator, tell us that Corydon keeps coming alone to a grove of beech trees to pour out his "unpolished" song to the mountains and the woods, in vain. Then Corydon begins to speak, and these are the first thirteen lines of his monologue. Virgil's model is the eleventh *Idyll* of Theocritus, in which the Cyclops Polyphemus sings to the sea nymph Galatea, who will not have him either. Keep that Cyclops in mind: he comes back in Excerpt 3. (The ancient biographers, inevitably, identified Alexis with a real slave boy given to Virgil by Pollio; there is no way of knowing, and in Greek pastoral the love of a shepherd for a boy was a standard literary situation.)

**Latin Text:**

> 'O crudelis Alexi, nihil mea carmina curas?
> nil nostri miserere? mori me denique cogis?
> nunc etiam pecudes umbras et frigora captant,
> nunc virides etiam occultant spineta lacertos,
> Thestylis et rapido fessis messoribus aestu               10
> alia serpyllumque herbas contundit olentis.
> at mecum raucis, tua dum vestigia lustro,
> sole sub ardenti resonant arbusta cicadis.
> nonne fuit satius tristis Amaryllidos iras
> atque superba pati fastidia? nonne Menalcan,               15
> quamvis ille niger, quamvis tu candidus esses?
> o formose puer, nimium ne crede colori;
> alba ligustra cadunt, vaccinia nigra leguntur.

**English Translation:**

"O cruel Alexis, do you care nothing for my songs?
Have you no pity for me? Will you drive me to my death at last?
Now even the flocks are looking for shade and cool air,
now even the thornbushes hide the green lizards,
and Thestylis, for the reapers worn out by the scorching heat,
is pounding garlic and thyme, the fragrant herbs.
But with me, as I follow your footsteps,
under the blazing sun, the orchards ring with hoarse cicadas.
Would it not have been better to put up with Amaryllis's
bitter rages and her proud disdain? Or with Menalcas,
however dark he was, however fair you are?
O beautiful boy, do not trust your colouring too much:
the white privet flowers fall, the dark bilberries are picked."

**Analysis:**

This is the kind of passage that makes the Eclogues the right place to start Virgil. The situation is simple, the sentences are short, and almost every line is a complete thought. The difficulties are real but manageable, and they are exactly the ones you will meet everywhere else in Virgil.

Start with the traps. *Nil nostri miserere*: *misereor* takes the genitive, and *nostri* is a poetic plural for *mei*, "of me". *Alia* in line 11 is not "other things" but the neuter plural of *alium*, "garlic", one of the classic false friends of Latin poetry; *olentis* is the old accusative plural for *olentes*, agreeing with *herbas*, and *serpyllum* is a Greek plant name, wild thyme. *Satius* is the comparative of *satis*, "better", and *quamvis... esses* is a concessive subjunctive. Line 15 ends on *Menalcan*, a Greek accusative. And *vaccinia* in the last line has been argued over for centuries: it may be bilberries or a kind of dark hyacinth; either way, it is the dark flower or fruit that people pick, against the white flower that is left to fall.

Then look at how the lines are built. *raucis... cicadis* frames lines 12 and 13 together, the hoarse cicadas surrounding the whole scene with their noise, while Corydon wanders in the noonday heat, the one time of day when every sensible creature, even the lizards, is in the shade. The repeated *nunc etiam... nunc... etiam* stresses that the whole world is resting except him. The last line is a perfect chiasmus: *alba ligustra cadunt, vaccinia nigra leguntur*, adjective-noun-verb, noun-adjective-verb, white against black, falling against being chosen. It is a proverb in miniature, telling a beautiful boy that beauty fades and fair skin is no guarantee of being wanted, and it is the first line of Virgil that many readers learn by heart.

Finally, notice the tone. Corydon is pathetic and a little ridiculous: he is literally out in the midday sun, he lists the people he could have had instead, and in the very next lines (not in the excerpt) he starts boasting about how many sheep he owns. Virgil lets us smile at him and feel for him at the same time, which is the emotional key to all of Virgil.

### Excerpt 2: Georgica IV.1-24 -- A Home for the Bees

**Source:** Virgil, *Georgica*, Book IV, lines 1-24, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vergil/geo4.shtml). The dashes around *nam pabula venti / ferre domum prohibent* in the source have been printed as parentheses.

**Context:** The opening of the last book of the *Georgics*. After crops, trees and livestock, Virgil turns to bees, and announces his subject to Maecenas in the first seven lines with a mixture of solemnity and irony: he is going to write about the bees as if they were a nation, with leaders, customs and wars. Then, with the word *Principio*, "first of all", the farming manual begins, and the first practical problem is where to put the hives. This is the book that ends with Aristaeus and Orpheus, and that Servius believed originally ended with the praise of Gallus.

**Latin Text:**

> Protinus aerii mellis caelestia dona
> exsequar: hanc etiam, Maecenas, adspice partem.
> Admiranda tibi levium spectacula rerum
> magnanimosque duces totiusque ordine gentis
> mores et studia et populos et proelia dicam.               5
> In tenui labor; at tenuis non gloria, si quem
> numina laeva sinunt auditque vocatus Apollo.
> Principio sedes apibus statioque petenda,
> quo neque sit ventis aditus (nam pabula venti
> ferre domum prohibent) neque oves haedique petulci               10
> floribus insultent aut errans bucula campo
> decutiat rorem et surgentes atterat herbas.
> Absint et picti squalentia terga lacerti
> pinguibus a stabulis meropesque aliaeque volucres
> et manibus Procne pectus signata cruentis;               15
> omnia nam late vastant ipsasque volantes
> ore ferunt dulcem nidis immitibus escam.
> At liquidi fontes et stagna virentia musco
> adsint et tenuis fugiens per gramina rivus,
> palmaque vestibulum aut ingens oleaster inumbret,               20
> ut, cum prima novi ducent examina reges
> vere suo ludetque favis emissa iuventus,
> vicina invitet decedere ripa calori,
> obviaque hospitiis teneat frondentibus arbos.

**English Translation:**

Next I shall go on to the heavenly gift of honey that comes from the air:
look on this part too, Maecenas.
I shall tell you of a marvellous spectacle of tiny things:
great-hearted leaders and, in due order, a whole nation's
customs and pursuits, its peoples and its battles.
The labour is on a slight subject; but not slight the glory, if the unfriendly
powers allow a poet, and Apollo hears when he is called.
First of all, a home and a settled place must be found for the bees,
where the winds can find no way in (for the winds stop them
from carrying their food home), where no sheep and frisky kids
trample on the flowers, and no heifer, straying across the plain,
shakes off the dew and wears down the growing grass.
Keep away from the rich hives the painted lizard with its scaly back,
and the bee-eaters and the other birds,
and Procne, her breast marked by bloodstained hands;
for they lay everything waste far and wide, and snatch the bees
themselves in flight, in their beaks, a sweet morsel for their pitiless nests.
But let there be clear springs nearby, and pools green with moss,
and a thin stream slipping away through the grass,
and let a palm or a great wild olive shade the entrance,
so that, when the new kings lead out the first swarms
in their own springtime, and the young ones, let out of the combs, are at play,
a nearby bank may invite them to escape the heat,
and a tree in their path may hold them in its leafy welcome.

**Analysis:**

The first seven lines are a small manifesto. *In tenui labor; at tenuis non gloria*: "the labour is on something slight, but the glory is not slight". The same adjective is used twice, first for the subject and then, negated, for the fame, a figure called polyptoton, and the line is a Callimachean declaration: small subjects, perfectly treated, can win great glory. At the same time Virgil plays the epic game in miniature: *magnanimos duces*, *populos*, *proelia* are the vocabulary of the *Aeneid*, applied to insects. The Romans thought the leader of the hive was a male, a king, which is why the bees have *reges* in line 21. Throughout Book IV the hive is a little model of the Roman state, hard-working, disciplined, and ready to die for its leader, and the reader is never quite sure how seriously to take the comparison.

From line 8 the manual begins, and with it the Georgics' typical difficulties. The syntax is instruction in the subjunctive: a gerundive of obligation (*petenda*, "must be found"), a relative clause of characteristic (*quo neque sit... neque... insultent... aut... decutiat... atterat*), and a series of jussives, *absint*, *adsint*, *inumbret*, "let them be absent", "let there be", "let it shade". The vocabulary is technical and zoological: *meropes* are bee-eaters, *oleaster* is the wild olive, *bucula* is a young cow, *haedi petulci* are kids that butt and frisk. And then, in the middle of the birdwatching, a myth arrives without warning: *manibus Procne pectus signata cruentis*. Procne killed her son Itys in revenge on her husband and was turned into a swallow, and the red mark on the swallow's breast was said to be the blood from her hands. Virgil does not explain any of this; he expects you to know. Note also the Greek-style accusative of respect, *pectus signata*, "marked as to the breast", and the framing of the line by *manibus... cruentis*, which encloses Procne in her own bloodstained hands.

The last section turns from danger to welcome. Notice how the future tenses (*ducent*, *ludet*) and the purpose clause (*ut... invitet... teneat*) imagine the young swarm's first spring, and how the vocabulary becomes almost human: *iuventus* is "the youth" of a city, *ludet* is play, *hospitiis* is hospitality. *Decedere calori* is a dative after a verb of yielding: "to give way to the heat", that is, to get out of it. The hive becomes a city, with a shaded entrance and a riverside park. This is the Georgics at their best and their hardest: a farming instruction, a lesson in natural history, a myth and a political allegory, all in the same twenty-four lines.

### Excerpt 3: Aeneis III.655-665 -- Polyphemus

**Source:** Virgil, *Aeneis*, Book III, lines 655-665, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vergil/aen3.shtml)

**Context:** In Book III Aeneas is telling Dido the story of his wanderings. Landing in Sicily, near Etna, the Trojans meet a ragged, starving Greek called Achaemenides, one of Ulysses' men, who was left behind when Ulysses escaped from the cave of the Cyclops. He has been hiding in the woods for three months, and he has just finished begging the Trojans to take him away, or kill him, when the Cyclops himself appears on the mountain. Virgil is rewriting *Odyssey* IX, but from the other side: we see Polyphemus after the blinding.

**Latin Text:**

> Vix ea fatus erat summo cum monte videmus               655
> ipsum inter pecudes vasta se mole moventem
> pastorem Polyphemum et litora nota petentem,
> monstrum horrendum, informe, ingens, cui lumen ademptum.
> trunca manum pinus regit et vestigia firmat;
> lanigerae comitantur oves; ea sola voluptas               660
> solamenque mali.
> postquam altos tetigit fluctus et ad aequora venit,
> luminis effossi fluidum lavit inde cruorem
> dentibus infrendens gemitu, graditurque per aequor
> iam medium, necdum fluctus latera ardua tinxit.                665

**English Translation:**

He had scarcely spoken these words when, high on the mountain, we see
the shepherd Polyphemus himself, moving his vast bulk among his flocks
and making for the shore he knew so well:
a monster, horrible, shapeless, huge, whose sight had been taken from him.
A lopped pine trunk guides his hand and steadies his steps;
his woolly sheep go with him, his only pleasure
and the comfort of his misery.
When he reached the deep water and came to the sea,
he washed away the blood still oozing from his gouged-out eye,
gnashing his teeth and groaning; and he walks through the sea,
right out into the middle, and still the waves have not wet his towering sides.

**Analysis:**

Line 658 is one of the most famous lines in Latin poetry, and it is famous for its sound: *monstrum horrendum, informe, ingens, cui lumen ademptum*. Read it aloud with the elisions, as a Roman would have heard it: *monstr'horrend'inform'ingens*. Three times in a row the final syllable of a word disappears into the next one (this fusion of vowels across words is called *synaloephe*), so that four words collapse into one long, shapeless mass of sound, exactly like the shapeless mass of the giant. The metre does the same: the first four feet of the line are all spondees, long syllable after long syllable, so the line moves as heavily and slowly as Polyphemus himself, lumbering down the mountain. Then the movement changes. After the pile of adjectives, the line ends with the ordinary, quiet cadence of the hexameter, *lumen ademptum*, and a bare, short relative clause: "whose sight had been taken". After the horror, the pity. That is Virgil's emotional method in a single verse: the sound makes us feel the monster's size, and the last words make us feel his loss.

The rest of the passage keeps the pity going. The giant leans on a pine trunk like an old man on a stick; his sheep, his only joy, walk beside him; he washes his wound in the sea, groaning. Notice the shift of tenses: the narration moves between historic presents (*videmus*, *regit*, *comitantur*, *graditur*) and perfects (*tetigit*, *venit*, *lavit*), which keeps the scene vivid and slightly unstable. Notice also the half-line, *solamenque mali.* (661): this is one of Virgil's unfinished "props", left in the poem when he died. Some manuscripts fill it out with *de collo fistula pendet*, "a pipe hangs from his neck", but that is a later addition, and modern editors leave the line as Virgil left it. And, if you have read Excerpt 1, notice the word *informe*. In Eclogue 2, a few lines after our excerpt ends, Corydon, the lovesick shepherd modelled on Theocritus's lovesick Cyclops, insists *nec sum adeo informis*, "I am not so ugly". Here, at last, is the Cyclops himself, and he is *informis* indeed. Between the Eclogues and the Aeneid, Virgil has turned a comic figure of pastoral into a figure of tragedy.

## Legacy and Impact

Virgil's legacy is so vast that it is hard to know where it ends. Within a few years of his death his poems were being taught in Roman schools, and they remained the basic school text of Latin education for the whole of antiquity and beyond. People scratched lines of the *Aeneid* on the walls of Pompeii. Later Romans even used his poems as an oracle, opening the book at random and reading the first line they found as a prophecy of their future, a practice called the *sortes Vergilianae*.

He became, above all, the model of stylistic perfection: the poet against whom every later Latin poet measured himself. That was especially true for epic. Every Roman epic of the Early Imperial Era was written in his shadow and had to find a way to deal with him. Lucan's *Bellum Civile* (or *Pharsalia*) is in many ways an anti-*Aeneid*, a civil-war epic without gods, without a hero and without hope. Statius ends his *Thebaid* by telling his own poem not to rival "the divine *Aeneid*" but to follow it from a distance and always worship its footprints. Silius Italicus, the author of the *Punica*, owned Virgil's tomb at Naples, visited it as if it were a temple, and celebrated Virgil's birthday more devoutly than his own. The hexameter they all wrote was Virgil's hexameter.

His survival into the Middle Ages owes a great deal to an extraordinary stroke of luck. Eclogue 4, written around 40 BC, announces the birth of a child under whom a new Golden Age will begin. Virgil almost certainly meant the child of a Roman aristocrat, probably connected with the political settlement of that year. But to Christian readers, beginning with Lactantius and the emperor Constantine, the poem looked like a prophecy of the birth of Christ, written by a pagan who had somehow been allowed to glimpse the truth. Virgil was promoted from poet to prophet, and his works were copied, read and protected by the Church with a reverence given to almost no other pagan author. Medieval legend went further and turned him into a magician: in Naples it was said that the city stood safe as long as an egg he had hidden in the foundations of the Castel dell'Ovo remained unbroken.

All this made him so famous that his fame eventually outgrew his poetry, and shifted from his works to his figure. The decisive moment was Dante. In the *Divine Comedy*, Dante chooses Virgil as his guide through Hell and Purgatory, greets him as "my master and my author", and even has the poet Statius claim that Virgil's fourth Eclogue made him a Christian: "through you I became a poet, through you a Christian". From then on, for a great many people, Virgil is first of all "Dante's guide", or simply "the man who wrote the *Aeneid*", rather than the author of poems they have actually read. The process has gone all the way to video games: the *Devil May Cry* series has twin brothers called Dante and Vergil. It is a strange fate for the shy man from Mantua who hid in doorways to avoid being recognised: to become so famous that people know his name without knowing why.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: A (Very Difficult)**

Virgil is hard in almost every criterion at once: poetic word order and transferred epithets, poetic plurals, Greek forms, archaisms, elision, technical vocabulary in the *Georgics*, and a density of literary and mythological allusion that rarely lets up. What keeps him from S is his honesty: compared with Horace there is no wilful game-playing with the reader, the narrative of the *Aeneid* carries you forward, and the Eclogues offer a genuinely approachable way in. Taken work by work: the **Eclogues** from B to low A, and the right place to start; the **Aeneid** in the middle to upper range of A; the **Georgics** the hardest of all, pushing into S. Overall, a firm A. Do not start with the *Aeneid*.

# QUINTUS HORATIUS FLACCUS -- HORACE (65 -- 8 BC)

## Biography

If Virgil is the shy genius of the Augustan Age, Horace is its best company. He is the friend who never takes himself too seriously, who will tell you your vices to your face, and then pour you a cup of Falernian to help you drink over them. He laughs at the greedy, the ambitious, the bores and the social climbers, and he laughs just as hard at himself: short, fat, prematurely grey, quick to lose his temper and quick to calm down again, and, by his own description, "a pig from Epicurus's herd". And then, having made you laugh, he writes some of the most beautiful lyric poetry in Latin. He is the great lyric poet of the Augustan Age, and probably the most quotable poet Rome ever produced.

**The freedman's son.** Quintus Horatius Flaccus was born on 8 December 65 BC at Venusia, a military colony on the border between Apulia and Lucania in southern Italy. His father was a freedman, a former slave, who worked as a *coactor*, a collector of payments at auctions: a modest job, but a profitable one. He made one decision that changed his son's life: instead of sending the boy to the local school, where the sons of centurions went with their satchels and their writing tablets, he took him to Rome to be educated alongside the children of senators and knights, and went with him to every lesson himself, as a guardian. Horace never forgot it. In one of his satires he writes that if he has any good qualities at all, he owes them to his father, and that he would not swap him for any noble parent in Rome. His schoolmaster in Rome, Orbilius, is remembered in literature for one thing only: Horace calls him *plagosus*, "fond of the whip", the earliest famous school beating in history.

**The general who ran away.** Around 45 BC he went to Athens to finish his education in philosophy, which is where he was when Caesar was murdered. Brutus came to Athens recruiting young Romans for the cause of the Republic, and Horace, the son of a former slave, found himself made a *tribunus militum*, one of the senior officers of a legion: a sign either of his talent or of how desperate Brutus was. At Philippi in 42 BC the republican army was destroyed, and Horace, by his own cheerful admission, ran. In an ode written years later he reminds an old comrade of the day "when I felt that swift flight with you, and left my little shield behind, not very gloriously" (*relicta non bene parmula*), and adds that the god Mercury picked him up in a cloud and carried him out of the battle. The joke is a literary one too: the Greek lyric poets Archilochus, Alcaeus and Anacreon had all boasted of throwing away their shields and running for their lives, so Horace makes his own cowardice a qualification for joining the club.

**Poverty made me a poet.** He came back to Italy under an amnesty to find his father dead and the family land confiscated. He bought himself a job as a clerk in the treasury, and, as he says, "bold poverty drove me to write verses". The verses were good enough to catch the attention of Virgil and Varius, who in about 38 BC introduced him to Maecenas.

**Maecenas.** The first meeting, as Horace describes it, was a disaster of shyness. He stammered out a few words, "for speechless shame stopped me from saying more"; Maecenas answered briefly, as was his habit; Horace went away. Then, nine months later, Maecenas called him back and asked him to join his circle of friends. It was the beginning of one of the great friendships of antiquity. Maecenas gave him financial security and, around 33 BC, a farm in the Sabine hills northeast of Rome, the place Horace loved most in the world, which appears again and again in his poems as the symbol of the quiet, independent life. In return Horace gave him the dedications of his books, his loyalty, and a promise: in one ode he swears that he will not outlive his friend, and that when Maecenas goes, they will go together. He very nearly kept it. Maecenas died in 8 BC, leaving a will that asked Augustus to "remember Horace as you would remember me". Horace died less than two months later, and was buried next to him on the Esquiline.

**The emperor's admirer.** Augustus liked him enormously, and wanted him close. He offered Horace the post of his private secretary, and Horace politely declined, pleading poor health; Augustus, to his credit, took no offence, and went on writing him teasing letters. In one he calls Horace "a most charming little man". In another, after receiving a slim book of his poems, he jokes that Horace seems to be afraid his books will be bigger than he is, since what Horace lacks in height he makes up for in girth. And in a third he complains, half seriously, that Horace never addresses any of his poems to him: "Are you afraid that being seen as my friend will damage your reputation with posterity?" Horace took the hint, and wrote him a long verse letter, the first in his second book of *Epistles*. In 17 BC Augustus chose him to write the official hymn for the Secular Games, the *Carmen Saeculare*: the freedman's son who had fought against Caesar's heir at Philippi had become the poet laureate of his regime.

**The last years.** He spent his last years between Rome and his Sabine farm, writing his last book of odes and his literary letters, and he died on 27 November 8 BC, a few days before his fifty-seventh birthday, leaving Augustus as his heir. He had predicted his own fame at the end of his third book of odes, in one of the proudest boasts in literature: *exegi monumentum aere perennius*, "I have built a monument more lasting than bronze", and *non omnis moriar*, "I shall not wholly die". He was right.

## Most Important Works

- **Sermones** (*Satires*, Book I c. 35 BC, Book II c. 30 BC) -- two books of hexameter "conversations", eighteen poems in all, in the tradition of Lucilius but gentler and more self-ironic. Horace's motto is *ridentem dicere verum*, "to tell the truth while laughing". The targets are the vices of everyday Roman life: greed, ambition, adultery, gluttony, superstition and the restless discontent that makes everyone want someone else's life. Among the most famous: the journey to Brundisium (I.5), the bore who will not let Horace go on the Via Sacra (I.9), and the town mouse and the country mouse (II.6). [Complete]
- **Epodi** or **Iambi** (*Epodes*, c. 30 BC) -- seventeen poems of iambic verse modelled on the Greek poet Archilochus, the founder of poetic invective. They include curses and insults (against the poet Mevius, against the witch Canidia), but also anguished poems on the civil wars, a celebration of the victory at Actium, and the famous praise of country life, *Beatus ille*, which turns out at the very end to have been spoken by a moneylender who promptly goes back to lending money. [Complete]
- **Carmina** (*Odes*, Books I-III 23 BC, Book IV c. 13 BC) -- one hundred and three lyric poems in the metres of the Greek poets Alcaeus and Sappho, Horace's masterpiece and his claim to be the first to bring Greek lyric into Latin. The themes are friendship, love, wine, the seasons, the shortness of life and the need to enjoy the present wisely, moderation (the *aurea mediocritas*), Rome and its moral renewal (the six "Roman Odes" that open Book III), Augustus and Actium (*Nunc est bibendum*, on the death of Cleopatra), and the immortality of poetry. [Complete]
- **Carmen Saeculare** (*Secular Hymn*, 17 BC) -- the official hymn for the Secular Games, the festival that marked the beginning of a new age for Rome, sung by a choir of twenty-seven boys and twenty-seven girls on the Palatine and the Capitol. A prayer to Apollo and Diana for the prosperity of Rome, its families and its harvests. The official record of the games, carved on stone and partly found in 1890, still names him: *carmen composuit Q. Horatius Flaccus*. [Complete]
- **Epistulae** (*Epistles*, Book I c. 20 BC, Book II c. 19-12 BC) -- two books of verse letters to friends, more philosophical and more serene than the *Satires*. Their subject is how to live: independence, contentment, friendship, self-knowledge. They contain some of his most famous phrases: *sapere aude*, "dare to be wise", *nil admirari*, "to be astonished by nothing", and *Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit*, "captive Greece took her savage conqueror captive". [Complete]
- **Ars Poetica** (*The Art of Poetry*, also called the *Epistula ad Pisones*) -- a long verse letter of 476 lines on how to write poetry, especially drama: unity, consistency of character, appropriateness of style, the need to start *in medias res*, to revise for years (*nonumque prematur in annum*, "let it be kept back until the ninth year"), and to aim at both instruction and pleasure. It is the most influential work of literary theory ever written in Latin. [Complete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Virgil is difficult, but he is still readable. Horace is a different matter. Virgil makes you work; Horace plays games with you.

The first game is **hyperbaton**, and in Horace it reaches lengths found nowhere else. Words that belong together grammatically, an adjective and its noun, a preposition and its object, are pulled apart and placed at opposite ends of a line, or of a whole stanza, with other words packed in between. In the *Odes* this is not decoration but structure: every word sits in exactly the place where its sound, its metre and its meaning do the most work. Nietzsche described an ode of Horace as a mosaic of words in which every single word, by its sound, its position and its meaning, radiates its force to right and left and over the whole. For the translator, that mosaic has to be taken apart tile by tile before it can be put back together in Italian or English. Horace himself gave the technique a name in the *Ars Poetica*: the *callida iunctura*, the "clever joining", by which a familiar word, placed in an unexpected combination, becomes new. In practice, for the student, a great deal of *callida iunctura* is simply more hyperbaton.

The second is **vocabulary**. Horace drops Greek words and Greek constructions into Latin without warning, uses rare words and new formations (in the *Ars Poetica* he explicitly defends the poet's right to coin new words, as long as it is done sparingly), and alludes constantly to Greek lyric poems the reader is expected to recognise. **Lexicon: HIGH; Allusion density: HIGH.**

The third is the **sermo cotidianus** of the *Satires* and *Epistles*. Here the problem is the opposite of the *Odes*: not compression into a perfect mosaic, but conversation. Horace writes the way educated Romans talked, rapid-fire and disorienting: unannounced changes of speaker, imaginary objections, half-sentences, ellipses, colloquial idioms, jokes that depend on a tone of voice we cannot hear. The *Satires* are the recommended starting point for Horace, and rightly, because the word order is closer to prose and the situations are often funny. But they are still tricky. **Syntactic complexity: HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH.**

And if you are trying to scan him, the *Odes* are a nightmare of their own. Virgil wrote in one metre. Horace uses around a dozen: the Sapphic stanza, the Alcaic stanza, several kinds of Asclepiad system, Archilochian and Pythiambic couplets, and more, each with its own rules, its own fixed and variable syllables and its own pauses, changing from one poem to the next.

None of this is a reason to avoid him. Horace is one of the most interesting authors in Latin to translate precisely because every line is a puzzle with a solution, and the solution is always worth finding.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Carmina II.10 -- The Golden Mean

**Source:** Horace, *Carmina* (*Odes*), Book II, poem 10, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/horace/carm2.shtml)

**Context:** An ode addressed to a friend called Licinius, probably Licinius Murena, the brother-in-law of Maecenas, and a man with a famously impulsive temperament. The theme is the *aurea mediocritas*, the "golden mean": the Aristotelian idea that virtue lies between two extremes, applied here to fortune and ambition. The irony is painful. If this Licinius is Murena, he did not take the advice: around 23-22 BC, about the time this book of odes was published, he was implicated in a conspiracy against Augustus and executed. of a palace that breeds envy.

The giant pine is shaken more often
by the winds, and lofty towers fall
with a heavier crash, and the lightning strikes
the highest peaks of the mountains.

A heart well prepared hopes in bad times
and fears in good times
for a change of fortune. Jupiter brings back
the ugly winters; he also

takes them away. If things are bad now, they will not
always be so: sometimes Apollo wakes
the silent Muse with his lyre, and does not
always draw his bow.

In hard times show yourself spirited
and brave; and in the same way, wisely,
when the wind is too favourable you will take in
your swelling sails.

**Analysis:**

First, a warning about the famous phrase. *Aurea mediocritas* does not mean "golden mediocrity" in the modern sense of being average and unambitious. *Mediocritas* is the middle point between two extremes, the Aristotelian mean, and it is "golden" because it is precious. The poem's argument is not "aim low" but "do not go to extremes, in either direction": not too far out to sea, not too close to the rocks; not a slum, not a palace; not despair in bad times, not overconfidence in good ones.

The structure is a ring. The ode opens and closes with the same image, sailing: in the first stanza Licinius must neither push out too far nor hug the coast, in the last he must be brave when the wind is against him and take in sail when it is too favourable. In between, the third stanza is a tricolon of disasters that strike whatever stands highest (the tallest pine, the highest towers, the mountain peaks), and the fourth and fifth turn to the gods: Jupiter brings winters and takes them away, and Apollo does not always shoot his arrows. Fortune always changes; a wise man expects it.

This is a good place to watch Horace's word order at work. In the second stanza, *obsoleti* waits until the start of the next line for its noun, *tecti*; *invidenda* is separated from *aula* by *sobrius*. The sense also runs across the ends of stanzas: the fourth stanza ends on *idem*, and the verb, *summovet*, only arrives at the start of the fifth, so that Jupiter literally takes the winter away across the gap. The grammar is mostly straightforward once the words have been reunited: a future used as advice (*Rectius vives*, *contrahes*), gerunds in the ablative (*urgendo*, *premendo*, "by pressing"), datives with *sperat* and *metuit* (*infestis*, *secundis*, "in adverse and favourable circumstances"), and an imperative, *appare*. Note *montis* and *Informis*, old accusative plurals in *-is*. And if you have read the Virgil entry, notice *Informis hiemes*, "ugly winters": the same adjective Virgil uses for Polyphemus.

### Excerpt 2: Epodi 10 -- A Curse on Mevius

**Source:** Horace, *Epodi*, poem 10 (complete), from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/horace/epodes.shtml)

**Context:** A *propempticon* is a send-off poem, wishing a friend a safe voyage; Horace himself wrote a famous one for Virgil's ship (*Odes* I.3). Epode 10 is the exact opposite: an anti-*propempticon*, a poem wishing a ship every possible disaster, because Mevius is on board. Mevius was a bad poet, and Virgil had already mocked him in the *Eclogues* along with his partner in crime Bavius: "whoever does not hate Bavius, let him love your poems, Mevius". Nobody knows exactly what Mevius did to deserve this; it may simply be that he wrote badly. The model is Archilochus, the Greek inventor of poetic invective, one of whose curses on a former friend, wishing him shipwrecked naked on the coast of Thrace, survives on a papyrus. The metre is the epodic couplet: an iambic trimeter followed by a shorter iambic dimeter.

**Latin Text:**

> Mala soluta navis exit alite
> ferens olentem Mevium.
> ut horridis utrumque verberes latus,
> Auster, memento fluctibus;
> niger rudentis Eurus inverso mari
> fractosque remos differat;
> insurgat Aquilo, quantus altis montibus
> frangit trementis ilices;
> nec sidus atra nocte amicum adpareat,
> qua tristis Orion cadit;
> quietiore nec feratur aequore
> quam Graia victorum manus,
> cum Pallas usto vertit iram ab Ilio
> in inpiam Aiacis ratem.
> o quantus instat navitis sudor tuis
> tibique pallor luteus
> et illa non virilis heiulatio
> preces et aversum ad Iovem,
> Ionius udo cum remugiens sinus
> Noto carinam ruperit.
> opima quodsi praeda curvo litore
> porrecta mergos iuverit,
> libidinosus immolabitur caper
> et agna Tempestatibus.

**English Translation:**

Under an evil omen the ship slips its moorings and sets out,
carrying stinking Mevius.
Remember, South Wind, to lash both its sides
with savage waves;
let the black East Wind, turning the sea upside down,
scatter its rigging and its broken oars;
let the North Wind rise, as strong as when on the high mountains
it shatters the trembling holm-oaks;
and let no friendly star appear in the black night
where grim Orion sets;
and may he be carried on no calmer sea
than the victorious army of the Greeks
when Pallas turned her anger from burnt Troy
onto the impious ship of Ajax.
Oh, what sweat is waiting for your sailors,
and for you, a sickly yellow pallor,
and that unmanly wailing,
and prayers to a Jupiter who has turned away,
when the Ionian gulf, bellowing under the rainy
South Wind, has smashed your keel!
And if you end up as a rich prize stretched out on the curving shore,
and make a feast for the seabirds,
a lustful billy goat will be sacrificed,
and a lamb, to the Storms.

**Analysis:**

The joke is in the form. A send-off poem should pray for calm seas, favourable winds and a friendly star; Horace runs through the same checklist in reverse. Every wind of the compass is called up in turn (Auster from the south, Eurus from the east, Aquilo from the north, then Notus, another name for the south wind), each with an instruction, in a series of jussive subjunctives (*differat*, *insurgat*, *adpareat*, *feratur*) and a future imperative with an *ut* clause (*ut... verberes... memento*, "remember to lash"). The last couplet completes the parody: a traveller who arrived safely would make a thank-offering to the gods of the sea; Horace promises one to the Storms if Mevius drowns.

In the middle, a myth arrives, as always in Horace, without explanation. After the fall of Troy, the lesser Ajax dragged Cassandra away from the altar of Pallas Athena, and the goddess took her revenge by wrecking his ship on the way home. The reader is supposed to know the story, and to enjoy the hyperbole: Mevius deserves the same storm that the gods sent against the worst sacrilege of the Trojan War.

The vocabulary is part of the fun. *Olentem*, "smelly", is the only thing we are told about Mevius. *Pallor luteus* is a yellowish, sickly pallor; *heiulatio* is a rare word for loud, undignified wailing, from the verb *heiulare*, and Horace adds *non virilis*, "unmanly", to make sure the insult lands. *Rudentes* are a ship's ropes and rigging; *mergi* are diving seabirds, cormorants or gulls. And look at the grammar of the climax: *cum... ruperit*, a future perfect, "when the gulf has broken your keel", followed by *quodsi... iuverit*, "but if you have pleased the birds", both imagining Mevius's death as already accomplished. *Rudentis*, *trementis*: old accusative plurals in *-is* again. *Libidinosus caper* may be a final dig: the goat was proverbially lustful, and a little too appropriate as an offering for Mevius.

A note on the text: The Latin Library prints *ilics*, which is not a word, for *ilices* ("holm-oaks") in line 8, and leaves out the punctuation at the end of line 20, after *ruperit*. Both are corrected here. *Inpiam* is the source's spelling of *impiam*, and has been kept.

### Excerpt 3: Sermones I.5.1-26 -- The Journey to Brundisium

**Source:** Horace, *Sermones* (*Satires*), Book I, poem 5, lines 1-26, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/horace/serm1.shtml)

**Context:** In 37 BC (or possibly 38) Maecenas travelled from Rome to Brundisium on a delicate diplomatic mission, to patch up the relationship between Octavian and Antony, and he took Horace along, together with Virgil, Varius and other friends. Horace turned the journey into a satire, a travel diary in verse, modelled on the *Iter Siculum*, the "Journey to Sicily", of Lucilius (whose fragments are in the Archaic Era chapter). The joke of the poem is that Horace tells us almost nothing about the diplomacy, and everything about bad water, bad inns, mosquitoes, his sore eyes and the comedians they met along the way. These are the first twenty-six lines: from Rome to Aricia, then by canal-boat through the Pontine Marshes, to Anxur, modern Terracina.

**Latin Text:**

> Egressum magna me accepit Aricia Roma
> hospitio modico; rhetor comes Heliodorus,
> Graecorum longe doctissimus; inde Forum Appi
> differtum nautis cauponibus atque malignis.
> hoc iter ignavi divisimus, altius ac nos
> praecinctis unum: minus est gravis Appia tardis.
> hic ego propter aquam, quod erat deterrima, ventri
> indico bellum, cenantis haud animo aequo
> exspectans comites. iam nox inducere terris
> umbras et caelo diffundere signa parabat:
> tum pueri nautis, pueris convicia nautae
> ingerere: 'huc adpelle'; 'trecentos inseris'; 'ohe,
> iam satis est.' dum aes exigitur, dum mula ligatur,
> tota abit hora. mali culices ranaeque palustres
> avertunt somnos; absentem cantat amicam
> multa prolutus vappa nauta atque viator
> certatim; tandem fessus dormire viator
> incipit ac missae pastum retinacula mulae
> nauta piger saxo religat stertitque supinus.
> iamque dies aderat, nil cum procedere lintrem
> sentimus, donec cerebrosus prosilit unus
> ac mulae nautaeque caput lumbosque saligno
> fuste dolat: quarta vix demum exponimur hora.
> ora manusque tua lavimus, Feronia, lympha.
> milia tum pransi tria repimus atque subimus
> inpositum saxis late candentibus Anxur.

**English Translation:**

When I had left great Rome, Aricia welcomed me
with modest hospitality; my companion was the rhetorician Heliodorus,
by far the most learned of the Greeks. From there to Forum Appii,
crammed with boatmen and crooked innkeepers.
This stretch we lazily split into two days, though travellers more briskly
girt than us do it in one: the Appian Way is less of a strain if you take it slowly.
Here, because of the water, which was foul,
I declare war on my stomach, and wait, in no good temper,
while my companions have their dinner. Now night was getting ready
to draw its shadows over the earth and scatter the stars across the sky.
Then the slaves start hurling abuse at the boatmen, the boatmen at the slaves:
"Pull in here!" "You're cramming in three hundred! Hey,
that's enough!" While the fares are collected and the mule is harnessed,
a whole hour goes by. Damned mosquitoes and marsh frogs
keep sleep away; a boatman, soaked in cheap wine,
sings of the girlfriend he left behind, and a passenger
competes with him: at last the passenger, worn out, starts to fall asleep,
and the lazy boatman lets the mule out to graze, ties its rope
to a stone, and lies on his back snoring.
Day was already breaking when we realise that the boat
is not moving at all, until one hothead jumps out
and with a willow cudgel gives both mule and boatman
a beating on the head and back. At last, only at the fourth hour, we are put ashore.
We wash our faces and hands in your spring, Feronia.
Then, after lunch, we crawl three miles and climb up
to Anxur, perched on its far-gleaming white rocks.

**Analysis:**

This is the *sermo cotidianus* in action, and it shows why the *Satires* are both the best way into Horace and still tricky. The narrative is simple, a day-by-day travel diary, and the word order is close to prose. But the texture changes every few lines. Lines 9-10 are a deliberate parody of epic: *iam nox inducere terris / umbras et caelo diffundere signa parabat*, "now night was preparing to draw its shadows over the earth and scatter the stars across the sky", a perfect Virgilian line about nightfall, followed immediately by the slaves and the boatmen screaming at each other. The shouting itself is pure spoken Latin, given without any indication of who is speaking: *huc adpelle* ("pull in here!"), *trecentos inseris* ("you're cramming in three hundred"), *ohe, iam satis est* ("hey, that's enough!"). The translator has to work out the speakers from the sense.

Watch the grammar. *Ingerere* in line 12 is a historic infinitive, used instead of a finite verb to describe a lively scene ("they started hurling"); the narrative jumps constantly between perfects (*accepit*, *divisimus*, *lavimus*) and historic presents (*indico*, *avertunt*, *sentimus*, *exponimur*, *repimus*). *Ventri indico bellum*, "I declare war on my stomach", is mock-military language for refusing to eat, because the water is too dangerous to drink. *Altius praecinctis*, "those girt higher than us", refers to travellers who hitch their tunics up to walk faster. *Vappa* is wine that has gone flat. *Cerebrosus* is a "hothead", from *cerebrum*. And *dolat*, from *dolare*, normally means to hew wood with an axe: the passenger "carpenters" the mule and the boatman with his cudgel.

Then there is the geography, which is real. Aricia is modern Ariccia in the Alban Hills, the first stop south of Rome on the Appian Way. Forum Appii was the start of a canal through the Pontine Marshes, travelled overnight in a barge towed by a mule along the bank, which is how the mule and the boatman both come to be asleep. The spring of the goddess Feronia and the town of Anxur, with the temple of Jupiter Anxur on its gleaming white cliffs, are at Terracina, where the canal ended. The diplomatic mission, the reason for the whole trip, is mentioned in a single line a little later. Everything else is mosquitoes.

## Legacy and Impact

Horace survived antiquity thanks to two different kinds of readers. For the schools, he was immediately a classic: within a generation of his death he was being taught to Roman schoolboys, and Quintilian called him "almost the only Roman lyric poet worth reading". For Christian readers in late antiquity and the Middle Ages, it was above all the *Satires* and the *Epistles* that mattered. A poet who exposed greed, ambition, luxury and hypocrisy, who preached contentment and moderation, and who laughed at the vanity of human desires could be read as a moralist, and medieval readers called him *Horatius ethicus*, "Horace the moralist". He was copied in monasteries all over Europe, and around 850 manuscripts survive, more than for almost any other classical poet.

That reputation is still visible in Dante. In the fourth canto of the *Inferno*, among the great poets of antiquity in Limbo, Horace appears second only to Homer, and Dante calls him *Orazio satiro*, "Horace the satirist": for Dante, the author of the *Satires* came before the author of the *Odes*. Petrarch, who loved him, wrote him a letter in verse. In the Renaissance the *Odes* came back into their own and became the model of lyric poetry across Europe: Ronsard in France, Ben Jonson in England, and in Italy the poets who tried to reproduce his metres in Italian, culminating centuries later in Carducci's *Odi barbare*. The *Ars Poetica*, meanwhile, became the rulebook of classical literary theory, the direct ancestor of Boileau's *Art poétique* and Pope's *Essay on Criticism*. For centuries Horace was not just a poet but the master of style, the authority educated Europeans quoted on how to write, how to judge and how to live.

And then the modern world forgot almost all of it and kept two words. *Carpe diem* comes from a short ode of eight lines, *Odes* I.11, addressed to a woman called Leuconoe who is trying to find out from astrologers how long she and the poet have to live. Horace tells her to stop asking, because nobody can know, and to be wise, strain the wine, cut back long hopes, and *carpe diem, quam minimum credula postero*: "pluck the day, trusting as little as possible in tomorrow". *Carpere* is a gardener's verb: you pluck a fruit when it is ripe, carefully, one at a time. The message is Epicurean, and it is about moderation: do not waste the present worrying about a future you cannot control. Modern culture, helped along by a famous film, has turned it into the opposite: "YOLO", live as if there were no tomorrow, party as hard as you can because you might die. It is hard to think of a worse misreading. The man who wrote *carpe diem* also wrote the ode on the golden mean, the warning to take in sail when the wind is too favourable, and two books of satires against people who cannot control their appetites.

Read him properly, and Horace is far more than a slogan: a satirist who laughs at everyone including himself, a moralist who never preaches, a friend who is loyal to the end, and one of the greatest lyric poets who ever lived.

## Final Rating

**Tier: S (GOD PLEASE HELP)**

Horace scores at or near the top in almost every criterion. In the *Odes*, the hyperbaton is more extreme than in any other Latin poet, the vocabulary mixes Greek words, rare words and new formations, the allusions to Greek lyric are constant and unexplained, and the metres change from poem to poem. In the *Satires* and *Epistles*, the challenge shifts to rapid, colloquial conversation, with changes of speaker that are never signalled and jokes that depend on tone. He is leagues harder than Virgil, who is difficult but honest; Horace is difficult on purpose. The *Satires* are the right place to start, but even they are tricky; the *Odes* are where the real summit is. Mid to high S, and well deserved. And worth every minute.
