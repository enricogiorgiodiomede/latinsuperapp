# AUGUSTAN ERA (44 BC -- 14 AD)

The men who stabbed Caesar in 44 BC believed they were saving the Republic. Instead they handed Rome to a sickly nineteen-year-old nobody had taken seriously. Octavian, Caesar's adopted son, avenged him at Philippi, shared the world with Mark Antony for a decade, and beat Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BC. In 27 BC he announced, with a perfectly straight face, that he was giving the Republic back; the grateful Senate gave him the name *Augustus* and, in practice, everything else. He ruled as *princeps*, "first citizen", for forty-one years, offering an exhausted Rome peace and a program to go with it: old gods, old morals, laws against adultery, and a city he claimed to have found in brick and left in marble. On his deathbed, says Suetonius, he asked his friends whether he had played his part in the comedy of life well, and if so, would they kindly applaud.

This is the **Golden Age** of Latin literature, the pinnacle of stylistic perfection. The neoterics had taught Roman poets to polish every line; the Augustans kept the polish and aimed it at the greatest Greek models. Virgil takes on Homer, Horace takes on Alcaeus, Sappho and Pindar. Rome no longer wants to translate Greece. It wants to equal it, and for once it arguably does.

The price is that the greatest poets of the era are **poets at the service of the state**. Maecenas, Augustus's friend and unofficial minister of culture, gathered Virgil, Horace and Propertius into his circle and gave them money and land. In return the regime got the *Aeneid*, an epic whose whole plot points towards Augustus, and Horace's *Carmen Saeculare*, the official hymn of the festival of 17 BC. Propaganda, in some sense, although nobody handed out scripts: the poets were friends of the regime and knew who was paying for the farm. When they did not want to write something, they used the *recusatio*, explaining in exquisite verse that they would love to sing Augustus's wars but their talent was, sadly, far too small. It is the most elegant way ever invented of saying no to an emperor.

Not everyone sang in the official key. The era's own Roman genre, **love elegy** (Gallus, Tibullus, Propertius, Ovid), was its counter-culture: the elegists refuse careers, war and marriage, and live as "slaves" to a mistress usually married to someone else. In a city passing laws against adultery, this was not a harmless hobby.

And that is the darker side of the Golden Age: Rome discovered **censorship**. In AD 8 Ovid, the most fashionable poet in the city, was banished to the Black Sea for *carmen et error*, "a poem and a mistake". The poem was the *Ars Amatoria*, a manual of seduction, and his books were removed from Rome's public libraries; he spent his last ten years begging to come home and never did. The historian Titus Labienus, so savage that people called him *Rabienus*, "the rabid one", had his books burned by order of the Senate, the first time Rome punished literature that way; he shut himself in his family tomb rather than outlive them. To be fair, Augustus censored himself too: he wrote a tragedy on Ajax, judged it bad and wiped it out with a sponge, joking that his Ajax had fallen on his sponge instead of his sword.

For a translator, the battlefield changes. In Caesar's era the enemy was Cicero's periodic sentence and Sallust's archaism; here it is mostly **verse**: the hexameter (Virgil, Ovid's *Metamorphoses*), the elegiac couplet (Tibullus, Sulpicia, Propertius) and the Greek lyric metres Horace brought to Rome. The archaisms are gone; in their place come free word order, poetic plurals and unexplained mythology. In Horace's *Quis multa gracilis te puer in rosa*, *multa* waits four words for *rosa*, and *te*, the girl, sits right between *gracilis* and *puer*, so the boy is literally wrapped around her. The result is a sharp spike in difficulty compared with Caesar's era: with so much of the literature written in verse, many of these authors are hard going, and Virgil and Horace above all. The prose is gentler, from Augustus's plain *Res Gestae* and Vitruvius's honest technical Latin to Livy's long, flowing periods.

The corpus is in excellent shape where it matters most. Virgil, Horace and Ovid survive complete, and Virgil only because Augustus refused his dying wish to burn the unfinished *Aeneid*. Livy survives in 35 of 142 books, the *Res Gestae* in a copy carved on a temple wall in Ankara. At the other extreme, Gallus, the founder of elegy, is down to about ten lines on a papyrus scrap found in Egypt in 1978.

# AUGUSTUS (63 BC -- 14 AD)

## Biography

Gaius Octavius was born in Rome on 23 September 63 BC, the year of Cicero's consulship and the Catilinarian conspiracy, into a respectable but unremarkable equestrian family from Velitrae, in the hills south of Rome. His father died when he was four. What made him remarkable was his mother, Atia, who was the niece of Julius Caesar. Caesar had no legitimate son, took an interest in the clever, sickly boy, and brought him along on campaign in Spain. In March 44 BC the eighteen-year-old Octavius was studying at Apollonia, across the Adriatic, when the news arrived: Caesar had been murdered, and his will adopted Octavius as his son and heir. Against the advice of his family, he went to Rome to claim the inheritance and, above all, the name. From now on he was Gaius Julius Caesar, and that name was worth more than any army.

**The boy who owed everything to his name.** Nobody took him seriously at first. Mark Antony, Caesar's right-hand man, called him "a boy who owes everything to his name". Cicero thought he could use him against Antony, and summed up his plan in a pun that somebody, inevitably, repeated to Octavian: the young man was to be *laudandum, ornandum, tollendum*, "praised, honoured, and lifted up", where *tollere* also means "got rid of". Octavian raised a private army out of Caesar's veterans, fought for the Senate against Antony at Mutina in 43 BC, and watched both consuls of the year, Hirtius and Pansa, die in the campaign (the same Hirtius who finished Caesar's *Gallic War*; ancient gossip, needless to say, blamed Octavian). Then he marched on Rome, had himself made consul at nineteen, and changed sides: he formed the Second Triumvirate with Antony and Lepidus, and the three drew up the proscription lists. Cicero's name was on them. So much for *tollendum*.

**Philippi, Actium, and a broken nose.** In 42 BC the triumvirs destroyed Brutus and Cassius at Philippi. Antony did the fighting; Octavian, who was ill, spent part of the battle hiding in a marsh for three days. For the next decade the Roman world was split: Antony took the East, and Cleopatra; Octavian took Italy and the West, the unpopular confiscations of land for veterans, and the war against Sextus Pompeius, which was won for him in 36 BC by his best friend and admiral, Marcus Agrippa. Lepidus was quietly pushed aside. The final showdown came at Actium on 2 September 31 BC, and again it was Agrippa who won it. The following year, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra killed themselves. Octavian went to see the embalmed body of Alexander the Great, touched it, and (says Cassius Dio) accidentally broke off a piece of the nose. Asked if he would also like to see the tombs of the Ptolemies, he said he had come to see a king, not a row of corpses.

**The restored Republic.** At thirty-three he was master of the world, and he had learned from Caesar's mistakes: Caesar had ruled openly as dictator and had been stabbed for it. So in January 27 BC Octavian solemnly gave the Republic back to the Senate and the People, and the Senate, which had no illusions about who was in charge, gave him the new name *Augustus*, "the revered one". Over the following decades he collected the powers of the old magistrates one by one (the powers of a tribune from 23 BC, the office of *pontifex maximus* in 12 BC, the title of *pater patriae* in 2 BC) without ever holding a title that sounded like "king". He called himself *princeps*, first among citizens, and the system he built, the Principate, lasted three centuries.

**The man.** Suetonius, who had access to the imperial archives, gives us a portrait that is wonderfully unheroic. Augustus was short and wore shoes with thick soles to look taller. His teeth were small, few and decaying. He was so afraid of thunderstorms that he carried a piece of sealskin as a lightning charm, and hid in an underground room when a storm got bad. He loved gambling with dice and wrote cheerful letters about losing. He was also capable of real despair: in AD 9, when Varus lost three legions in the forests of Germany, Augustus went for months without cutting his hair or beard, beating his head against the doors and shouting *Quintili Vare, legiones redde!*, "Quintilius Varus, give me back my legions!"

**The succession problem.** The one thing he could not arrange was an heir. He had a single child, Julia, and a long and genuinely devoted marriage to Livia, whom he had taken from her first husband while she was pregnant. One by one, every successor he chose died before him: his nephew Marcellus in 23 BC, Agrippa in 12 BC, his grandsons Lucius and Gaius in AD 2 and AD 4. He ended up adopting Livia's son Tiberius, whom he never much liked. Julia was exiled for adultery in 2 BC and her daughter, also Julia, in AD 8; Augustus used to call his daughter, his granddaughter and his last grandson "my three boils", and quoted Homer: "I wish I had never married, and died without children."

**The death, and the figs.** He died at Nola on 19 August AD 14, in the month that had already been renamed after him, a few weeks short of his seventy-sixth birthday. Suetonius gives him a perfect theatrical exit: he asked for a mirror, had his hair combed, asked his friends whether he had played his part in the comedy of life well, and quoted the Greek line that ends a play, "if the show has pleased you, give it your applause". His last words were to Livia: "Livia, live mindful of our marriage, and farewell." Cassius Dio reports a darker version. Augustus, who was careful about what he ate, liked to pick figs with his own hands straight from the trees in his garden. Livia, so the rumour went, smeared poison on the figs still hanging there, ate the clean ones herself, and let her husband eat the rest, to make sure Tiberius succeeded before Augustus could change his mind. Nobody can prove it, and Dio himself only reports it as something "some suspected", but it has been too good a story to die. The Senate made him a god, helped by a former praetor who swore he had seen Augustus's spirit rising from the funeral pyre into the sky; Dio adds that Livia rewarded him with a million sesterces for his excellent eyesight.

## Most Important Works

- **Res Gestae Divi Augusti** (*The Achievements of the Divine Augustus*, finished AD 13-14) -- Augustus's own account of his career in thirty-five short chapters, written in the first person in his seventy-sixth year and left with his will, with orders to have it inscribed on two bronze pillars in front of his mausoleum. Modern editors divide it into three parts: the honours he received (chapters 1-14), the money he spent on the Roman people (15-24), and his deeds in war and peace (25-35), with a short appendix added after his death. The bronze originals are lost; the text survives because copies were carved on temples in the province of Galatia, above all the Temple of Rome and Augustus at Ankara. [Complete]
- **De Vita Sua** (*On His Own Life*) -- an autobiography in thirteen books, taking the story up to the Cantabrian war in Spain (around 25 BC). [Lost]
- **Letters** -- Augustus was a lively and prolific letter-writer, and Suetonius and Aulus Gellius quote him often, which is how we know about his spelling habits and his dice games. [Fragmentary]
- **Literary experiments** -- a hexameter poem called *Sicilia*, a book of epigrams written mostly in the bath, a reply to Brutus's praise of Cato, and a tragedy on Ajax, which he destroyed himself: when asked how his Ajax was doing, he said it had "fallen on its sponge". [Lost]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Augustus had firm opinions about Latin style, and Suetonius records them gleefully. He wanted above all to be clear. He avoided what he called "the stench of far-fetched words", mocked Maecenas for his perfumed, curling prose, mocked Tiberius for digging up obsolete words, and mocked Antony for writing in a style that people admired rather than understood. He repeated prepositions rather than leave any doubt about the construction. His everyday speech was full of homely expressions: someone who would never pay would pay "on the Greek Kalends", a quick job was done "faster than asparagus cooks".

The *Res Gestae* is that taste for clarity carved in stone. It is official, inscriptional prose: short sentences, almost always in the first person, almost always ending on a perfect-tense verb (*comparavi*, *vindicavi*, *feci*, *refeci*, *dedi*). The syntax is the gentlest of any author so far after Nepos. The main constructions are the ablative absolute, used constantly for dates (*C. Pansa et A. Hirtio consulibus*, "when Pansa and Hirtius were consuls"), relative clauses, a few subordinate clauses with *cum* and *ut*, and lists, lists, lists. **Syntactic complexity: LOW; Stylistic figures: LOW.**

The real difficulty is elsewhere. The first is **lexicon**: the text is full of the technical vocabulary of Roman institutions (*tribunicia potestas*, *imperium*, *ovatio*, *supplicatio*, *lustrum*, *congiarium*, *aurum coronarium*) and of Roman numbers, written the Roman way: distributives like *trecenos*, "three hundred each", multiplicative adverbs like *quinquiens* and *viciens et semel*, and sums in sesterces such as *HS milliens*, which means a hundred million. A student who has never met these will spend more time with the dictionary than with the grammar. **Lexicon: MEDIUM.** The second is **context**. The *Res Gestae* is propaganda by omission, and it is a masterpiece of the genre. Antony, Brutus and Cassius, Lepidus and Cleopatra are never named; the proscriptions are never mentioned; civil wars become "wars against the enemies of the Republic", and Sextus Pompeius becomes a pirate. To translate it is easy. To understand what it is not saying, you need to know the history of the previous thirty years. **Allusion density: MEDIUM to HIGH, but historical rather than literary.**

This is exactly why the *Res Gestae* is the best possible way into the Augustan Era. In thirty-five short chapters it gives you the whole political frame that the poets of the period take for granted: the civil wars, the victory, the "restored Republic", the buildings, the games, the conquests, the peace, and the man at the centre of it all. Virgil, Horace, Livy and Ovid are much easier to understand once you have read the official version they are writing with, around or against. It is also an ideal text for brushing up on prose before the era's poetry hits: clean classical Latin, regular word order, standard constructions, and a sense of moving quickly through a real text. Read it first.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Res Gestae 1-3 -- Part I: the Rise to Power

**Source:** Augustus, *Res Gestae Divi Augusti*, chapters 1-3, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/augustus/resgestae.html)

**Context:** The opening of the *Res Gestae* and of its first part, on the honours Augustus received. In three chapters he covers the years from 44 to about 30 BC: the private army he raised after Caesar's murder, the war of Mutina, his first consulship, the triumvirate, Philippi and the civil wars. That is, the most violent fourteen years of his life. Read it with the biography above open beside it, and count how many of those events are actually named.

**Latin Text:**

[1] Annos undeviginti natus exercitum privato consilio et privata impensa comparavi, per quem rem publicam a dominatione factionis oppressam in libertatem vindicavi. [Ob quae] senatus decretis honorificis in ordinem suum me adlegit, C. Pansa et A. Hirtio consulibus, consularem locum sententiae dicendae tribuens, et imperium mihi dedit. Res publica ne quid detrimenti caperet, me propraetore simul cum consulibus providere iussit. Populus autem eodem anno me consulem, cum cos. uterque bello cecidisset, et triumvirum rei publicae constituendae creavit.

[2] Qui parentem meum trucidaverunt, eos in exilium expuli iudiciis legitimis ultus eorum facinus, et postea bellum inferentis rei publicae vici bis acie.

[3] Bella terra et mari civilia externaque toto in orbe terrarum saepe gessi, victorque omnibus veniam petentibus civibus peperci. Externas gentes, quibus tuto ignosci potuit, conservare quam excidere malui. Millia civium Romanorum sub sacramento meo fuerunt circiter quingenta. Ex quibus deduxi in colonias aut remisi in municipia sua stipendis emeritis millia aliquanto plura quam trecenta, et iis omnibus agros adsignavi aut pecuniam pro praemiis militiae dedi. Naves cepi sescentas praeter eas, si quae minores quam triremes fuerunt.

**English Translation:**

[1] At the age of nineteen, on my own initiative and at my own expense, I raised an army, with which I set free the Republic, oppressed by the tyranny of a faction. For this the Senate, with honorific decrees, enrolled me in its order in the consulship of Gaius Pansa and Aulus Hirtius, giving me the right to speak among the former consuls, and it granted me military command. It ordered me, as propraetor, to see to it together with the consuls that the Republic should suffer no harm. And in the same year, since both consuls had fallen in war, the people made me consul and triumvir for the restoration of the Republic.

[2] Those who butchered my father I drove into exile, avenging their crime through lawful courts; and afterwards, when they made war on the Republic, I defeated them twice in battle.

[3] I waged wars, civil and foreign, by land and sea, all over the world, many times, and as victor I spared all citizens who asked for mercy. Foreign peoples who could safely be pardoned I preferred to preserve rather than destroy. About five hundred thousand Roman citizens were bound to me by the military oath. Of these I settled in colonies, or sent back to their own towns when their service was over, somewhat more than three hundred thousand, and to all of them I assigned land or gave money as a reward for their service. I captured six hundred ships, not counting any that were smaller than triremes.

**Analysis:**

*Annos undeviginti natus*, "at the age of nineteen": the first words of the text are not a name or a title but an age, and they set the tone of everything that follows. The boy that Antony and Cicero underestimated becomes the young saviour who acted when nobody else would. Look at how he presents the facts. A nineteen-year-old raising an army of his own, *privato consilio et privata impensa*, was flatly illegal: no magistrate had authorised it. Augustus turns that illegality into a virtue, repeating *privato... privata* so that it sounds like personal generosity. The "faction" whose tyranny oppressed the Republic is Antony's, but Antony is never named, here or anywhere else in the text. Nor are the consuls' deaths explained (*cum cos. uterque bello cecidisset*, a simple fact of war), nor what the *triumvir rei publicae constituendae* actually did in his first months in office: the proscriptions, and the murder of Cicero, simply do not exist.

The phrase *Res publica ne quid detrimenti caperet*, "that the Republic should suffer no harm", is a legal formula: the wording of the Senate's emergency decree, the *senatus consultum ultimum*. It is the same decree that Caesar complains about in *Bellum Civile* I.7, used this time in favour of a Caesar rather than against one. Augustus quotes it because it makes his private army the Senate's army.

Chapter 2 is a small masterpiece of silence. *Qui parentem meum trucidaverunt*, "those who butchered my father": Brutus and Cassius go unnamed, and the verb *trucidare*, a violent, butcher's word, is the only emotional word in the passage. *Iudiciis legitimis* refers to the special court set up in 43 BC that condemned the assassins in their absence; *bis acie* is the two battles of Philippi, one of which Augustus spent in a marsh.

Chapter 3 introduces the great theme of the reign, *clementia*: he spared the citizens who asked, and preferred to preserve foreign peoples "who could safely be pardoned", a very large qualification hidden in a relative clause. Then come the numbers, which are the real challenge for a translator: *millia... circiter quingenta*, where *millia* is separated from its numeral by four words, and *millia aliquanto plura quam trecenta*, "somewhat more than three hundred thousand".

Grammatically, the passage is a good revision test. Note the long line of first-person perfects (*comparavi, vindicavi, expuli, vici, gessi, peperci, malui, deduxi, remisi, adsignavi, dedi, cepi*); the ablatives absolute (*C. Pansa et A. Hirtio consulibus*, *stipendis emeritis*); the deponent participle *ultus*, "having avenged"; the old accusative plural *inferentis* for *inferentes*; *si quae* for *si aliquae*; and above all *quibus tuto ignosci potuit*. Since *ignosco* takes the dative, its passive is impersonal: literally "to whom it was possible to be forgiven safely", that is, "who could safely be pardoned". That one clause will catch more students than the rest of the passage put together.

### Excerpt 2: Res Gestae 20-21 -- Part II: the Builder of Rome

**Source:** Augustus, *Res Gestae Divi Augusti*, chapters 20-21, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/augustus/resgestae.html)

**Context:** From the second part of the *Res Gestae*, the *impensae*: the money Augustus spent on the Roman people out of his own pocket. Chapters 15-18 list cash handouts and grain, chapter 19 lists new buildings, and chapters 20-21 cover restorations and the great monuments paid for out of war booty. This is the Augustus who boasted, according to Suetonius, that he had found Rome a city of brick and left it a city of marble, and many of the buildings named here can still be visited in Rome today.

**Latin Text:**

[20] Capitolium et Pompeium theatrum utrumque opus impensa grandi refeci sine ulla inscriptione nominis mei. Rivos aquarum compluribus locis vetustate labentes refeci, et aquam quae Marcia appellatur duplicavi fonte novo in rivum eius inmisso. Forum Iulium et basilicam quae fuit inter aedem Castoris et aedem Saturni, coepta profligataque opera a patre meo, perfeci et eandem basilicam consumptam incendio, ampliato eius solo, sub titulo nominis filiorum meorum incohavi, et, si vivus non perfecissem, perfici ab heredibus meis iussi. Duo et octoginta templa deum in urbe consul sextum ex auctoritate senatus refeci nullo praetermisso quod eo tempore refici debebat. Consul septimum viam Flaminiam ab urbe Ariminum refeci pontesque omnes praeter Mulvium et Minucium.

[21] In privato solo Martis Ultoris templum forumque Augustum ex manibiis feci. Theatrum ad aedem Apollinis in solo magna ex parte a privatis empto feci, quod sub nomine M. Marcelli generi mei esset. Dona ex manibiis in Capitolio et in aede divi Iuli et in aede Apollinis et in aede Vestae et in templo Martis Ultoris consacravi, quae mihi constiterunt HS circiter milliens. Auri coronari pondo triginta et quinque millia municipiis et colonis Italiae conferentibus ad triumphos meos quintum consul remisi, et postea, quotienscumque imperator appellatus sum, aurum coronarium non accepi decernentibus municipiis et colonis aeque benigne adque antea decreverant.

**English Translation:**

[20] The Capitol and the theatre of Pompey, both of them, I restored at great expense without any inscription of my own name. I restored the channels of the aqueducts, which in many places were collapsing with age, and I doubled the aqueduct called the Marcia by bringing a new spring into its channel. The Forum of Julius and the basilica that stood between the temple of Castor and the temple of Saturn, works begun and nearly finished by my father, I completed; and when that same basilica was destroyed by fire, I began to rebuild it on an enlarged site, under the name of my sons, and gave orders that, if I did not finish it in my lifetime, it should be finished by my heirs. In my sixth consulship, on the authority of the Senate, I restored eighty-two temples of the gods in the city, leaving out none that needed restoring at the time. In my seventh consulship I restored the Via Flaminia from Rome to Ariminum, and all its bridges except the Mulvian and the Minucian.

[21] On land I owned privately, and with the spoils of war, I built the temple of Mars the Avenger and the Forum of Augustus. Next to the temple of Apollo, on ground bought for the most part from private owners, I built a theatre, to bear the name of my son-in-law Marcus Marcellus. With the spoils of war I dedicated gifts on the Capitol and in the temple of the Deified Julius, in the temple of Apollo, in the temple of Vesta and in the temple of Mars the Avenger, which cost me about a hundred million sesterces. Thirty-five thousand pounds of crown gold, which the towns and colonies of Italy were contributing for my triumphs, I remitted in my fifth consulship; and afterwards, whenever I was hailed as *imperator*, I did not accept the crown gold, although the towns and colonies voted it just as generously as they had before.

**Analysis:**

The first sentence is the best joke in the *Res Gestae*, although Augustus did not intend it as one. He restored the Capitol and Pompey's theatre "without any inscription of my own name", and he tells us so in an inscription of his own name, set up in front of his own mausoleum. Modesty, in Augustan Rome, was something you advertised. The choice of buildings is not innocent either: the Capitol was the religious heart of the state, and the theatre of Pompey had been built by the man his adoptive father defeated. Restoring it without his name was a public gesture of reconciliation.

The passage works like a rhythmic inventory. Almost every clause ends on the same verb or one of its family, *refeci... refeci... perfeci... incohavi... refeci... refeci... feci... feci*, so that the city seems to rise again sentence by sentence. Behind the inventory there is a family history. *A patre meo* is Caesar again, whose unfinished projects Augustus completes as a son should. *Sub titulo nominis filiorum meorum*, "under the name of my sons", refers to Gaius and Lucius, his grandsons and adopted sons, both dead by the time he wrote this. The theatre in the name of Marcellus, the nephew and son-in-law who died at nineteen in 23 BC, is the same young man Virgil mourns at the end of *Aeneid* VI. And the temple of *Mars Ultor*, Mars the Avenger, was vowed at Philippi: it is chapter 2's revenge for the "butchered father", turned into marble.

The last sentence is about refusing money, which is the most Augustan kind of boast. *Aurum coronarium*, "crown gold", was the gold that towns traditionally sent a victorious general to make his triumphal crowns. Augustus refused thirty-five thousand pounds of it, and the ablative absolute at the end, *decernentibus municipiis et colonis aeque benigne adque antea decreverant*, makes sure we know that the towns kept offering it.

For the translator, the passage is a lesson in Latin word order and in compact constructions rather than in hard grammar. Watch for the objects placed first, before the verb they depend on (*Capitolium et Pompeium theatrum... refeci*, *Forum Iulium et basilicam... perfeci*), with *utrumque opus* in apposition; the participles *coepta profligataque*, "begun and nearly finished" (*profligare* here means "to bring almost to completion", a sense the dictionary gives second); the passive infinitive after *iussi*, *perfici ab heredibus meis iussi*, "I ordered it to be finished by my heirs"; the pluperfect subjunctive *si vivus non perfecissem*, a condition in reported form; the relative clause of purpose *quod sub nomine M. Marcelli... esset*; *Ariminum* without a preposition, because names of towns take a plain accusative of motion; the adverbial numerals *consul sextum*, *consul septimum*, *quintum consul*; and the old spelling *adque* for *atque*. Then the numbers again: *HS circiter milliens* is about a hundred million sesterces, and *pondo triginta et quinque millia* is thirty-five thousand pounds by weight.

## Legacy and Impact

Augustus's name is still on every calendar: in 8 BC the Senate renamed the month of Sextilis after him, and it has been August ever since. His title became a rank. Every Roman emperor after him was an *Augustus*, just as every one of them was a *Caesar*, and the model of power he invented, a monarchy that insists it is not one, has been imitated by rulers ever since.

The *Res Gestae* itself had an unusual afterlife. The bronze pillars in Rome disappeared, and the text survived only because the province of Galatia, in modern Turkey, had copies carved on its temples. The most complete, in Latin with a Greek translation, is on the walls of the Temple of Rome and Augustus at Ankara, the *Monumentum Ancyranum*, which was copied by a Western embassy to the Ottoman court in 1555. Two other copies, at Antioch in Pisidia and Apollonia, have filled most of the gaps. The great German historian Theodor Mommsen called it the *regina inscriptionum*, the queen of Latin inscriptions.

In 1938, for the two-thousandth anniversary of Augustus's birth, Mussolini had the whole text carved on the wall of the pavilion built to house the reconstructed Ara Pacis, beside the Mausoleum of Augustus in Rome. It is still there, on the side of the modern museum facing Via di Ripetta: the propaganda of one regime, reused by another. Few texts have been used so successfully, twice, for exactly the purpose they were written for.

## Final Rating

**Tier: D (Good Exercise)**

The *Res Gestae* is short, complete, and written in clean, regular classical prose: first-person statements, standard constructions, almost no subordination, almost no figures of speech. Its only real obstacles are institutional vocabulary and Roman numerals, both solved with a good dictionary and a little patience, and its political silences, which make it harder to *understand* than to translate. It is the right text to open the Augustan Era with: it gives the reader the historical background that every other author of the period assumes, and it is a comfortable prose warm-up before the era's poetry, which is a very different matter. A clear D, and one of the most useful texts in the whole project.

# MARCUS VITRUVIUS POLLIO -- VITRUVIUS (c. 80 -- c. 15 BC)

## Biography

For the man who wrote the most influential book on architecture in Western history, we know remarkably little about Vitruvius, and almost all of it comes from his own prefaces. Even his name is uncertain: *Vitruvius* is secure, but *Marcus* rests on weak evidence and *Pollio* comes from a later writer who summarised him. He was probably born in the 80s or 70s BC, possibly in Campania or in central Italy, and he tells us that his parents gave him a broad education in the liberal arts, for which he is still grateful.

**Caesar's artillery man.** As a young man he served Julius Caesar as a military engineer. Together with three colleagues, whom he names, he was in charge of building and repairing *ballistae*, *scorpiones* and the other siege engines of the Roman army, which makes him the Roman equivalent of an artillery officer. Where exactly he served we do not know, though Gaul and Africa are the usual guesses. A German scholar once tried to identify him with Mamurra, Caesar's chief engineer, whom Catullus mocks in some of his crudest poems as a corrupt, bankrupt playboy. Almost nobody accepts the theory, which is a pity, because it would be the best-documented career change in Latin literature.

**A pension from Octavia.** After Caesar's death he passed into the service of Octavian, and here comes the most charming detail of his life. In the dedication of his book he thanks the young Caesar for a pension that allowed him to work without worrying about poverty in his old age, and he adds that the pension was kept up "on the recommendation of your sister", Octavia. The great theorist of Roman architecture, in other words, owed his comfortable retirement to a reference letter from the emperor's sister.

**The ugly architect.** Vitruvius is also the only Roman author who opens a book by apologising for his face. In the preface to Book II he tells the story of Dinocrates, a tall, handsome Greek architect who, unable to get an audience with Alexander the Great, oiled himself, put on a lion skin, crowned himself with poplar leaves, took a club and walked into the king's assembly dressed as Hercules. It worked: Alexander noticed him, and Dinocrates went on to plan the city of Alexandria (after first proposing to carve Mount Athos into a giant man holding a city in his hand, which Alexander politely declined). Vitruvius then turns to Augustus and admits: "nature has not given me height, age has disfigured my face, and illness has taken away my strength". Since he cannot win the emperor over with his looks, he will have to do it with his knowledge. It is the most endearing self-portrait in Latin prose.

**One building.** For all his theory, Vitruvius mentions only one building of his own: a basilica at Fanum Fortunae, modern Fano on the Adriatic coast, which he describes in detail in Book V. For centuries architects tried to reconstruct it from his description, and nobody knew where it stood. In recent years excavations in the centre of Fano have uncovered remains that some archaeologists believe may finally be his basilica, although the identification is still debated. He wrote the *De Architectura* in the 20s BC, as an old man, and dedicated it to Augustus. After that he disappears, and we do not know when he died.

## Most Important Works

- **De Architectura** (*On Architecture*, c. 30-20 BC) -- ten books, dedicated to Augustus, covering everything a Roman architect was expected to know. Book I deals with the education of the architect and the planning of cities; Book II with building materials (and the origins of human housing); Books III and IV with temples and the architectural orders; Book V with public buildings, including the acoustics of theatres; Book VI with private houses; Book VII with plaster, stucco, floors and pigments; Book VIII with water and aqueducts (where he warns that lead pipes are bad for your health, two thousand years ahead of schedule); Book IX with astronomy and sundials; and Book X with machines, from water wheels to siege engines. The text survives complete, but the illustrations he refers to throughout (*subscripta forma*, "a diagram is drawn below") were lost, which has kept readers guessing for two millennia. [Complete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Vitruvius knew perfectly well that he was not a great writer, and he said so. At the end of the first chapter of Book I he asks Caesar and his readers to forgive him if anything is "not quite according to the rules of grammar", because he has written "not as a great philosopher, nor as an eloquent rhetorician, nor as a grammarian trained in the highest principles of the art, but as an architect who has had a taste of letters". This is not false modesty. His Latin is the Latin of a practical man with a technical subject and no literary training: honest, functional and frequently clumsy.

The first difficulty is the **lexicon**, which is enormous and highly technical. Vitruvius needs words for column bases, capitals, cornices, mouldings, types of brick and sand, kinds of lime, the parts of catapults and water organs, and Latin does not have most of them. So he borrows from Greek wholesale, sometimes translating, sometimes not: *symmetria*, *analogia*, *eurythmia*, *taxis*, *gnomonice*, *teleon*. A student meets a new term in almost every line, and many of them are not in a school dictionary. **Lexicon: HIGH.**

The second is **syntax**, which is difficult in a different way from Cicero's or Sallust's. Their sentences are hard because they are built with great art; Vitruvius's sentences are hard because they are not quite built at all. They run on, pile up clauses, change construction halfway through, and occasionally lose track of their subject. The great Renaissance architect Leon Battista Alberti, who owed him almost everything, still complained that Vitruvius wrote in such a way that the Latins thought he wanted to sound Greek and the Greeks thought he was speaking Latin. Add the lost diagrams, which leave several descriptions of machines nearly impossible to visualise, and you get a text that is easy to parse sentence by sentence and surprisingly hard to understand paragraph by paragraph. **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM.**

In everything else he is gentle. He uses almost no rhetorical figures, his allusions are to Greek scientists and mathematicians rather than to myth or poetry, and he likes to break the technical material with good stories: Dinocrates dressed as Hercules, Archimedes running naked through Syracuse, the origins of the Corinthian capital in a basket left on a young girl's grave, around which an acanthus plant grew. **Stylistic figures: LOW; Allusion density: LOW to MEDIUM.** And he writes prose, which in the Augustan Era is already a gift.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: De Architectura I.3.1-2 -- Firmitas, Utilitas, Venustas

**Source:** Vitruvius, *De Architectura*, Book I, chapter 3, sections 1-2, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vitruvius/vitruvius1.html)

**Context:** After two chapters on what an architect must study and on the basic concepts of the discipline, Vitruvius divides architecture into its parts, and then sets out the three requirements that every building must meet. Those three words, *firmitas, utilitas, venustas*, are the most famous sentence ever written about architecture. In the seventeenth century the English diplomat Sir Henry Wotton translated them as "commodity, firmness and delight", and they are still taught in every school of architecture today.

**Latin Text:**

[1] Partes ipsius architecturae sunt tres: aedificatio, gnomonice, machinatio. Aedificatio autem divisa est bipertito, e quibus una est moenium et communium operum in publicis locis conlocatio, altera est privatorum aedificiorum explicatio. Publicorum autem distributiones sunt tres, e quibus est una defensionis, altera religionis, tertia opportunitatis. Defensionis est murorum turriumque et portarum ratio ad hostium impetus perpetuo repellendos excogitata, religionis deorum immortalium fanorum aediumque sacrarum conlocatio, opportunitatis communium locorum ad usum publicum dispositio, uti portus, fora, porticus, balinea, theatra, inambulationes ceteraque, quae isdem rationibus in publicis locis designantur.

[2] Haec autem ita fieri debent, ut habeatur ratio firmitatis, utilitatis, venustatis. Firmitatis erit habita ratio, cum fuerit fundamentorum ad solidum depressio, quaque e materia, copiarum sine avaritia diligens electio; utilitatis autem, [cum fuerit] emendata et sine inpeditione usus locorum dispositio et ad regiones sui cuiusque generis apta et conmoda distributio; venustatis vero, cum fuerit operis species grata et elegans membrorumque commensus iustas habeat symmetriarum ratiocinationes.

**English Translation:**

[1] Architecture itself has three parts: building, gnomonics (the making of sundials) and mechanics. Building in turn is divided into two: one part is the placing of city walls and public works in public spaces, the other is the construction of private buildings. Public buildings are of three kinds: one for defence, another for religion, the third for convenience. Defence means the planning of walls, towers and gates, designed to drive back enemy attacks for all time; religion, the siting of the shrines and sacred temples of the immortal gods; convenience, the layout of common spaces for public use, such as harbours, forums, porticoes, baths, theatres, promenades and all the rest that are designed on the same principles in public places.

[2] All of these must be built in such a way that account is taken of strength, utility and beauty. Account will have been taken of strength when the foundations have been dug down to solid ground and the materials, whatever they are, have been chosen with care and without stinginess; of utility, when the arrangement of the spaces is faultless and allows them to be used without obstruction, and each type of building is placed suitably and conveniently with respect to its orientation; and of beauty, when the appearance of the work is pleasing and elegant, and the proportions of its parts follow the correct calculations of symmetry.

**Analysis:**

This is Vitruvius at his most organised, and it shows how his mind works: by division. Architecture has three parts; building has two; public buildings have three kinds; each kind gets a definition in the genitive (*defensionis est... religionis... opportunitatis...*), with the verb *est* understood after the first. It reads less like an essay than like a table of contents spoken aloud, which is exactly what it is.

Then comes the famous sentence: *ut habeatur ratio firmitatis, utilitatis, venustatis*. Three abstract nouns in *-tas*, in asyndeton, all depending on *ratio*, "account, consideration". Notice that Vitruvius does not say that a building must *be* strong, useful and beautiful, but that the builder must *take account* of strength, utility and beauty. Architecture, for him, is a matter of method. Each quality is then defined with a future perfect (*erit habita ratio, cum fuerit...*), "account will have been taken when there will have been...", the tense of the instruction manual: when you have done this, you will have achieved that. The definition of *venustas* is the one that matters most for the rest of the book: beauty is not taste, it is proportion, *symmetriarum ratiocinationes*, a matter of calculation. That idea leads straight to Excerpt 2.

This passage also shows why Vitruvius needs a careful translator. *Gnomonice* is a Greek word for the science of sundials; *machinatio* covers everything from cranes to catapults; *ad regiones* refers to the orientation of a building towards the points of the compass and the winds, a subject he has just discussed in chapter 2. And *quaque e materia* ("from whatever material") is dropped into the middle of the clause with no grammatical connection, a typical Vitruvian shortcut.

A note on the text: The Latin Library prints *turrimque* for *turriumque*, *distrtbutio* for *distributio*, and leaves out two pieces of punctuation (the comma after *aedificatio* and the break before *venustatis*). All four are plain printing errors and have been corrected here, checked against Krohn's edition on LacusCurtius. The square brackets around *cum fuerit* are the editor's: the words are not in the manuscripts but are needed for the sense.

### Excerpt 2: De Architectura III.1.2-3 -- The Vitruvian Man

**Source:** Vitruvius, *De Architectura*, Book III, chapter 1, sections 2-3, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vitruvius/vitruvius3.html)

**Context:** Book III is about temples, and Vitruvius opens it by explaining that a temple must be built with *symmetria*, the correct proportion of every part to the whole. To show what he means, he turns to the most perfectly proportioned thing in nature: the human body. Around 1490 Leonardo da Vinci read this passage, took a pen, and drew a man inside a circle and a square, with a note in his mirror-writing that opens by naming "Vetruvio architecto" as his source. The drawing, now in Venice, is probably the most reproduced image in the history of art, and it is on the Italian one-euro coin.

**Latin Text:**

[2] Corpus enim hominis ita natura composuit, uti os capitis a mento ad frontem summam et radices imas capilli esset decimae partis, item manus palma ab articulo ad extremum medium digitum tantundem, caput a mento ad summum verticem octavae, cum cervicibus imis ab summo pectore ad imas radices capillorum sextae, <a medio pectore> ad summum verticem quartae. Ipsius autem oris altitudinis tertia est pars ab imo mento ad imas nares, nasum ab imis naribus ad finem medium superciliorum tantundem, ab ea fine ad imas radices capilli frons efficitur item tertiae partis. Pes vero altitudinis corporis sextae, cubitum quartae, pectus item quartae. Reliqua quoque membra suas habent commensus proportiones, quibus etiam antiqui pictores et statuarii nobiles usi magnas et infinitas laudes sunt adsecuti.

[3] Similiter vero sacrarum aedium membra ad universam totius magnitudinis summam ex partibus singulis convenientissimum debent habere commensus responsum. Item corporis centrum medium naturaliter est umbilicus. Namque si homo conlocatus fuerit supinus manibus et pedibus pansis circinique conlocatum centrum in umbilico eius, circumagendo rotundationem utrarumque manuum et pedum digiti linea tangentur. Non minus quemadmodum schema rotundationis in corpore efficitur, item quadrata designatio in eo invenietur. Nam si a pedibus imis ad summum caput mensum erit eaque mensura relata fuerit ad manus pansas, invenietur eadem latitudo uti altitudo, quemadmodum areae quae ad normam sunt quadratae.

**English Translation:**

[2] For nature has composed the human body in such a way that the face, from the chin to the top of the forehead and the lowest roots of the hair, is a tenth of the whole height; the palm of the hand, from the wrist to the tip of the middle finger, the same; the head, from the chin to the crown, an eighth; from the top of the chest, including the base of the neck, to the lowest roots of the hair, a sixth; from the middle of the chest to the crown, a quarter. Of the height of the face itself, one third runs from the bottom of the chin to the bottom of the nostrils; the nose, from the bottom of the nostrils to the point between the eyebrows, is the same; and from that point to the lowest roots of the hair, the forehead also makes up a third. The foot is a sixth of the height of the body, the forearm a quarter, the chest also a quarter. The other limbs, too, have their own proportions, and it was by using them that the famous painters and sculptors of antiquity won great and boundless praise.

[3] In the same way, the parts of sacred temples must have, from each single part up to the total size of the whole, the most harmonious correspondence of measurements. Likewise, the natural centre of the body is the navel. For if a man is laid on his back with his hands and feet spread out, and the point of a compass is placed on his navel, then by drawing a circle around him the fingers of both his hands and the toes of both his feet will touch the line. And just as a circle can be traced on the body, a square will also be found in it. For if you measure from the soles of the feet to the top of the head, and set that measure against the outstretched hands, the width will be found to be the same as the height, just as in surfaces squared off with a set square.

**Analysis:**

The first section is a list of fractions, and it is a fine exercise in the genitive of quality and measure: *esset decimae partis*, "was of a tenth part", then *octavae*, *sextae*, *quartae*, with *partis* understood each time and the verb *esset* carried silently through the whole series. Vitruvius never repeats what he can leave out, which is the main reason the passage looks harder than it is. Read *est* or *esset* and *partis* into every clause and it falls into place.

The second section contains the image that made the passage immortal. A man lying on his back, arms and legs spread out, a compass on his navel: the circle touches his fingers and toes. Then, measured from head to foot and from fingertip to fingertip, the same body fits into a square. The circle and the square were, for ancient and Renaissance thinkers alike, the two perfect geometrical figures, so the human body turns out to be the meeting point of geometry and nature. And since temples must be proportioned like bodies, a well-built temple is a kind of portrait of man. Leonardo's famous drawing is, quite literally, an illustration of these two sentences, and a correction of them: Leonardo noticed that if the man is to fit both figures, the circle and the square cannot share a centre, so he moved the square down and put its centre at the groin.

Grammatically, watch the future perfects again (*conlocatus fuerit*, *mensum erit*, *relata fuerit*), the tense of instructions; the gerund *circumagendo*, "by turning (the compass) round", which has no expressed object; *pansis*, from *pando*, a rarer participle form than *passis*; and the impersonal *mensum erit*, "it will have been measured", which Italian and English both turn into "if you measure".

A note on the text: The Latin Library has three misprints here: *ReIiqua* (with a capital I) for *Reliqua*, *vertices* for *verticem* in the first sentence, and *conlocantum*, which is not a word, for *conlocatum*. All three are corrected, as confirmed by Krohn's edition on LacusCurtius. The angle brackets around *a medio pectore* are the editor's: the words are restored to fill a gap in the manuscripts.

### Excerpt 3: De Architectura IX, Praefatio 9-10 -- Eureka!

**Source:** Vitruvius, *De Architectura*, Book IX, preface, sections 9-10, from *LacusCurtius* (penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Vitruvius/9*.html), which reproduces F. Krohn's Teubner edition (1912). The Latin Library's text of this passage is unusually corrupt (it prints *Nimium* for *nimirum* and *verius* for *versus*, and omits Archimedes' Greek words altogether), so LacusCurtius is used instead. Sentence-initial capitals have been added, since Krohn prints the text in lower case throughout.

**Context:** Each book of the *De Architectura* has a preface, and Vitruvius uses them for his best stories. Book IX, on astronomy and sundials, opens with a reflection on how athletes are showered with honours while the thinkers who truly benefit humanity are forgotten. To prove the point, he tells stories about great thinkers. The first two are about Plato and Pythagoras; the third is this one, the oldest surviving version of the most famous story in the history of science.

**Latin Text:**

[9] Archimedis vero cum multa miranda inventa et varia fuerint, ex omnibus etiam infinita sollertia id quod exponam videtur esse expressum. Nimirum Hiero Syracusis auctus regia potestate, rebus bene gestis cum auream coronam votivam diis inmortalibus in quodam fano constituisset ponendam, manupretio locavit faciendam et aurum ad sacoma adpendit redemptori. Is ad tempus opus manu factum subtiliter regi adprobavit et ad sacoma pondus coronae visus est praestitisse.

[10] Posteaquam indicium est factum dempto auro tantundem argenti in id coronarium opus admixtum esse, indignatus Hiero se contemptum esse neque inveniens qua ratione id furtum deprehenderet, rogavit Archimeden uti insumeret sibi de eo cogitationem. Tunc is cum haberet eius rei curam, casu venit in balineum ibique cum in solium descenderet, animadvertit quantum corporis sui in eo insideret tantum aquae extra solium effluere. Idque cum eius rei rationem explicationis ostendisset, non est moratus sed exsiluit gaudio motus de solio et nudus vadens domum versus significabat clara voce invenisse quod quaereret. Nam currens identidem graece clamabat ευρηκα ευρηκα.

**English Translation:**

[9] Of the many and varied marvellous discoveries of Archimedes, the one I am about to describe seems to show, above all the others, an ingenuity without limits. Hiero, you see, having risen to royal power in Syracuse, decided after his successes to place a golden crown in a certain temple as a votive offering to the immortal gods. He contracted out the work for a fee, and weighed out the gold for the contractor on the scales. The contractor delivered the work on time, finely crafted, to the king's satisfaction, and on the scales the crown seemed to match the weight of the gold.

[10] Afterwards, word came that some of the gold had been taken out and the same weight of silver mixed into the crown. Hiero was furious at having been cheated, but since he could find no way to prove the theft, he asked Archimedes to take the problem on. While Archimedes had the matter on his mind, he happened to go to the baths, and as he got down into the tub he noticed that the more of his body sank into it, the more water overflowed from the tub. Since this showed him how the problem could be explained, he did not wait a moment: overjoyed, he leapt out of the tub and ran home naked, announcing at the top of his voice that he had found what he was looking for. For as he ran he kept shouting in Greek: *heureka, heureka*, "I have found it, I have found it!"

**Analysis:**

This is Vitruvius in storytelling mode, and his Latin relaxes accordingly. The construction is mostly the narrative staple of every Latin textbook: *cum* with the subjunctive (*cum... constituisset*, *cum haberet*, *cum... descenderet*, *cum... ostendisset*), the ablative absolute (*rebus bene gestis*, *dempto auro*), indirect statement after verbs of perceiving and saying (*animadvertit... effluere*, *significabat... invenisse*), and an indirect question (*quod quaereret*). A student who has read Caesar will feel at home.

The technical touches are where the care is needed. *Manupretio locavit faciendam* is legal-commercial language: *locare* with a gerundive means "to put out to contract", and *manupretium* is the price of the labour, so Hiero hired a goldsmith for a fee. *Ad sacoma* is a Greek loan-word for the counterweight on a scale. *Redemptor* is not a "redeemer" but the contractor who has undertaken the job. And the central observation, *quantum corporis sui in eo insideret tantum aquae extra solium effluere*, is a correlative pair, *quantum... tantum*, "as much... so much", which sets out in one line what we now call the displacement of water. Notice that Vitruvius does not mention a law of buoyancy, or anything about the weight of displaced water: that is Archimedes' later work *On Floating Bodies*. What he describes is volume. Gold is denser than silver, so a crown with silver in it takes up more space than the same weight of pure gold, and pushes out more water. The rest of the preface (sections 11-12) describes the experiment, which proves the theft.

And then the punchline: *nudus vadens domum versus*. The greatest mathematician of antiquity running naked through the streets of Syracuse. Vitruvius ends the section on Archimedes' words in Greek, *heureka*, which every modern language has borrowed. For an author who apologised for his writing, it is a perfectly told anecdote.

## Legacy and Impact

The *De Architectura* is the only treatise on architecture to survive from the ancient world. Greek architects wrote many books on their buildings, and Vitruvius quotes them; Romans after him wrote more; every one of them is lost. Everything we know at first hand about how a Roman architect was trained, how he thought about proportion, how he chose his sand or sited his city, comes from this one book. That alone would make him important. What made him decisive was the Renaissance.

The text never quite disappeared: it was copied in Carolingian monasteries, and dozens of medieval manuscripts survive. But it was the humanists who turned it into a sacred text. In 1416 Poggio Bracciolini, the great book-hunter of the age, found a copy at the monastery of St Gall and helped bring it back into circulation. From then on, anyone in Italy who wanted to build *all'antica*, in the manner of the ancients, read Vitruvius. When humanist princes and popes rebuilt their cities and Renaissance architects designed their churches, palaces and squares, Vitruvius was the manual open on the table, and the ruins of Rome were the examples they measured him against. Brunelleschi studied the ruins; Alberti studied Vitruvius; Bramante, Raphael and Michelangelo all worked in a world shaped by him. In the 1540s a group of humanists in Rome even founded an *Accademia della Virtù*, nicknamed the Vitruvian Academy, with the aim of producing a complete edition and commentary of the text.

His influence was just as great on the books that came after him, because every major architectural treatise of the Renaissance was built on Vitruvius, whether it followed him, corrected him or argued with him. Leon Battista Alberti's *De re aedificatoria* (1452), the first great architectural treatise of the modern age, is written in Latin, deliberately divided into ten books like Vitruvius's, and built on his ideas from start to finish, even where it corrects him. It was followed by the first printed edition of Vitruvius (Rome, c. 1486), the first illustrated edition by Fra Giocondo (Venice, 1511), the first Italian translation by Cesare Cesariano (Como, 1521), and the great annotated edition of Daniele Barbaro (Venice, 1556), with illustrations by Andrea Palladio. Then came the manuals that taught architecture to the whole of Europe: Sebastiano Serlio's books on architecture, Vignola's *Regola delli cinque ordini* (1562), and Palladio's *I quattro libri dell'architettura* (1570), whose villas and churches were copied from England to Virginia. The five classical orders of columns, the idea that a building should be proportioned like a human body, and the triad of *firmitas, utilitas, venustas* all reached the modern world through these books, and all of them go back to Vitruvius. When Thomas Jefferson designed his house at Monticello and the University of Virginia, he worked from Palladio, which means that, at second hand, he was working from a retired Roman artillery engineer with a pension from Octavia.

And then, of course, there is Leonardo's *Vitruvian Man*, which made Vitruvius's name famous even among people who have never opened his book.

## Final Rating

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

Vitruvius is prose, and in the Augustan Era that already puts him on the easier side. His grammar is standard, his stories are told in plain narrative Latin, and he uses almost no rhetorical figures or literary allusion. What lifts him above D is his lexicon: a dense, technical vocabulary full of Greek loan-words, many of them missing from school dictionaries, together with a loose and sometimes awkward sentence structure that makes his descriptions harder to follow than to parse, especially without the lost diagrams. The narrative prefaces, like the Archimedes story, sit close to D; the technical chapters on temples or machines can touch B. On balance, a solid C: approachable for any student who has read Caesar and Cicero, provided they keep a good dictionary and a lot of patience within reach.

# GAIUS CORNELIUS GALLUS (70/69 -- 26 BC)

## Biography

Gaius Cornelius Gallus was the most dangerous kind of man to be in Augustan Rome: brilliant at two things at once. He invented a whole genre of Latin poetry, and he governed Egypt. The second of these killed him, and in killing him it took most of the first one with it.

**A provincial from Fréjus.** He was born in 70 or 69 BC at Forum Iulii, modern Fréjus on the coast of Provence, in the province of Gallia Narbonensis, probably into a family of no particular distinction; his name may even suggest Gallic origins. He was almost exactly the same age as Virgil, and like Virgil he came to Rome from the provinces with nothing but talent. The first time he appears in history is in a letter of 43 BC from Asinius Pollio to Cicero, where he is mentioned in passing as "my friend Cornelius Gallus". Within a few years he was one of the most admired young poets in the city.

**Virgil's friend.** Gallus and Virgil were close friends, and the friendship is written into the *Eclogues*. According to the ancient commentators, when Virgil's family farm near Mantua was confiscated for veterans after Philippi, Gallus was one of the influential men who helped him. Virgil paid him back in the only currency a poet has. In *Eclogue* 6 he imagines Gallus being led up Mount Helicon by one of the Muses, where the whole chorus of Apollo rises to greet him and he is handed the pipes of Hesiod: a solemn consecration as a poet. And the whole of *Eclogue* 10, the last poem of the book, is dedicated to him: *pauca meo Gallo*, "a few verses for my Gallus". In it Gallus is lying under a rock in Arcadia, dying of love, while shepherds, gods and even the sheep gather round to ask what is wrong. It is Gallus who speaks the most famous line of the poem, *omnia vincit Amor: et nos cedamus Amori*, "Love conquers all: let us too give in to Love". Ancient commentators believed that some of the lines Virgil gives him there were adapted from Gallus's own poems, which would make *Eclogue* 10 the best surviving witness to what they sounded like.

**Lycoris.** The woman Gallus suffers for in *Eclogue* 10, and the heroine of his four books of love elegies, is called Lycoris. Her real name, according to the commentators, was Volumnia Cytheris, and she was the most famous actress in Rome: a freedwoman who performed in mime, the scandalous, semi-improvised popular theatre of the day, and who had previously been the mistress of Mark Antony, with whom she toured Italy in a litter, to the horror of respectable society. Cicero once found himself at a dinner party next to her and wrote to a friend, plainly embarrassed, that he had had no idea she would be there. In *Eclogue* 10 Lycoris has left Gallus to follow a soldier to the snows of the Alps and the Rhine. The pattern of Roman love elegy, the poet devoted to a beautiful, unfaithful woman under a Greek pseudonym, begins here.

**The general.** Unlike almost every other Roman poet, Gallus was also a successful soldier. In the final campaign against Antony and Cleopatra in 30 BC he commanded Octavian's legions in the west of Egypt, captured the town of Paraetonium and drove back Antony's attack on it. He then played a part in the capture of Cleopatra: according to Plutarch, Gallus kept the queen talking at the barred door of her mausoleum while his colleague Proculeius climbed in through a window behind her. Octavian rewarded him with the greatest prize available to a man of his rank: in 30 BC Gallus became the first Roman prefect of Egypt.

**Too much glory.** Egypt was not an ordinary province. It was the richest territory in the empire and the granary of Rome, and Augustus governed it as his personal possession, through a man of equestrian rank, not a senator, so that nobody powerful enough to rebel could ever control it. Gallus did his job extremely well. He crushed a revolt in Upper Egypt, took five cities in fifteen days, pushed beyond the First Cataract of the Nile, and received ambassadors from the king of Ethiopia. Then he made the fatal mistake of telling everybody. In April 29 BC he set up at Philae a stone inscription in three languages (Latin, Greek and Egyptian hieroglyphs) in which he boasted that he had led an army "where neither the Roman people nor the kings of Egypt had ever carried arms", and mentioned Octavian only briefly. According to Cassius Dio, he also put up statues of himself all over Egypt, had his achievements carved on the pyramids, and, worst of all, said disrespectful things about Augustus, probably after a few drinks.

**The fall.** In 27 or 26 BC he was recalled and denounced by a former friend. Augustus formally renounced his friendship (*renuntiatio amicitiae*) and banned him from his house and his provinces, which in Augustan Rome was the end of a career and very nearly the end of a life. The Senate, eager to show its loyalty, piled on: it voted to have him tried, exiled, and his property handed over to Augustus. Gallus did not wait for the verdict. He killed himself in 26 BC, at the age of forty-three. Suetonius says that Augustus wept when he heard the news, and complained that he alone was not allowed to be angry with his friends only as much as he wanted. Ovid, who knew something about displeasing Augustus, later summed up the case in two lines: it was not writing about Lycoris that ruined Gallus, but not holding his tongue when he had had too much wine.

**Erased.** What followed is often called a *damnatio memoriae*, and although the term is modern and there is no record of a formal decree under that name, the effect was very much the same: Gallus was removed from the record. The Philae inscription was smashed and its pieces used as building material for a later temple, where archaeologists found it in 1896. In Alexandria he had built a new forum for Octavian and dedicated an obelisk there in his own name; his inscription was removed and replaced. That obelisk was later brought to Rome by Caligula and now stands in the middle of St Peter's Square, and in the 1960s the archaeologist Filippo Magi realised that the rows of small holes on its base were the fixing holes of bronze letters from the original dedication. When he joined the dots, they spelled out the name of Gallus. Every pilgrim who has ever crossed St Peter's Square has walked past the one monument in Rome that still, invisibly, carries his name. The fourth-century commentator Servius adds a more famous and more doubtful story: that the fourth book of Virgil's *Georgics* originally ended with a long praise of Gallus, and that after his fall Augustus ordered Virgil to replace it, which is how we got the story of Aristaeus and Orpheus instead. Most modern scholars think this is a legend, but it says a great deal that ancient readers found it believable.

His poetry did not vanish immediately. Ovid could still promise that "Gallus will be known in the West, Gallus in the East, and his Lycoris with him", and Quintilian read and ranked him at the end of the first century AD. But no manuscript of his four books survived the end of antiquity. Until 1978, the founder of Latin love elegy was represented by exactly one line.

## Most Important Works

- **Amores** (*Love Poems*, probably in the 40s and 30s BC) -- four books of love elegies addressed to Lycoris, the work that founded Roman love elegy as a genre. Before 1978 only one pentameter survived, quoted by a late geographer for its description of a river: *uno tellures dividit amne duas*, "with a single stream it divides two lands". In 1978 a papyrus found at Qasr Ibrim in Egyptian Nubia gave us about ten more lines (see the excerpt below). [Fragmentary]
- **Translations or adaptations of Euphorion** -- Virgil's *Eclogues* hint that Gallus adapted the obscure, learned Greek poet Euphorion of Chalcis, the kind of Alexandrian model the neoterics loved, possibly including a poem on the origin of the Grynean grove of Apollo. [Lost]
- **The Philae inscription** (29 BC) -- not literature, but the only surviving text certainly written in Gallus's name: his official account of his victories in Egypt, in Latin and Greek, with an Egyptian version in hieroglyphs. It is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. [Survives, damaged]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

With about ten lines of verse, there is very little to say about Gallus's style from his own words, and anything we say rests largely on what others said about him. Quintilian, ranking the Latin elegists, called Tibullus the most polished and elegant, said some preferred Propertius, found Ovid too playful, and described Gallus as *durior*, "rather harsh". That fits what the papyrus shows. His couplets are self-contained, each making a single point and stopping. He ends a pentameter on a five-syllable word, *historiae*, which Ovid, a generation later, would almost never do. And his spelling is old-fashioned: *quom* for *cum*, *spolieis* and *deiuitiora* with *ei* for long *i*, *Kato* with a K. These may be the habits of the copyist rather than of the poet, but they give the lines an archaic look that fits an author writing at the very beginning of the Augustan age, closer to Catullus than to Ovid.

The papyrus also confirms, at least in miniature, what the ancients said of his range: in nine lines he moves from his mistress, to Caesar's glory, to the Muses, to the literary critics whose judgement he fears. Love, politics and poetry in one breath. That combination, the love poet who also writes for the regime and keeps one eye on his critics, is exactly the tension that runs through the whole of Augustan elegy.

## Latin Excerpt

### Excerpt: Gallus fr. 2 (the Qasr Ibrim papyrus)

**Source:** Gallus, fragment 2 Courtney, from *Living Poets*, Durham University (livingpoets.awh.durham.ac.uk/w/index.php/Gallus_fr._2), following the text first published by R. D. Anderson, P. J. Parsons and R. G. M. Nisbet in the *Journal of Roman Studies* (1979). The Latin Library and Splash Latino do not carry Gallus. Square brackets mark letters lost on the papyrus and restored by editors; dots mark letters that are lost or illegible.

**Context:** In 1978 an expedition of the Egypt Exploration Society was excavating Qasr Ibrim, a fortress on a cliff above the Nile in Nubia, far to the south of Egypt, where a Roman garrison was stationed around 25-21 BC. In a heap of rubbish from the Roman occupation they found a scrap of papyrus with Latin elegiac couplets written on it, with the name *Lycoris* in the first surviving line. Since Lycoris was Gallus's mistress, and since no other poet is known to have used the name, the lines were identified as his. The papyrus was probably copied within a few decades of the poems being written, possibly in the poet's own lifetime, which makes it the oldest surviving manuscript of Latin poetry. That Gallus's poems should reappear in Egypt, the province that ruined him, carried there perhaps by one of the soldiers who served under his successor, is an irony he would have appreciated.

**Latin Text:**

> ] tristia nequit[ia fact]a, Lycori, tua.
>
> fata mihi, Caesar, tum erunt mea dulcia quom tu
> maxima Romanae pars eris historiae,
> postque tuum reditum multorum templa deorum
> fixa legam spolieis deiuitiora tueis.
>
> . . . . . ] . . . . . tandem fecerunt c[ar]mina Musae
> quae possem domina deicere digna mea.
> . . . . . . . . . . . ] . atur idem tibi, non ego, Visce,
> . . ] . . . . . . . . l . Kato, iudice te uereor.

**English Translation:**

> ... made sad, Lycoris, by your wickedness.
>
> My fate will be sweet to me, Caesar, at the moment when you
> become the greatest part of Roman history,
> and when, after your return, I read that the temples of many gods
> are the richer for your spoils hung up in them.
>
> ... at last the Muses have made poems
> that I could recite, worthy of my mistress.
> ... the same to you, I do not, Viscus,
> ... Cato, with you as my judge, fear.

**Analysis:**

These nine lines, some of them broken, are almost everything we have of the man who invented Latin love elegy, so every word has been argued over. The first line, of which only the end survives, is a pentameter addressed to Lycoris, accusing her of *nequitia*, a word that means anything from naughtiness to sheer wickedness and will become a key word of later elegy, where the lover's *nequitia* and the mistress's are both on display. That single half-line already contains the whole elegiac situation: the poet, the woman, and the hurt.

The next four lines are a complete epigram of a completely different kind: a political compliment. "My fate will be sweet to me, Caesar, when you become the greatest part of Roman history." Which Caesar is meant is debated. Many scholars think it is Julius Caesar, about to leave for his planned war against Parthia in 44 BC, which would date the poem before the Ides of March; others think it is Octavian. Either way, the lines show that the founder of love elegy was also, already, writing praise of the ruler, the same mix that Propertius and Ovid would handle so uneasily. The grammar is clean: a future *erunt* answered by *quom tu... eris* (with *quom*, the old spelling of *cum*), then *legam*, "I shall read", governing an indirect statement with *esse* understood, *templa... deiuitiora*, "that the temples are richer". *Fixa... spolieis... tueis* refers to the Roman custom of nailing captured arms and trophies to the walls of temples.

The last four lines are literary: at last the Muses have produced poems "worthy of my mistress", and the poet wonders how they will be received by two critics, Viscus and Cato. Viscus is probably one of the Visci brothers whom Horace lists among the readers whose approval he wants; Cato is almost certainly Valerius Cato, the grammarian and poet who was the great authority of the neoteric generation. So in nine lines Gallus places himself exactly: between his mistress, the most powerful man in Rome, and the literary establishment.

Formally, each couplet stands alone, and the pentameters end on a two-syllable word (*tua*, *mea*) or, unusually, on a longer one (*historiae*, *uereor*). Later elegists, above all Ovid, made the two-syllable ending almost compulsory, so the papyrus gives us a glimpse of the genre before its rules were fixed. Notice also the old-fashioned spellings, *spolieis*, *deiuitiora*, *deicere* (for *dicere*), *quom*, *Kato*, and the use of *u* for consonantal *v* (*deiuitiora*, *uereor*), which is how the Romans themselves wrote it.

A word of caution: a minority of scholars have doubted that these lines are by Gallus at all, or have found them disappointingly flat for such a famous poet. Most accept them. Whether they are his best lines is another question, and an unfair one: they are simply the lines that happened to end up on a rubbish heap in Nubia.

---

## Legacy and Impact

Gallus is the clearest case in the whole project of an author whose importance has nothing to do with how much of him survives. He is the founder of Latin love elegy as a genre. Catullus had written love poems in elegiac couplets, but Gallus seems to have been the first to make an entire book, and then four, out of elegies about a single mistress under a Greek pseudonym, and in doing so he fixed the shape of the genre that Tibullus, Propertius and Ovid would bring to its peak. The ancients were in no doubt about this. When Ovid lists the line of Roman elegists, Gallus comes first; when Quintilian claims that in elegy "we challenge the Greeks", Gallus is one of the four names he gives; and Virgil's *Eclogue* 10 turns him into the very image of the elegiac lover, suffering in Arcadia, defeated by Love.

Many of the conventions of Roman elegy probably go back to him: the poet as the slave of his mistress (*servitium amoris*), love as a kind of military service (*militia amoris*), the beloved under a pseudonym that hides a real woman, the poet who refuses war and career in favour of love and then writes about Caesar's victories anyway. We cannot prove most of this from his own lines, because the lines are gone. But the genre he started is one of the great Roman contributions to European literature, and it runs from the Augustan elegists to the troubadours, Petrarch, and every love poet who has ever complained in verse about a cruel beloved.

His other legacy is a warning. Gallus was the first great writer of the Augustan age to discover that the regime gave a great deal and could take it all back. His fall in 26 BC came only a year after the "restored Republic" of 27 BC, and it showed everyone, poets included, where the limits were. Ovid would learn the same lesson thirty-four years later.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: NC (Not Comparable)**

About ten lines of verse, several of them broken, cannot support a reliable rating. What survives is not especially hard: the syntax of the papyrus is clear, the vocabulary standard, and the main obstacles are the archaic spelling, the gaps in the text and the elegiac metre itself. But nine lines cannot tell us how Gallus handled the long, dense, allusive elegies his contemporaries admired, and Quintilian's *durior* suggests that he was not an easy read. So NC, as with Livius Andronicus. What NC does not measure is importance, and here Gallus ranks with anyone in the era: without him there would be no Tibullus, no Propertius, and no Ovid as we know them.

# PUBLIUS VERGILIUS MARO -- VIRGIL (70 -- 19 BC)

## Biography

Virgil is the most famous Latin poet who ever lived, and possibly the most famous Latin author of any kind. He is also, by every ancient account, one of the shyest, most awkward and most anxious men ever to become a celebrity. Most of what we know about his life comes from a biography by Suetonius that survives in a version by the fourth-century grammarian Donatus, and from the commentary of Servius. Some of it is legend. All of it is worth telling. And set him beside his friend Horace, and the difference between them is clear at once: Virgil is always aiming at the stars and beyond, at fate, empire, the gods and the meaning of history, while Horace is content to remind us of our humanity. Both are reasons to enjoy them fully.

**A laurel in a ditch.** Publius Vergilius Maro was born on 15 October 70 BC in the village of Andes, near Mantua, in what was then Cisalpine Gaul. His father was a man of modest origins: according to Donatus, either a potter or the hired hand of a minor official called Magius, who did well enough to marry his employer's daughter, Magia Polla, and to buy land and bees of his own. Before Virgil was born, his mother dreamed that she gave birth to a laurel branch, which took root and grew at once into a fully grown tree covered in fruit and flowers. The next day she gave birth in a ditch by the side of the road, on the way to the country. The baby, we are told, did not cry, and had such a gentle expression that everyone took it as a sign of good fortune. A poplar planted on the spot grew so fast that local women started praying to it. Every great Roman got a few omens; Virgil got a botanical garden.

**The maiden.** He was educated at Cremona and Milan, then in Rome, where he studied rhetoric to become a lawyer. It did not go well. Donatus says he pleaded exactly one case in court, and spoke so slowly that he seemed almost uneducated. He then turned to medicine and, above all, mathematics, before ending up at Naples in the Epicurean school of Siro. He was tall, dark-skinned, with the face of a countryman, and in poor health all his life: he suffered from his stomach, his throat and headaches, and often coughed up blood. In Naples people called him *Parthenias*, "the maiden", because of the modesty of his life. When he did become famous, on the rare occasions he visited Rome and people recognised him in the street and pointed him out, he would duck into the nearest house to escape them.

**The farm.** In 41 BC, after Philippi, the triumvirs confiscated land all over northern Italy to settle their veterans, and the Virgil family farm was among them. According to the ancient commentators, Virgil was nearly killed by the veteran who took it, and only got it back (if he ever did) thanks to influential friends, among them Asinius Pollio and Cornelius Gallus. The experience went straight into his first book. The opening poem of the *Eclogues* is a dialogue between a shepherd who has kept his land and one who has been thrown off it, and the dispossessed shepherd's question to the new soldier-owner, *impius haec tam culta novalia miles habebit?*, "is some godless soldier going to have these well-tended fields?", is the civil wars seen from the farmyard.

**Maecenas, and a bear licking her cubs.** The *Eclogues* made him famous almost overnight: they were even performed on stage by actors, to wild applause. They also brought him into the circle of Maecenas, who became his patron and friend, and who asked him for a poem on farming. Virgil spent seven years on the four books of the *Georgics*, about 2,200 lines, which works out at less than a line a day. His method, as Donatus describes it, explains why. Every morning he dictated a large number of verses; then he spent the rest of the day reworking them and cutting them down to a very few, saying that he produced his poems the way a she-bear produces her cubs: by licking them into shape. In 29 BC he read the finished *Georgics* to Octavian, just back from Actium, at Atella in Campania. The reading took four days, and whenever Virgil's voice gave out, Maecenas took over.

**The Aeneid, and an emperor who would not wait.** For the last eleven years of his life he worked on the *Aeneid*. He first wrote the whole story out in prose and divided it into twelve books, then turned it into verse piece by piece, in no particular order, as the mood took him. Where he could not yet find the right line, he left a half-line and moved on, calling these unfinished verses "props" (*tibicines*), put in to hold up the building until the real columns were ready. Fifty-eight of those half-lines are still in the poem, and one of them appears in the excerpt below. Augustus could hardly bear the wait. While fighting in Spain in 26-25 BC he wrote to Virgil begging, half jokingly and half threateningly, for "even a first sketch or any section at all" of the poem. Virgil replied that he had nothing worthy to send, and that he had taken on something so enormous that he sometimes thought he must have been out of his mind to start it.

**Octavia faints.** Eventually he gave in, and read three books to Augustus and his family: Books II, IV and VI. Book VI ends with Aeneas in the underworld, where his father Anchises shows him the souls of the great Romans of the future waiting to be born. Last of all comes a young man of extraordinary promise, surrounded by mourning, and Anchises, weeping, reveals who he is: *tu Marcellus eris*, "you will be Marcellus". This was Augustus's nephew, son-in-law and intended heir, who had died in 23 BC at the age of nineteen, and his mother, Augustus's sister Octavia, was in the room. Donatus says that when Virgil reached those lines she fainted, and was revived only with difficulty; later tradition adds that Augustus and Octavia both wept, and that Octavia rewarded the poet with ten thousand sesterces for every line. It is the best evidence we have of what Virgil's poetry could do to an audience. These were not readers studying a set text. They were a grieving family hearing their dead boy turned into the last and saddest of Rome's heroes, in lines so beautiful that they could not bear them.

**Best or bust.** Virgil was, in the most literal sense, a perfectionist: he would rather have no poem than an imperfect one. In 19 BC, with the *Aeneid* complete in draft, he set out for Greece and Asia, planning to spend three more years revising it and then devote the rest of his life to philosophy. In Athens he met Augustus, who persuaded him to come home. On a visit to Megara in the burning sun he fell ill; the illness worsened on the voyage, and he died at Brundisium on 21 September 19 BC, a few weeks short of his fifty-first birthday. Before leaving Italy he had asked his friend Varius to burn the *Aeneid* if anything happened to him, and on his deathbed he called for the manuscript boxes so that he could burn it himself. Nobody brought them. In his will he left the manuscript to Varius and Plotius Tucca, on condition that they publish nothing he had not already published. Augustus overruled the will, and ordered them to publish the poem exactly as it was, adding nothing and correcting only what was obviously wrong: which is why the half-lines are still there. We have the most important poem in Latin literature because the emperor refused to respect a dead man's wishes. It is very nearly a matter of luck.

**Fame, fortune and thieves.** He died rich, with a fortune of almost ten million sesterces from the generosity of his friends, and a house on the Esquiline next to the gardens of Maecenas, which he hardly used. He was buried at Naples, under an epitaph that sums up his whole career in two lines: *Mantua me genuit, Calabri rapuere, tenet nunc / Parthenope; cecini pascua, rura, duces*, "Mantua bore me, Calabria took me, now Naples holds me; I sang of pastures, fields and leaders". He also had his critics, who accused him of stealing whole lines from Homer. His answer, according to Donatus, was the best literary comeback in antiquity: "Why don't they try the same thefts themselves? They will find it is easier to steal the club from Hercules than a line from Homer."

## Most Important Works

- **Bucolica** or **Eclogae** (*Eclogues*, c. 42-38 BC) -- ten short pastoral poems, modelled on the Greek idylls of Theocritus, set in an imaginary countryside of singing shepherds that later centuries would call Arcadia. The themes alternate between love (Eclogues 2, 8 and 10, the last one for Gallus), singing contests and poetry (3, 5, 7), the land confiscations and the intrusion of history into the countryside (1 and 9), and grand visions of the cosmos and the future (4 and 6). The fourth, which announces the birth of a child who will bring back the Golden Age, was later read by Christians as a prophecy of Christ. [Complete]
- **Georgica** (*Georgics*, c. 37-29 BC) -- a didactic poem on farming in four books, dedicated to Maecenas. Book I is about crops and the weather, Book II about trees and vines (with the famous praise of Italy), Book III about cattle, horses and sheep (ending in a terrible plague), Book IV about bees. On the surface it is a manual; underneath, it is a poem about work (*labor omnia vicit / improbus*, "relentless work conquered everything"), about Italy, and about how order can be rebuilt after the chaos of civil war. It ends with the myth of Aristaeus, who loses his bees and gets them back, and of Orpheus, who loses Eurydice and does not. [Complete]
- **Aeneis** (*Aeneid*, c. 29-19 BC) -- the national epic of Rome, in twelve books, unfinished at the poet's death. The first six books, the wanderings of Aeneas from burning Troy to Italy, are Virgil's *Odyssey*; the last six, the war in Latium, are his *Iliad*. Its hero is defined not by anger, like Achilles, or cunning, like Ulysses, but by *pietas*, duty to the gods, to his family and to his mission. Its great theme is the price of that mission: Rome is founded, but at the cost of Troy, of Dido, of Turnus and of countless young men, and the poem ends abruptly, with Aeneas killing Turnus in a moment of rage. It is a celebration of Augustan Rome and, at the same time, one of the saddest poems ever written about what empires cost. [Complete, but unrevised]
- **Appendix Vergiliana** -- a collection of minor poems attributed to Virgil in antiquity (the *Culex*, the *Ciris*, the *Moretum* and others), almost all of them written by other people and later attached to his name, which is what happens when you become the most famous poet in the world. [Mostly spurious]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Virgil is the point at which Latin poetry reaches stylistic perfection, and he made the dactylic hexameter what it would be for the rest of antiquity. Ennius had brought the hexameter into Latin two centuries earlier, and Lucretius and Catullus had refined it; but it was Virgil who fixed its rules, its rhythms, its pauses and its endings, so completely that after him every Latin epic poet wrote Virgil's hexameter, and every poet who wrote in hexameters in the genres he touched, pastoral and didactic as well as epic, wrote in his shadow. Every line is weighed. The sound imitates the sense: slow, heavy spondees for effort, grief or great size, quick dactyls for speed. Words are placed for emphasis at the start of the line or at its main pause. And there is not a word too many. For a translator, this perfection is precisely the problem.

The difficulties come in layers. The first is **poetic word order**. Adjectives and nouns are separated across the line, often framing it, and the most famous device of all is the transferred epithet (*hypallage*), where an adjective is attached grammatically to one noun while it really describes another, for the sake of the meaning and of the metre. The seventh line of the *Aeneid* is the classic example: *altae moenia Romae*, literally "the walls of high Rome", where we would expect "the high walls of Rome". Then come **poetic plurals** (*colla* for a single neck, *silentia* for silence), Greek forms and case endings for Greek names, archaisms (*olli* for *illi*, *aulai* for *aulae*), and **elision**, where a final vowel or *-m* before another vowel disappears in pronunciation, sometimes several times in one line, so that words have to be mentally pulled apart before they can be read. **Lexicon: MEDIUM to HIGH; Syntactic complexity: HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH.**

The second layer is **allusion**. Virgil writes with the whole of Greek and Latin literature open on the desk: Homer above all, but also Hesiod, Theocritus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Ennius, Lucretius and Catullus. Myths are referred to, not told; a single epithet can stand for a whole story. And compared with Horace, the Latin is honest: there is none of Horace's wilful game-playing with word order. But the density of everything at once makes Virgil hard all the time. **Allusion density: HIGH.**

Difficulty also varies a great deal across his works, and here a widespread school habit is simply wrong. Students are very often started on the *Aeneid*, as if Virgil's most famous poem were the natural way in. It is not. The **Eclogues** are the right place to begin: they are short, the vocabulary is limited, the situations are simple, and a student can translate a whole poem from beginning to end and enjoy the rare satisfaction of having actually finished a work of Virgil. They range from B to low A. The **Aeneid** is a step up: the narrative helps, but the speeches, the similes and the sheer density of the verse put it in the middle to upper range of A. The **Georgics** are the hardest of the three, and probably the hardest Latin poetry of the whole era outside Horace's lyrics: technical farming vocabulary, rare plant and animal names, Greek words, mythological allusions and a compressed didactic syntax all at once. At their most difficult they push into S territory.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Bucolica (Eclogue) 2.6-18 -- Corydon's Complaint

**Source:** Virgil, *Bucolica*, Eclogue 2, lines 6-18, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vergil/ec2.shtml)

**Context:** Eclogue 2 is a love complaint. The shepherd Corydon is in love with Alexis, a beautiful young slave who is his master's favourite, and who does not care for him at all. The first five lines, spoken by the narrator, tell us that Corydon keeps coming alone to a grove of beech trees to pour out his "unpolished" song to the mountains and the woods, in vain. Then Corydon begins to speak, and these are the first thirteen lines of his monologue. Virgil's model is the eleventh *Idyll* of Theocritus, in which the Cyclops Polyphemus sings to the sea nymph Galatea, who will not have him either. Keep that Cyclops in mind: he comes back in Excerpt 3. (The ancient biographers, inevitably, identified Alexis with a real slave boy given to Virgil by Pollio; there is no way of knowing, and in Greek pastoral the love of a shepherd for a boy was a standard literary situation.)

**Latin Text:**

> 'O crudelis Alexi, nihil mea carmina curas?
> nil nostri miserere? mori me denique cogis?
> nunc etiam pecudes umbras et frigora captant,
> nunc virides etiam occultant spineta lacertos,
> Thestylis et rapido fessis messoribus aestu               10
> alia serpyllumque herbas contundit olentis.
> at mecum raucis, tua dum vestigia lustro,
> sole sub ardenti resonant arbusta cicadis.
> nonne fuit satius tristis Amaryllidos iras
> atque superba pati fastidia? nonne Menalcan,               15
> quamvis ille niger, quamvis tu candidus esses?
> o formose puer, nimium ne crede colori;
> alba ligustra cadunt, vaccinia nigra leguntur.

**English Translation:**

"O cruel Alexis, do you care nothing for my songs?
Have you no pity for me? Will you drive me to my death at last?
Now even the flocks are looking for shade and cool air,
now even the thornbushes hide the green lizards,
and Thestylis, for the reapers worn out by the scorching heat,
is pounding garlic and thyme, the fragrant herbs.
But with me, as I follow your footsteps,
under the blazing sun, the orchards ring with hoarse cicadas.
Would it not have been better to put up with Amaryllis's
bitter rages and her proud disdain? Or with Menalcas,
however dark he was, however fair you are?
O beautiful boy, do not trust your colouring too much:
the white privet flowers fall, the dark bilberries are picked."

**Analysis:**

This is the kind of passage that makes the Eclogues the right place to start Virgil. The situation is simple, the sentences are short, and almost every line is a complete thought. The difficulties are real but manageable, and they are exactly the ones you will meet everywhere else in Virgil.

Start with the traps. *Nil nostri miserere*: *misereor* takes the genitive, and *nostri* is a poetic plural for *mei*, "of me". *Alia* in line 11 is not "other things" but the neuter plural of *alium*, "garlic", one of the classic false friends of Latin poetry; *olentis* is the old accusative plural for *olentes*, agreeing with *herbas*, and *serpyllum* is a Greek plant name, wild thyme. *Satius* is the comparative of *satis*, "better", and *quamvis... esses* is a concessive subjunctive. Line 15 ends on *Menalcan*, a Greek accusative. And *vaccinia* in the last line has been argued over for centuries: it may be bilberries or a kind of dark hyacinth; either way, it is the dark flower or fruit that people pick, against the white flower that is left to fall.

Then look at how the lines are built. *raucis... cicadis* frames lines 12 and 13 together, the hoarse cicadas surrounding the whole scene with their noise, while Corydon wanders in the noonday heat, the one time of day when every sensible creature, even the lizards, is in the shade. The repeated *nunc etiam... nunc... etiam* stresses that the whole world is resting except him. The last line is a perfect chiasmus: *alba ligustra cadunt, vaccinia nigra leguntur*, adjective-noun-verb, noun-adjective-verb, white against black, falling against being chosen. It is a proverb in miniature, telling a beautiful boy that beauty fades and fair skin is no guarantee of being wanted, and it is the first line of Virgil that many readers learn by heart.

Finally, notice the tone. Corydon is pathetic and a little ridiculous: he is literally out in the midday sun, he lists the people he could have had instead, and in the very next lines (not in the excerpt) he starts boasting about how many sheep he owns. Virgil lets us smile at him and feel for him at the same time, which is the emotional key to all of Virgil.

### Excerpt 2: Georgica IV.1-24 -- A Home for the Bees

**Source:** Virgil, *Georgica*, Book IV, lines 1-24, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vergil/geo4.shtml). The dashes around *nam pabula venti / ferre domum prohibent* in the source have been printed as parentheses.

**Context:** The opening of the last book of the *Georgics*. After crops, trees and livestock, Virgil turns to bees, and announces his subject to Maecenas in the first seven lines with a mixture of solemnity and irony: he is going to write about the bees as if they were a nation, with leaders, customs and wars. Then, with the word *Principio*, "first of all", the farming manual begins, and the first practical problem is where to put the hives. This is the book that ends with Aristaeus and Orpheus, and that Servius believed originally ended with the praise of Gallus.

**Latin Text:**

> Protinus aerii mellis caelestia dona
> exsequar: hanc etiam, Maecenas, adspice partem.
> Admiranda tibi levium spectacula rerum
> magnanimosque duces totiusque ordine gentis
> mores et studia et populos et proelia dicam.               5
> In tenui labor; at tenuis non gloria, si quem
> numina laeva sinunt auditque vocatus Apollo.
> Principio sedes apibus statioque petenda,
> quo neque sit ventis aditus (nam pabula venti
> ferre domum prohibent) neque oves haedique petulci               10
> floribus insultent aut errans bucula campo
> decutiat rorem et surgentes atterat herbas.
> Absint et picti squalentia terga lacerti
> pinguibus a stabulis meropesque aliaeque volucres
> et manibus Procne pectus signata cruentis;               15
> omnia nam late vastant ipsasque volantes
> ore ferunt dulcem nidis immitibus escam.
> At liquidi fontes et stagna virentia musco
> adsint et tenuis fugiens per gramina rivus,
> palmaque vestibulum aut ingens oleaster inumbret,               20
> ut, cum prima novi ducent examina reges
> vere suo ludetque favis emissa iuventus,
> vicina invitet decedere ripa calori,
> obviaque hospitiis teneat frondentibus arbos.

**English Translation:**

Next I shall go on to the heavenly gift of honey that comes from the air:
look on this part too, Maecenas.
I shall tell you of a marvellous spectacle of tiny things:
great-hearted leaders and, in due order, a whole nation's
customs and pursuits, its peoples and its battles.
The labour is on a slight subject; but not slight the glory, if the unfriendly
powers allow a poet, and Apollo hears when he is called.
First of all, a home and a settled place must be found for the bees,
where the winds can find no way in (for the winds stop them
from carrying their food home), where no sheep and frisky kids
trample on the flowers, and no heifer, straying across the plain,
shakes off the dew and wears down the growing grass.
Keep away from the rich hives the painted lizard with its scaly back,
and the bee-eaters and the other birds,
and Procne, her breast marked by bloodstained hands;
for they lay everything waste far and wide, and snatch the bees
themselves in flight, in their beaks, a sweet morsel for their pitiless nests.
But let there be clear springs nearby, and pools green with moss,
and a thin stream slipping away through the grass,
and let a palm or a great wild olive shade the entrance,
so that, when the new kings lead out the first swarms
in their own springtime, and the young ones, let out of the combs, are at play,
a nearby bank may invite them to escape the heat,
and a tree in their path may hold them in its leafy welcome.

**Analysis:**

The first seven lines are a small manifesto. *In tenui labor; at tenuis non gloria*: "the labour is on something slight, but the glory is not slight". The same adjective is used twice, first for the subject and then, negated, for the fame, a figure called polyptoton, and the line is a Callimachean declaration: small subjects, perfectly treated, can win great glory. At the same time Virgil plays the epic game in miniature: *magnanimos duces*, *populos*, *proelia* are the vocabulary of the *Aeneid*, applied to insects. The Romans thought the leader of the hive was a male, a king, which is why the bees have *reges* in line 21. Throughout Book IV the hive is a little model of the Roman state, hard-working, disciplined, and ready to die for its leader, and the reader is never quite sure how seriously to take the comparison.

From line 8 the manual begins, and with it the Georgics' typical difficulties. The syntax is instruction in the subjunctive: a gerundive of obligation (*petenda*, "must be found"), a relative clause of characteristic (*quo neque sit... neque... insultent... aut... decutiat... atterat*), and a series of jussives, *absint*, *adsint*, *inumbret*, "let them be absent", "let there be", "let it shade". The vocabulary is technical and zoological: *meropes* are bee-eaters, *oleaster* is the wild olive, *bucula* is a young cow, *haedi petulci* are kids that butt and frisk. And then, in the middle of the birdwatching, a myth arrives without warning: *manibus Procne pectus signata cruentis*. Procne killed her son Itys in revenge on her husband and was turned into a swallow, and the red mark on the swallow's breast was said to be the blood from her hands. Virgil does not explain any of this; he expects you to know. Note also the Greek-style accusative of respect, *pectus signata*, "marked as to the breast", and the framing of the line by *manibus... cruentis*, which encloses Procne in her own bloodstained hands.

The last section turns from danger to welcome. Notice how the future tenses (*ducent*, *ludet*) and the purpose clause (*ut... invitet... teneat*) imagine the young swarm's first spring, and how the vocabulary becomes almost human: *iuventus* is "the youth" of a city, *ludet* is play, *hospitiis* is hospitality. *Decedere calori* is a dative after a verb of yielding: "to give way to the heat", that is, to get out of it. The hive becomes a city, with a shaded entrance and a riverside park. This is the Georgics at their best and their hardest: a farming instruction, a lesson in natural history, a myth and a political allegory, all in the same twenty-four lines.

### Excerpt 3: Aeneis III.655-665 -- Polyphemus

**Source:** Virgil, *Aeneis*, Book III, lines 655-665, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/vergil/aen3.shtml)

**Context:** In Book III Aeneas is telling Dido the story of his wanderings. Landing in Sicily, near Etna, the Trojans meet a ragged, starving Greek called Achaemenides, one of Ulysses' men, who was left behind when Ulysses escaped from the cave of the Cyclops. He has been hiding in the woods for three months, and he has just finished begging the Trojans to take him away, or kill him, when the Cyclops himself appears on the mountain. Virgil is rewriting *Odyssey* IX, but from the other side: we see Polyphemus after the blinding.

**Latin Text:**

> Vix ea fatus erat summo cum monte videmus               655
> ipsum inter pecudes vasta se mole moventem
> pastorem Polyphemum et litora nota petentem,
> monstrum horrendum, informe, ingens, cui lumen ademptum.
> trunca manum pinus regit et vestigia firmat;
> lanigerae comitantur oves; ea sola voluptas               660
> solamenque mali.
> postquam altos tetigit fluctus et ad aequora venit,
> luminis effossi fluidum lavit inde cruorem
> dentibus infrendens gemitu, graditurque per aequor
> iam medium, necdum fluctus latera ardua tinxit.                665

**English Translation:**

He had scarcely spoken these words when, high on the mountain, we see
the shepherd Polyphemus himself, moving his vast bulk among his flocks
and making for the shore he knew so well:
a monster, horrible, shapeless, huge, whose sight had been taken from him.
A lopped pine trunk guides his hand and steadies his steps;
his woolly sheep go with him, his only pleasure
and the comfort of his misery.
When he reached the deep water and came to the sea,
he washed away the blood still oozing from his gouged-out eye,
gnashing his teeth and groaning; and he walks through the sea,
right out into the middle, and still the waves have not wet his towering sides.

**Analysis:**

Line 658 is one of the most famous lines in Latin poetry, and it is famous for its sound: *monstrum horrendum, informe, ingens, cui lumen ademptum*. Read it aloud with the elisions, as a Roman would have heard it: *monstr'horrend'inform'ingens*. Three times in a row the final syllable of a word disappears into the next one (this fusion of vowels across words is called *synaloephe*), so that four words collapse into one long, shapeless mass of sound, exactly like the shapeless mass of the giant. The metre does the same: the first four feet of the line are all spondees, long syllable after long syllable, so the line moves as heavily and slowly as Polyphemus himself, lumbering down the mountain. Then the movement changes. After the pile of adjectives, the line ends with the ordinary, quiet cadence of the hexameter, *lumen ademptum*, and a bare, short relative clause: "whose sight had been taken". After the horror, the pity. That is Virgil's emotional method in a single verse: the sound makes us feel the monster's size, and the last words make us feel his loss.

The rest of the passage keeps the pity going. The giant leans on a pine trunk like an old man on a stick; his sheep, his only joy, walk beside him; he washes his wound in the sea, groaning. Notice the shift of tenses: the narration moves between historic presents (*videmus*, *regit*, *comitantur*, *graditur*) and perfects (*tetigit*, *venit*, *lavit*), which keeps the scene vivid and slightly unstable. Notice also the half-line, *solamenque mali.* (661): this is one of Virgil's unfinished "props", left in the poem when he died. Some manuscripts fill it out with *de collo fistula pendet*, "a pipe hangs from his neck", but that is a later addition, and modern editors leave the line as Virgil left it. And, if you have read Excerpt 1, notice the word *informe*. In Eclogue 2, a few lines after our excerpt ends, Corydon, the lovesick shepherd modelled on Theocritus's lovesick Cyclops, insists *nec sum adeo informis*, "I am not so ugly". Here, at last, is the Cyclops himself, and he is *informis* indeed. Between the Eclogues and the Aeneid, Virgil has turned a comic figure of pastoral into a figure of tragedy.

## Legacy and Impact

Virgil's legacy is so vast that it is hard to know where it ends. Within a few years of his death his poems were being taught in Roman schools, and they remained the basic school text of Latin education for the whole of antiquity and beyond. People scratched lines of the *Aeneid* on the walls of Pompeii. Later Romans even used his poems as an oracle, opening the book at random and reading the first line they found as a prophecy of their future, a practice called the *sortes Vergilianae*.

He became, above all, the model of stylistic perfection: the poet against whom every later Latin poet measured himself. That was especially true for epic. Every Roman epic of the Early Imperial Era was written in his shadow and had to find a way to deal with him. Lucan's *Bellum Civile* (or *Pharsalia*) is in many ways an anti-*Aeneid*, a civil-war epic without gods, without a hero and without hope. Statius ends his *Thebaid* by telling his own poem not to rival "the divine *Aeneid*" but to follow it from a distance and always worship its footprints. Silius Italicus, the author of the *Punica*, owned Virgil's tomb at Naples, visited it as if it were a temple, and celebrated Virgil's birthday more devoutly than his own. The hexameter they all wrote was Virgil's hexameter.

His survival into the Middle Ages owes a great deal to an extraordinary stroke of luck. Eclogue 4, written around 40 BC, announces the birth of a child under whom a new Golden Age will begin. Virgil almost certainly meant the child of a Roman aristocrat, probably connected with the political settlement of that year. But to Christian readers, beginning with Lactantius and the emperor Constantine, the poem looked like a prophecy of the birth of Christ, written by a pagan who had somehow been allowed to glimpse the truth. Virgil was promoted from poet to prophet, and his works were copied, read and protected by the Church with a reverence given to almost no other pagan author. Medieval legend went further and turned him into a magician: in Naples it was said that the city stood safe as long as an egg he had hidden in the foundations of the Castel dell'Ovo remained unbroken.

All this made him so famous that his fame eventually outgrew his poetry, and shifted from his works to his figure. The decisive moment was Dante. In the *Divine Comedy*, Dante chooses Virgil as his guide through Hell and Purgatory, greets him as "my master and my author", and even has the poet Statius claim that Virgil's fourth Eclogue made him a Christian: "through you I became a poet, through you a Christian". From then on, for a great many people, Virgil is first of all "Dante's guide", or simply "the man who wrote the *Aeneid*", rather than the author of poems they have actually read. The process has gone all the way to video games: the *Devil May Cry* series has twin brothers called Dante and Vergil. It is a strange fate for the shy man from Mantua who hid in doorways to avoid being recognised: to become so famous that people know his name without knowing why.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: A (Very Difficult)**

Virgil is hard in almost every criterion at once: poetic word order and transferred epithets, poetic plurals, Greek forms, archaisms, elision, technical vocabulary in the *Georgics*, and a density of literary and mythological allusion that rarely lets up. What keeps him from S is his honesty: compared with Horace there is no wilful game-playing with the reader, the narrative of the *Aeneid* carries you forward, and the Eclogues offer a genuinely approachable way in. Taken work by work: the **Eclogues** from B to low A, and the right place to start; the **Aeneid** in the middle to upper range of A; the **Georgics** the hardest of all, pushing into S. Overall, a firm A. Do not start with the *Aeneid*.

# QUINTUS HORATIUS FLACCUS -- HORACE (65 -- 8 BC)

## Biography

If Virgil is the shy genius of the Augustan Age, Horace is its best company. He is the friend who never takes himself too seriously, who will tell you your vices to your face, and then pour you a cup of Falernian to help you drink over them. He laughs at the greedy, the ambitious, the bores and the social climbers, and he laughs just as hard at himself: short, fat, prematurely grey, quick to lose his temper and quick to calm down again, and, by his own description, "a pig from Epicurus's herd". And then, having made you laugh, he writes some of the most beautiful lyric poetry in Latin. He is the great lyric poet of the Augustan Age, and probably the most quotable poet Rome ever produced. Where Virgil is always aiming at the stars and beyond, Horace is content to remind us of our humanity: friends, wine, the turning seasons, the limits of a single life, and how to live well inside them. Both are reasons to enjoy them fully.

**The freedman's son.** Quintus Horatius Flaccus was born on 8 December 65 BC at Venusia, a military colony on the border between Apulia and Lucania in southern Italy. His father was a freedman, a former slave, who worked as a *coactor*, a collector of payments at auctions: a modest job, but a profitable one. He made one decision that changed his son's life: instead of sending the boy to the local school, where the sons of centurions went with their satchels and their writing tablets, he took him to Rome to be educated alongside the children of senators and knights, and went with him to every lesson himself, as a guardian. Horace never forgot it. In one of his satires he writes that if he has any good qualities at all, he owes them to his father, and that he would not swap him for any noble parent in Rome. His schoolmaster in Rome, Orbilius, is remembered in literature for one thing only: Horace calls him *plagosus*, "fond of the whip", the earliest famous school beating in history.

**The general who ran away.** Around 45 BC he went to Athens to finish his education in philosophy, which is where he was when Caesar was murdered. Brutus came to Athens recruiting young Romans for the cause of the Republic, and Horace, the son of a former slave, found himself made a *tribunus militum*, one of the senior officers of a legion: a sign either of his talent or of how desperate Brutus was. At Philippi in 42 BC the republican army was destroyed, and Horace, by his own cheerful admission, ran. In an ode written years later he reminds an old comrade of the day "when I felt that swift flight with you, and left my little shield behind, not very gloriously" (*relicta non bene parmula*), and adds that the god Mercury picked him up in a cloud and carried him out of the battle. The joke is a literary one too: the Greek lyric poets Archilochus, Alcaeus and Anacreon had all boasted of throwing away their shields and running for their lives, so Horace makes his own cowardice a qualification for joining the club.

**Poverty made me a poet.** He came back to Italy under an amnesty to find his father dead and the family land confiscated. He bought himself a job as a clerk in the treasury, and, as he says, "bold poverty drove me to write verses". The verses were good enough to catch the attention of Virgil and Varius, who in about 38 BC introduced him to Maecenas.

**Maecenas.** The first meeting, as Horace describes it, was a disaster of shyness. He stammered out a few words, "for speechless shame stopped me from saying more"; Maecenas answered briefly, as was his habit; Horace went away. Then, nine months later, Maecenas called him back and asked him to join his circle of friends. It was the beginning of one of the great friendships of antiquity. Maecenas gave him financial security and, around 33 BC, a farm in the Sabine hills northeast of Rome, the place Horace loved most in the world, which appears again and again in his poems as the symbol of the quiet, independent life. In return Horace gave him the dedications of his books, his loyalty, and a promise: in one ode he swears that he will not outlive his friend, and that when Maecenas goes, they will go together. He very nearly kept it. Maecenas died in 8 BC, leaving a will that asked Augustus to "remember Horace as you would remember me". Horace died less than two months later, and was buried next to him on the Esquiline.

**The emperor's admirer.** Augustus liked him enormously, and wanted him close. He offered Horace the post of his private secretary, and Horace politely declined, pleading poor health; Augustus, to his credit, took no offence, and went on writing him teasing letters. In one he calls Horace "a most charming little man". In another, after receiving a slim book of his poems, he jokes that Horace seems to be afraid his books will be bigger than he is, since what Horace lacks in height he makes up for in girth. And in a third he complains, half seriously, that Horace never addresses any of his poems to him: "Are you afraid that being seen as my friend will damage your reputation with posterity?" Horace took the hint, and wrote him a long verse letter, the first in his second book of *Epistles*. In 17 BC Augustus chose him to write the official hymn for the Secular Games, the *Carmen Saeculare*: the freedman's son who had fought against Caesar's heir at Philippi had become the poet laureate of his regime.

**The last years.** He spent his last years between Rome and his Sabine farm, writing his last book of odes and his literary letters, and he died on 27 November 8 BC, a few days before his fifty-seventh birthday, leaving Augustus as his heir. He had predicted his own fame at the end of his third book of odes, in one of the proudest boasts in literature: *exegi monumentum aere perennius*, "I have built a monument more lasting than bronze", and *non omnis moriar*, "I shall not wholly die". He was right.

## Most Important Works

- **Sermones** (*Satires*, Book I c. 35 BC, Book II c. 30 BC) -- two books of hexameter "conversations", eighteen poems in all, in the tradition of Lucilius but gentler and more self-ironic. Horace's motto is *ridentem dicere verum*, "to tell the truth while laughing". The targets are the vices of everyday Roman life: greed, ambition, adultery, gluttony, superstition and the restless discontent that makes everyone want someone else's life. Among the most famous: the journey to Brundisium (I.5), the bore who will not let Horace go on the Via Sacra (I.9), and the town mouse and the country mouse (II.6). [Complete]
- **Epodi** or **Iambi** (*Epodes*, c. 30 BC) -- seventeen poems of iambic verse modelled on the Greek poet Archilochus, the founder of poetic invective. They include curses and insults (against the poet Mevius, against the witch Canidia), but also anguished poems on the civil wars, a celebration of the victory at Actium, and the famous praise of country life, *Beatus ille*, which turns out at the very end to have been spoken by a moneylender who promptly goes back to lending money. [Complete]
- **Carmina** (*Odes*, Books I-III 23 BC, Book IV c. 13 BC) -- one hundred and three lyric poems in the metres of the Greek poets Alcaeus and Sappho, Horace's masterpiece and his claim to be the first to bring Greek lyric into Latin. The themes are friendship, love, wine, the seasons, the shortness of life and the need to enjoy the present wisely, moderation (the *aurea mediocritas*), Rome and its moral renewal (the six "Roman Odes" that open Book III), Augustus and Actium (*Nunc est bibendum*, on the death of Cleopatra), and the immortality of poetry. [Complete]
- **Carmen Saeculare** (*Secular Hymn*, 17 BC) -- the official hymn for the Secular Games, the festival that marked the beginning of a new age for Rome, sung by a choir of twenty-seven boys and twenty-seven girls on the Palatine and the Capitol. A prayer to Apollo and Diana for the prosperity of Rome, its families and its harvests. The official record of the games, carved on stone and partly found in 1890, still names him: *carmen composuit Q. Horatius Flaccus*. [Complete]
- **Epistulae** (*Epistles*, Book I c. 20 BC, Book II c. 19-12 BC) -- two books of verse letters to friends, more philosophical and more serene than the *Satires*. Their subject is how to live: independence, contentment, friendship, self-knowledge. They contain some of his most famous phrases: *sapere aude*, "dare to be wise", *nil admirari*, "to be astonished by nothing", and *Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit*, "captive Greece took her savage conqueror captive". [Complete]
- **Ars Poetica** (*The Art of Poetry*, also called the *Epistula ad Pisones*) -- a long verse letter of 476 lines on how to write poetry, especially drama: unity, consistency of character, appropriateness of style, the need to start *in medias res*, to revise for years (*nonumque prematur in annum*, "let it be kept back until the ninth year"), and to aim at both instruction and pleasure. It is the most influential work of literary theory ever written in Latin. [Complete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Virgil is difficult, but he is still readable. Horace is a different matter. Virgil makes you work; Horace plays games with you.

The first game is **hyperbaton**, and in Horace it reaches lengths found nowhere else. Words that belong together grammatically, an adjective and its noun, a preposition and its object, are pulled apart and placed at opposite ends of a line, or of a whole stanza, with other words packed in between. In the *Odes* this is not decoration but structure: every word sits in exactly the place where its sound, its metre and its meaning do the most work. Nietzsche described an ode of Horace as a mosaic of words in which every single word, by its sound, its position and its meaning, radiates its force to right and left and over the whole. For the translator, that mosaic has to be taken apart tile by tile before it can be put back together in Italian or English. Horace himself gave the technique a name in the *Ars Poetica*: the *callida iunctura*, the "clever joining", by which a familiar word, placed in an unexpected combination, becomes new. In practice, for the student, a great deal of *callida iunctura* is simply more hyperbaton.

The second is **vocabulary**. Horace drops Greek words and Greek constructions into Latin without warning, uses rare words and new formations (in the *Ars Poetica* he explicitly defends the poet's right to coin new words, as long as it is done sparingly), and alludes constantly to Greek lyric poems the reader is expected to recognise. **Lexicon: HIGH; Allusion density: HIGH.**

The third is the **sermo cotidianus** of the *Satires* and *Epistles*. Here the problem is the opposite of the *Odes*: not compression into a perfect mosaic, but conversation. Horace writes the way educated Romans talked, rapid-fire and disorienting: unannounced changes of speaker, imaginary objections, half-sentences, ellipses, colloquial idioms, jokes that depend on a tone of voice we cannot hear. The *Satires* are the recommended starting point for Horace, and rightly, because the word order is closer to prose and the situations are often funny. But they are still tricky. **Syntactic complexity: HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH.**

And if you are trying to scan him, the *Odes* are a nightmare of their own. Virgil wrote in one metre. Horace uses around a dozen: the Sapphic stanza, the Alcaic stanza, several kinds of Asclepiad system, Archilochian and Pythiambic couplets, and more, each with its own rules, its own fixed and variable syllables and its own pauses, changing from one poem to the next.

None of this is a reason to avoid him. Horace is one of the most interesting authors in Latin to translate precisely because every line is a puzzle with a solution, and the solution is always worth finding.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Carmina II.10 -- The Golden Mean

**Source:** Horace, *Carmina* (*Odes*), Book II, poem 10, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/horace/carm2.shtml)

**Context:** An ode addressed to a friend called Licinius, probably Licinius Murena, the brother-in-law of Maecenas, and a man with a famously impulsive temperament. The theme is the *aurea mediocritas*, the "golden mean": the Aristotelian idea that virtue lies between two extremes, applied here to fortune and ambition. The irony is painful. If this Licinius is Murena, he did not take the advice: around 23-22 BC, about the time this book of odes was published, he was implicated in a conspiracy against Augustus and executed. you shudder at the storms, hugging too closely
the treacherous shore.

Whoever loves the golden mean
is safe: he is free of the squalor
of a crumbling house, and, temperate, he is free
of a palace that breeds envy.

The giant pine is shaken more often
by the winds, and lofty towers fall
with a heavier crash, and the lightning strikes
the highest peaks of the mountains.

A heart well prepared hopes in bad times
and fears in good times
for a change of fortune. Jupiter brings back
the ugly winters; he also

takes them away. If things are bad now, they will not
always be so: sometimes Apollo wakes
the silent Muse with his lyre, and does not
always draw his bow.

In hard times show yourself spirited
and brave; and in the same way, wisely,
when the wind is too favourable you will take in
your swelling sails.

**Analysis:**

First, a warning about the famous phrase. *Aurea mediocritas* does not mean "golden mediocrity" in the modern sense of being average and unambitious. *Mediocritas* is the middle point between two extremes, the Aristotelian mean, and it is "golden" because it is precious. The poem's argument is not "aim low" but "do not go to extremes, in either direction": not too far out to sea, not too close to the rocks; not a slum, not a palace; not despair in bad times, not overconfidence in good ones.

The structure is a ring. The ode opens and closes with the same image, sailing: in the first stanza Licinius must neither push out too far nor hug the coast, in the last he must be brave when the wind is against him and take in sail when it is too favourable. In between, the third stanza is a tricolon of disasters that strike whatever stands highest (the tallest pine, the highest towers, the mountain peaks), and the fourth and fifth turn to the gods: Jupiter brings winters and takes them away, and Apollo does not always shoot his arrows. Fortune always changes; a wise man expects it.

This is a good place to watch Horace's word order at work. In the second stanza, *obsoleti* waits until the start of the next line for its noun, *tecti*; *invidenda* is separated from *aula* by *sobrius*. The sense also runs across the ends of stanzas: the fourth stanza ends on *idem*, and the verb, *summovet*, only arrives at the start of the fifth, so that Jupiter literally takes the winter away across the gap. The grammar is mostly straightforward once the words have been reunited: a future used as advice (*Rectius vives*, *contrahes*), gerunds in the ablative (*urgendo*, *premendo*, "by pressing"), datives with *sperat* and *metuit* (*infestis*, *secundis*, "in adverse and favourable circumstances"), and an imperative, *appare*. Note *montis* and *Informis*, old accusative plurals in *-is*. And if you have read the Virgil entry, notice *Informis hiemes*, "ugly winters": the same adjective Virgil uses for Polyphemus.

### Excerpt 2: Epodi 10 -- A Curse on Mevius

**Source:** Horace, *Epodi*, poem 10 (complete), from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/horace/epodes.shtml)

**Context:** A *propempticon* is a send-off poem, wishing a friend a safe voyage; Horace himself wrote a famous one for Virgil's ship (*Odes* I.3). Epode 10 is the exact opposite: an anti-*propempticon*, a poem wishing a ship every possible disaster, because Mevius is on board. Mevius was a bad poet, and Virgil had already mocked him in the *Eclogues* along with his partner in crime Bavius: "whoever does not hate Bavius, let him love your poems, Mevius". Nobody knows exactly what Mevius did to deserve this; it may simply be that he wrote badly. The model is Archilochus, the Greek inventor of poetic invective, one of whose curses on a former friend, wishing him shipwrecked naked on the coast of Thrace, survives on a papyrus. The metre is the epodic couplet: an iambic trimeter followed by a shorter iambic dimeter.

**Latin Text:**

> Mala soluta navis exit alite
> ferens olentem Mevium.
> ut horridis utrumque verberes latus,
> Auster, memento fluctibus;
> niger rudentis Eurus inverso mari
> fractosque remos differat;
> insurgat Aquilo, quantus altis montibus
> frangit trementis ilices;
> nec sidus atra nocte amicum adpareat,
> qua tristis Orion cadit;
> quietiore nec feratur aequore
> quam Graia victorum manus,
> cum Pallas usto vertit iram ab Ilio
> in inpiam Aiacis ratem.
> o quantus instat navitis sudor tuis
> tibique pallor luteus
> et illa non virilis heiulatio
> preces et aversum ad Iovem,
> Ionius udo cum remugiens sinus
> Noto carinam ruperit.
> opima quodsi praeda curvo litore
> porrecta mergos iuverit,
> libidinosus immolabitur caper
> et agna Tempestatibus.

**English Translation:**

Under an evil omen the ship slips its moorings and sets out,
carrying stinking Mevius.
Remember, South Wind, to lash both its sides
with savage waves;
let the black East Wind, turning the sea upside down,
scatter its rigging and its broken oars;
let the North Wind rise, as strong as when on the high mountains
it shatters the trembling holm-oaks;
and let no friendly star appear in the black night
where grim Orion sets;
and may he be carried on no calmer sea
than the victorious army of the Greeks
when Pallas turned her anger from burnt Troy
onto the impious ship of Ajax.
Oh, what sweat is waiting for your sailors,
and for you, a sickly yellow pallor,
and that unmanly wailing,
and prayers to a Jupiter who has turned away,
when the Ionian gulf, bellowing under the rainy
South Wind, has smashed your keel!
And if you end up as a rich prize stretched out on the curving shore,
and make a feast for the seabirds,
a lustful billy goat will be sacrificed,
and a lamb, to the Storms.

**Analysis:**

The joke is in the form. A send-off poem should pray for calm seas, favourable winds and a friendly star; Horace runs through the same checklist in reverse. Every wind of the compass is called up in turn (Auster from the south, Eurus from the east, Aquilo from the north, then Notus, another name for the south wind), each with an instruction, in a series of jussive subjunctives (*differat*, *insurgat*, *adpareat*, *feratur*) and a future imperative with an *ut* clause (*ut... verberes... memento*, "remember to lash"). The last couplet completes the parody: a traveller who arrived safely would make a thank-offering to the gods of the sea; Horace promises one to the Storms if Mevius drowns.

In the middle, a myth arrives, as always in Horace, without explanation. After the fall of Troy, the lesser Ajax dragged Cassandra away from the altar of Pallas Athena, and the goddess took her revenge by wrecking his ship on the way home. The reader is supposed to know the story, and to enjoy the hyperbole: Mevius deserves the same storm that the gods sent against the worst sacrilege of the Trojan War.

The vocabulary is part of the fun. *Olentem*, "smelly", is the only thing we are told about Mevius. *Pallor luteus* is a yellowish, sickly pallor; *heiulatio* is a rare word for loud, undignified wailing, from the verb *heiulare*, and Horace adds *non virilis*, "unmanly", to make sure the insult lands. *Rudentes* are a ship's ropes and rigging; *mergi* are diving seabirds, cormorants or gulls. And look at the grammar of the climax: *cum... ruperit*, a future perfect, "when the gulf has broken your keel", followed by *quodsi... iuverit*, "but if you have pleased the birds", both imagining Mevius's death as already accomplished. *Rudentis*, *trementis*: old accusative plurals in *-is* again. *Libidinosus caper* may be a final dig: the goat was proverbially lustful, and a little too appropriate as an offering for Mevius.

A note on the text: The Latin Library prints *ilics*, which is not a word, for *ilices* ("holm-oaks") in line 8, and leaves out the punctuation at the end of line 20, after *ruperit*. Both are corrected here. *Inpiam* is the source's spelling of *impiam*, and has been kept.

### Excerpt 3: Sermones I.5.1-26 -- The Journey to Brundisium

**Source:** Horace, *Sermones* (*Satires*), Book I, poem 5, lines 1-26, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/horace/serm1.shtml)

**Context:** In 37 BC (or possibly 38) Maecenas travelled from Rome to Brundisium on a delicate diplomatic mission, to patch up the relationship between Octavian and Antony, and he took Horace along, together with Virgil, Varius and other friends. Horace turned the journey into a satire, a travel diary in verse, modelled on the *Iter Siculum*, the "Journey to Sicily", of Lucilius (whose fragments are in the Archaic Era chapter). The joke of the poem is that Horace tells us almost nothing about the diplomacy, and everything about bad water, bad inns, mosquitoes, his sore eyes and the comedians they met along the way. These are the first twenty-six lines: from Rome to Aricia, then by canal-boat through the Pontine Marshes, to Anxur, modern Terracina.

**Latin Text:**

> Egressum magna me accepit Aricia Roma
> hospitio modico; rhetor comes Heliodorus,
> Graecorum longe doctissimus; inde Forum Appi
> differtum nautis cauponibus atque malignis.
> hoc iter ignavi divisimus, altius ac nos
> praecinctis unum: minus est gravis Appia tardis.
> hic ego propter aquam, quod erat deterrima, ventri
> indico bellum, cenantis haud animo aequo
> exspectans comites. iam nox inducere terris
> umbras et caelo diffundere signa parabat:
> tum pueri nautis, pueris convicia nautae
> ingerere: 'huc adpelle'; 'trecentos inseris'; 'ohe,
> iam satis est.' dum aes exigitur, dum mula ligatur,
> tota abit hora. mali culices ranaeque palustres
> avertunt somnos; absentem cantat amicam
> multa prolutus vappa nauta atque viator
> certatim; tandem fessus dormire viator
> incipit ac missae pastum retinacula mulae
> nauta piger saxo religat stertitque supinus.
> iamque dies aderat, nil cum procedere lintrem
> sentimus, donec cerebrosus prosilit unus
> ac mulae nautaeque caput lumbosque saligno
> fuste dolat: quarta vix demum exponimur hora.
> ora manusque tua lavimus, Feronia, lympha.
> milia tum pransi tria repimus atque subimus
> inpositum saxis late candentibus Anxur.

**English Translation:**

When I had left great Rome, Aricia welcomed me
with modest hospitality; my companion was the rhetorician Heliodorus,
by far the most learned of the Greeks. From there to Forum Appii,
crammed with boatmen and crooked innkeepers.
This stretch we lazily split into two days, though travellers more briskly
girt than us do it in one: the Appian Way is less of a strain if you take it slowly.
Here, because of the water, which was foul,
I declare war on my stomach, and wait, in no good temper,
while my companions have their dinner. Now night was getting ready
to draw its shadows over the earth and scatter the stars across the sky.
Then the slaves start hurling abuse at the boatmen, the boatmen at the slaves:
"Pull in here!" "You're cramming in three hundred! Hey,
that's enough!" While the fares are collected and the mule is harnessed,
a whole hour goes by. Damned mosquitoes and marsh frogs
keep sleep away; a boatman, soaked in cheap wine,
sings of the girlfriend he left behind, and a passenger
competes with him: at last the passenger, worn out, starts to fall asleep,
and the lazy boatman lets the mule out to graze, ties its rope
to a stone, and lies on his back snoring.
Day was already breaking when we realise that the boat
is not moving at all, until one hothead jumps out
and with a willow cudgel gives both mule and boatman
a beating on the head and back. At last, only at the fourth hour, we are put ashore.
We wash our faces and hands in your spring, Feronia.
Then, after lunch, we crawl three miles and climb up
to Anxur, perched on its far-gleaming white rocks.

**Analysis:**

This is the *sermo cotidianus* in action, and it shows why the *Satires* are both the best way into Horace and still tricky. The narrative is simple, a day-by-day travel diary, and the word order is close to prose. But the texture changes every few lines. Lines 9-10 are a deliberate parody of epic: *iam nox inducere terris / umbras et caelo diffundere signa parabat*, "now night was preparing to draw its shadows over the earth and scatter the stars across the sky", a perfect Virgilian line about nightfall, followed immediately by the slaves and the boatmen screaming at each other. The shouting itself is pure spoken Latin, given without any indication of who is speaking: *huc adpelle* ("pull in here!"), *trecentos inseris* ("you're cramming in three hundred"), *ohe, iam satis est* ("hey, that's enough!"). The translator has to work out the speakers from the sense.

Watch the grammar. *Ingerere* in line 12 is a historic infinitive, used instead of a finite verb to describe a lively scene ("they started hurling"); the narrative jumps constantly between perfects (*accepit*, *divisimus*, *lavimus*) and historic presents (*indico*, *avertunt*, *sentimus*, *exponimur*, *repimus*). *Ventri indico bellum*, "I declare war on my stomach", is mock-military language for refusing to eat, because the water is too dangerous to drink. *Altius praecinctis*, "those girt higher than us", refers to travellers who hitch their tunics up to walk faster. *Vappa* is wine that has gone flat. *Cerebrosus* is a "hothead", from *cerebrum*. And *dolat*, from *dolare*, normally means to hew wood with an axe: the passenger "carpenters" the mule and the boatman with his cudgel.

Then there is the geography, which is real. Aricia is modern Ariccia in the Alban Hills, the first stop south of Rome on the Appian Way. Forum Appii was the start of a canal through the Pontine Marshes, travelled overnight in a barge towed by a mule along the bank, which is how the mule and the boatman both come to be asleep. The spring of the goddess Feronia and the town of Anxur, with the temple of Jupiter Anxur on its gleaming white cliffs, are at Terracina, where the canal ended. The diplomatic mission, the reason for the whole trip, is mentioned in a single line a little later. Everything else is mosquitoes.

## Legacy and Impact

Horace survived antiquity thanks to two different kinds of readers. For the schools, he was immediately a classic: within a generation of his death he was being taught to Roman schoolboys, and Quintilian called him "almost the only Roman lyric poet worth reading". For Christian readers in late antiquity and the Middle Ages, it was above all the *Satires* and the *Epistles* that mattered. A poet who exposed greed, ambition, luxury and hypocrisy, who preached contentment and moderation, and who laughed at the vanity of human desires could be read as a moralist, and medieval readers called him *Horatius ethicus*, "Horace the moralist". He was copied in monasteries all over Europe, and around 850 manuscripts survive, more than for almost any other classical poet.

That reputation is still visible in Dante. In the fourth canto of the *Inferno*, among the great poets of antiquity in Limbo, Horace appears second only to Homer, and Dante calls him *Orazio satiro*, "Horace the satirist": for Dante, the author of the *Satires* came before the author of the *Odes*. Petrarch, who loved him, wrote him a letter in verse. In the Renaissance the *Odes* came back into their own and became the model of lyric poetry across Europe: Ronsard in France, Ben Jonson in England, and in Italy the poets who tried to reproduce his metres in Italian, culminating centuries later in Carducci's *Odi barbare*. The *Ars Poetica*, meanwhile, became the rulebook of classical literary theory, the direct ancestor of Boileau's *Art poétique* and Pope's *Essay on Criticism*. For centuries Horace was not just a poet but the master of style, the authority educated Europeans quoted on how to write, how to judge and how to live.

And then the modern world forgot almost all of it and kept two words. *Carpe diem* comes from a short ode of eight lines, *Odes* I.11, addressed to a woman called Leuconoe who is trying to find out from astrologers how long she and the poet have to live. Horace tells her to stop asking, because nobody can know, and to be wise, strain the wine, cut back long hopes, and *carpe diem, quam minimum credula postero*: "pluck the day, trusting as little as possible in tomorrow". *Carpere* is a gardener's verb: you pluck a fruit when it is ripe, carefully, one at a time. The message is Epicurean, and it is about moderation: do not waste the present worrying about a future you cannot control. Modern culture, helped along by a famous film, has turned it into the opposite: "YOLO", live as if there were no tomorrow, party as hard as you can because you might die. It is hard to think of a worse misreading. The man who wrote *carpe diem* also wrote the ode on the golden mean, the warning to take in sail when the wind is too favourable, and two books of satires against people who cannot control their appetites.

Read him properly, and Horace is far more than a slogan: a satirist who laughs at everyone including himself, a moralist who never preaches, a friend who is loyal to the end, and one of the greatest lyric poets who ever lived.

## Final Rating

**Tier: S (GOD PLEASE HELP)**

Horace scores at or near the top in almost every criterion. In the *Odes*, the hyperbaton is more extreme than in any other Latin poet, the vocabulary mixes Greek words, rare words and new formations, the allusions to Greek lyric are constant and unexplained, and the metres change from poem to poem. In the *Satires* and *Epistles*, the challenge shifts to rapid, colloquial conversation, with changes of speaker that are never signalled and jokes that depend on tone. He is leagues harder than Virgil, who is difficult but honest; Horace is difficult on purpose. The *Satires* are the right place to start, but even they are tricky; the *Odes* are where the real summit is. Mid to high S, and well deserved. And worth every minute.

# TITUS LIVIUS -- LIVY (64 or 59 BC -- 17 AD)

## Biography

Livy wrote the history of Rome from the arrival of Aeneas in Italy down to his own day, in one hundred and forty-two books. Let that number settle for a moment. Caesar's *Gallic War* is seven books. Sallust wrote two short monographs. Livy wrote one hundred and forty-two books, each of them long, over roughly forty years, which works out at three or four books a year, every year, for the whole of his adult life. Even the Romans thought this was remarkable, and Livy himself seems to have found it a little alarming.

**The man from Padua.** Titus Livius was born at Patavium, modern Padua, in northern Italy: in 59 BC according to Jerome, though many scholars prefer 64. Patavium was one of the richest cities in Italy, and it had a reputation for old-fashioned morality and strict family life, which shows in every page of his history. It also gave him an accent. The historian and politician Asinius Pollio, who never missed a chance to be unpleasant, sneered that Livy's Latin had a touch of *Patavinitas*, "Paduan-ness": a provincial flavour that a true Roman ear could detect. Nobody since has been able to work out exactly what Pollio meant, which may be the best proof that he was simply being a snob.

**A historian with no career.** What makes Livy unusual among Roman historians is what he did not do. Sallust had been a senator and a governor; Caesar had conquered Gaul; Tacitus would be a consul. Livy never held office, never commanded an army, never sat in the Senate, and as far as we know never even tried. He was a private man of letters who moved to Rome, probably in the 30s BC, and devoted his life to writing. Before the history he wrote philosophical dialogues, now lost, and a letter to his son on how to write well, in which he advised him to read Demosthenes and Cicero and to imitate whoever was most like them. Around 27 BC, as Augustus was "restoring the Republic", he began the *Ab Urbe Condita*. He would still be writing it forty years later.

**"My little Pompeian".** Livy was on good terms with Augustus, but on his own terms. In his account of the civil wars he praised Pompey so warmly that Augustus, teasing him, used to call him a "Pompeian" (*Pompeianum eum Augustus appellaret*): a partisan of the losing side, the side his adoptive father Caesar had destroyed. And yet, as Tacitus records, "this did not damage their friendship". Livy also wrote of Brutus and Cassius, Caesar's assassins, not as bandits and parricides but as distinguished men, and Augustus let it pass. The story is preserved for a reason. Tacitus puts it in the mouth of the historian Cremutius Cordus, on trial under Tiberius in AD 25 for having praised Brutus and Cassius in his own history; Cordus cites Livy as proof that under Augustus a historian could say such things. It did not save him. Cordus starved himself to death, and the Senate ordered his books to be burned. The contrast says everything about how much freedom Livy had, and how quickly it disappeared after him.

**The tutor of an emperor.** Livy's influence reached into the imperial family. Suetonius says that it was Livy who encouraged the young Claudius, the future emperor, awkward, stammering and generally regarded by his family as a joke, to write history. Claudius took the advice, and wrote dozens of books, including a history of the Etruscans and one of Carthage, all of them lost.

**The most famous man in Rome.** By the end of his life Livy was a celebrity. Pliny the Younger tells the story of a man from Gades, modern Cadiz in Spain, at the far western edge of the known world, who was so overwhelmed by Livy's fame that he travelled all the way to Rome just to see him, looked at him, and went home again. Livy himself, according to Pliny the Elder, wrote in the preface to one of his later books that he had already won enough glory and could have stopped, if his restless mind had not fed on the work. He could not stop. He carried the story down to the death of Augustus's stepson Drusus in 9 BC, and then, it seems, simply ran out of life rather than material.

**Death, and a skeleton in Padua.** He returned to Padua and died there in AD 17, the same year as Ovid, if Jerome is right. Padua never forgot him. In 1413, during building work in the city, a lead coffin was dug up near an old inscription mentioning a Titus Livius, and the Paduans, delirious with civic pride, declared that they had found the bones of the great historian. The remains were carried in triumph and eventually placed in the Palazzo della Ragione, where a monument to Livy still stands. The inscription almost certainly belonged to a different, entirely obscure Livius, a freedman. Padua did not let that spoil the celebration.

## Most Important Works

- **Ab Urbe Condita Libri** (*Books from the Foundation of the City*, c. 27 BC -- AD 17) -- the history of Rome in one hundred and forty-two books, from the arrival of Aeneas and the founding of the city down to 9 BC: more than seven hundred years of history. Livy organised it in groups of five and ten books (pentads and decades), each around a great period: the kings and the early Republic, the conquest of Italy, the wars with Carthage, the conquest of the Greek East, the civil wars. It is a history built on moral examples: Livy says in his preface that the great value of history is that it shows you, set out in a clear record, models to imitate and disgraces to avoid. Only thirty-five books survive: Books I-X (from the beginnings to 293 BC) and Books XXI-XLV (from Hannibal's war in 218 BC to 167 BC), the last five with gaps. For almost all the rest we have the *Periochae*, brief summaries written in late antiquity, plus a few fragments and quotations. [Fragmentary -- 35 of 142 books]
- **Philosophical dialogues** and **a letter to his son** on style -- mentioned by Seneca and Quintilian. [Lost]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

After the hell of Virgil and Horace, Livy is honestly a breath of fresh air. He writes prose, his vocabulary is the standard vocabulary of classical Latin, and his Latin is clear, correct and full-bodied. Quintilian, contrasting him with the abrupt, compressed Sallust, praised his *lactea ubertas*, his "milky richness": the prose of an author who never cuts a corner and never leaves you short of anything.

That richness is also the problem. Livy builds his narrative out of long periods, sentences in which the main clause is surrounded by participles, ablatives absolute, *cum* clauses and relative clauses, each adding a detail, a cause, a circumstance or a consequence, all held together until the main verb arrives at the end. He narrates every step of every event, every embassy, every debate in the Senate, every manoeuvre before a battle, and he does it painstakingly slowly. It is no wonder he needed one hundred and forty-two books to cover seven centuries. The hardest thing about Livy may simply be staying awake until you get to the interesting part. **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM to HIGH.**

There are tricky points as well. The speeches, of which there are hundreds, are often given in indirect discourse, so that whole paragraphs run in the accusative and infinitive and the subjunctive, and the reader has to keep track of who is speaking and when. Livy is fond of the **historic infinitive**, an infinitive used instead of a finite verb to describe rapid, vivid or repeated action ("they shouted, they ran, they begged"), which regularly confuses students who are looking for a main verb that is not there. The first decade, on the legends of early Rome, has a poetic colouring, with words and turns of phrase that recall Virgil and Ennius. And Livy loves variety in construction (*variatio*), changing the form of a phrase just when you expect it to repeat. **Lexicon: MEDIUM; Stylistic figures: MEDIUM.** The allusions are mostly to Roman history and institutions rather than to literature, and they are usually explained by the narrative itself. **Allusion density: LOW to MEDIUM.**

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Ab Urbe Condita XXV.31.4-11 -- The Fall of Syracuse and the Death of Archimedes

**Source:** Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita*, Book XXV, chapter 31, sections 4-11, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/livy/liv.25.shtml). The Latin Library's text of Books XXV and XXXIX writes consonantal *v* as *u* (*uoluerit*, *uiis*) and does not capitalise sentences; both are kept here as printed.

**Context:** 212 BC, the Second Punic War. Syracuse, Rome's old ally under King Hiero, had gone over to Carthage after Hiero's death, and the Roman general Marcus Claudius Marcellus had been besieging it for more than two years, held off largely by the war machines of the city's most famous citizen, the mathematician Archimedes. Now the last quarter of the city, Achradina, has surrendered, and its envoys ask only for their lives. This passage gives Marcellus's reply, the sack of the city, and the death of Archimedes, the scientist of Vitruvius's *eureka* story.

**Latin Text:**

Marcellus consilio aduocato et adhibitis etiam Syracusanis qui per seditiones pulsi ab domo intra praesidia Romana fuerant, respondit non plura per annos quinquaginta benefacta Hieronis quam paucis his annis maleficia eorum qui Syracusas tenuerint erga populum Romanum esse; sed pleraque eorum quo debuerint reccidisse foederumque ruptorum ipsos ab se grauiores multo quam populus Romanus uoluerit poenas exegisse. se quidem tertium annum circumsedere Syracusas, non ut populus Romanus seruam ciuitatem haberet sed ne transfugarum duces captam et oppressam tenerent. quid potuerint Syracusani facere, exemplo uel eos esse Syracusanorum qui intra praesidia Romana fuerint uel Hispanum ducem Moericum qui praesidium tradiderit uel ipsorum Syracusanorum postremo serum quidem, sed forte consilium. sibi omnium laborum periculorumque circa moenia Syracusana terra marique tam diu exhaustorum nequaquam tantum fructum esse quod capere <sibi contigerit, quantum si seruare> Syracusas potuisset. inde quaestor cum praesidio ad Nassum ad accipiendam pecuniam regiam custodiendamque missus. urbs diripienda militi data est custodibus diuisis per domos eorum qui intra praesidia Romana fuerant. cum multa irae, multa auaritiae foeda exempla ederentur, Archimeden memoriae proditum est in tanto tumultu, quantum captae urbis in <uiis> discursus diripientium militum ciere poterat, intentum formis quas in puluere descripserat ab ignaro milite quis esset interfectum; aegre id Marcellum tulisse sepulturaeque curam habitam, et propinquis etiam inquisitis honori praesidioque nomen ac memoriam eius fuisse. hoc maxime modo Syracusae captae; in quibus praedae tantum fuit, quantum uix capta Carthagine tum fuisset cum qua uiribus aequis certabatur.

**English Translation:**

Marcellus called a council, to which he also invited those Syracusans who had been driven from their homes in the civil strife and had taken refuge inside the Roman lines, and replied that the good deeds of Hiero over fifty years were no more numerous than the crimes committed against the Roman people in these last few years by those who had held Syracuse; but that most of those crimes had come back down on the heads that deserved them, and that the breakers of the treaties had exacted from themselves a far heavier penalty than the Roman people would have wished. He himself, he said, had been besieging Syracuse for the third year now, not so that the Roman people might hold the city in slavery, but so that the leaders of the deserters should not keep it captive and oppressed. What the Syracusans could have done was shown by the example of those Syracusans who had been inside the Roman lines, or of the Spanish commander Moericus, who had handed over the garrison, or finally by the decision of the Syracusans themselves, late, it was true, but brave. For all the labours and dangers he had endured for so long by land and sea around the walls of Syracuse, the reward of having managed to capture it was nowhere near as great as it would have been if he had been able to save Syracuse. Then the quaestor was sent with a guard to the Island, to take over and protect the royal treasury. The city was handed over to the soldiers to plunder, after guards had been posted at the houses of those who had been inside the Roman lines. While many shameful examples of rage and of greed were being given, it is recorded that Archimedes, amid all the uproar that soldiers running through the streets of a captured city to plunder it could raise, was absorbed in figures he had drawn in the dust, and was killed by a soldier who did not know who he was; that Marcellus took this hard; that care was taken for his burial; and that his relatives were even searched out, and his name and memory brought them honour and protection. This, more or less, is how Syracuse was taken; and there was as much booty in it as there would hardly have been in Carthage, had Carthage been taken at that time, a city with which Rome was fighting on equal terms.

**Analysis:**

This passage shows both sides of Livy's style. The first half is Marcellus's reply, and it is entirely in **indirect discourse**: one long chain of accusatives and infinitives (*non plura... benefacta... esse*, *pleraque... reccidisse*, *ipsos... poenas exegisse*, *se... circumsedere*) and subjunctives in the subordinate clauses (*tenuerint*, *debuerint*, *uoluerit*, *haberet*, *tenerent*, *potuerint*), all depending on the single verb *respondit*. Nobody is quoted directly, and the translator has to rebuild the speech in the first person while keeping track of who "he" and "they" are. The final sentence of the speech is a good test: *nequaquam tantum fructum esse quod capere sibi contigerit, quantum si seruare Syracusas potuisset*, "the fruit of having succeeded in capturing it was nowhere near as great as if he had been able to save it". A conqueror regretting that he had to conquer is a very Roman pose, and Livy gives it to Marcellus with conviction.

The second half is narrative, and here comes the death of Archimedes, told with a restraint that makes it more moving. Notice how Livy distances himself from the story: *memoriae proditum est*, "it has been handed down to memory", followed by another chain of infinitives (*Archimeden... interfectum*, *Marcellum tulisse*, *curam habitam*, *nomen... fuisse*). He is reporting a tradition, not vouching for it. And notice the contrast he builds: the whole city in uproar, soldiers running through the streets, *multa irae, multa auaritiae foeda exempla* (a fine example of anaphora and of Livy's moralising), and in the middle of it all one old man staring at geometrical figures in the sand, so absorbed that he does not notice the end of his own city. Later writers added that he told the soldier "do not disturb my circles"; Livy does not, and his version is better for it.

Grammar to watch: the ablatives absolute at the start (*consilio aduocato et adhibitis... Syracusanis*) and later (*custodibus diuisis*, *propinquis... inquisitis*); the gerundives *ad accipiendam pecuniam... custodiendamque* and *urbs diripienda... data est*, "the city was given to be plundered"; the double dative *honori praesidioque... fuisse*, "was an honour and a protection to them"; and the last sentence, an unreal comparison in the past, *quantum uix capta Carthagine tum fuisset*, "as much as there would hardly have been if Carthage had been captured then". The angle brackets in *<sibi contigerit, quantum si seruare>* and *<uiis>* mark words supplied by modern editors where the manuscripts have a gap.

### Excerpt 2: Ab Urbe Condita XXXIX.8.3-8 -- The Bacchanalia Scandal

**Source:** Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita*, Book XXXIX, chapter 8, sections 3-8, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/livy/liv.39.shtml)

**Context:** 186 BC. Rome has just won its wars in the Greek East, and Greek culture is pouring into the city. That year the consuls were ordered to investigate "secret conspiracies", and what they uncovered was the worship of Bacchus (Dionysus), carried out in secret, at night, by thousands of men and women. Livy tells the story at length, almost like a novel, with an informer, a brave freedwoman and a dramatic investigation, and the Senate's reaction was one of the most violent religious crackdowns in Roman history: thousands were arrested, many were executed, and the cult was placed under strict state control. The Senate's actual decree survives, engraved on a bronze tablet found in southern Italy in 1640 and now in Vienna, so we can compare Livy's account with the original document. This passage is Livy's description of how the cult began and what it involved. It is a fascinating text, because it is also a portrait of Roman fear: of foreigners, of secrecy, of women, and of anything happening at night.

**Latin Text:**

Graecus ignobilis in Etruriam primum uenit nulla cum arte earum, quas multas ad animorum corporumque cultum nobis eruditissima omnium gens inuexit, sacrificulus et uates; nec is qui aperta religione, propalam et quaestum et disciplinam profitendo, animos errore imbueret, sed occultorum et nocturnorum antistes sacrorum. initia erant, quae primo paucis tradita sunt, deinde uulgari coepta sunt per uiros mulieresque. additae uoluptates religioni uini et epularum, quo plurium animi illicerentur. cum uinum animos <incendissent>, et nox et mixti feminis mares, aetatis tenerae maioribus, discrimen omne pudoris exstinxissent, corruptelae primum omnis generis fieri coeptae, cum ad id quisque, quo natura pronioris libidinis esset, paratam uoluptatem haberet. nec unum genus noxae, stupra promiscua ingenuorum feminarumque erant, sed falsi testes, falsa signa testamentaque et indicia ex eadem officina exibant: uenena indidem intestinaeque caedes, ita ut ne corpora quidem interdum ad sepulturam exstarent. multa dolo, pleraque per uim audebantur. occulebat uim quod prae ululatibus tympanorumque et cymbalorum strepitu nulla uox quiritantium inter stupra et caedes exaudiri poterat.

**English Translation:**

A Greek of low birth came first into Etruria, bringing none of the many arts that the most learned of all peoples has brought to us for the cultivation of mind and body: a petty sacrificer and soothsayer; and not one of those who fill people's minds with error through an open religion, professing both their trade and their teaching in public, but a priest of secret, nocturnal rites. There were initiations, which at first were passed on to a few, but then began to spread widely among men and women. To the religion were added the pleasures of wine and feasting, to lure in the minds of more people. When wine had inflamed their minds, and the night, and the mingling of men with women and of the young with their elders, had extinguished every sense of shame, corruption of every kind began to be practised, since each person had at hand the pleasure to which his nature was most inclined. Nor was there only one kind of wrongdoing, the promiscuous debauchery of free men and women: false witnesses, forged seals and wills, and false evidence also came out of the same workshop; from the same source, too, came poisonings and murders within families, so that sometimes not even the bodies could be found for burial. Much was dared by trickery, more by violence. The violence was hidden, because amid the howling and the din of drums and cymbals no voice of those crying for help could be heard among the debauchery and the murders.

**Analysis:**

The first sentence is a small masterpiece of Roman prejudice. The man is *Graecus* and *ignobilis*, Greek and of low birth; he brings none of the arts for which the Greeks, *eruditissima omnium gens*, "the most learned of all peoples", are admired; he is only a *sacrificulus*, a diminutive and contemptuous word for a dealer in sacrifices. Livy then distinguishes him from the ordinary religious charlatans who at least work in the open (*aperta religione, propalam*) with a relative clause of characteristic, *nec is qui... imbueret*, "not the kind of man who would...". The real danger is secrecy: *occultorum et nocturnorum antistes sacrorum*, with the noun *sacrorum* held back to the very end, after its two adjectives.

From there the passage builds like a prosecution speech. The cult spreads from the few to the many; wine and food are added *quo plurium animi illicerentur*, a purpose clause with *quo* because it contains a comparative (*plurium*); then the great *cum* clause, where wine, night and the mixing of sexes and ages extinguish all shame. Then the list of crimes grows: from sexual misconduct to forgery, perjury, poisoning and murder, all from *eadem officina*, "the same workshop", a deliberately commercial image, as if crime were being mass-produced. The chiasmus *multa dolo, pleraque per uim* sums it up, and the last sentence closes on sound: *ululatibus tympanorumque et cymbalorum strepitu*, the howling and the drums drowning out the cries for help.

How much of this is true is another question. The surviving decree of the Senate is much more measured: it does not ban the worship of Bacchus but limits it, forbidding large gatherings, male priests and secret funds unless the Senate gives permission. Livy's account reads like the official justification of a panic, and it is a valuable reminder that Roman historians, too, wrote from a point of view. For the translator, the main traps are the vocabulary of religion and law (*initia*, "initiation rites"; *antistes*, "priest"; *signa*, here "seals", not "signs"; *indicia*, "evidence"; *quiritantium*, from *quiritare*, "to cry out for help", originally to call on the Quirites, the Roman citizens) and the length of the central *cum* clause. *Intestinae caedes* are murders inside the family, not in the intestines.

### Excerpt 3: Ab Urbe Condita CXX (fragments) -- The Death of Cicero, and Livy's Verdict

**Source:** Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita*, Book CXX (lost), preserved by Seneca the Elder, *Suasoriae* 6.17 and 6.22, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/seneca.suasoriae.html). The round brackets mark words supplied by modern editors. Like the Livy pages above, the text does not capitalise sentences, and that is kept here.

**Context:** Book CXX of Livy, which covered the year 43 BC, is lost, like every book after XLV. But two of its paragraphs survive because Seneca the Elder, the father of the philosopher, quoted them in a collection of rhetorical exercises. One of the set topics for practice speeches was "Cicero deliberates whether to beg Antony for his life", and Seneca, discussing it, quotes what the historians had written about Cicero's death. Livy's account of the murder and his final judgement on Cicero are the most substantial pieces of the lost books we have. They are especially valuable because Livy wrote them under Augustus, the man who had agreed to put Cicero's name on the proscription lists.

**Latin Text:**

[17] M. Cicero sub adventum triumvirorum urbe cesserat pro certo habens, id quod erat, non magis Antonio (se) eripi quam Caesari Cassium et Brutum posse. primo in Tusculanum fugerat, inde transversis itineribus in Formianum ut ab Caieta navem conscensurus proficiscitur. unde aliquotiens in altum provectum cum modo venti adversi rettulissent, modo ipse iactationem navis caeco volvente fluctu pati non posset, taedium tandem eum et fugae et vitae cepit regressusque ad superiorem villam, quae paulo plus mille passibus a mari abest, 'moriar' inquit 'in patria saepe servata'. satis constat servos fortiter fideliterque paratos fuisse ad dimicandum; ipsum deponi lecticam et quietos pati quod fors iniqua cogeret iussisse. prominenti ex lectica praebentique immotam cervicem caput praecisum est. nec satis stolidae crudelitati militum fuit: manus quoque scripsisse aliquid in Antonium exprobrantes praeciderunt. ita relatum caput ad Antonium iussuque eius inter duas manus in rostris positum, ubi ille consul, ubi saepe consularis, ubi eo ipso anno adversus Antonium quanta nulla umquam humana vox cum admiratione eloquentiae auditus fuerat. vix attollentes (madentes) lacrimis oculos homines intueri trucidati membra civis poterant.

[22] Vixit tres et sexaginta annos, ut, si vis afuisset, ne immatura quidem mors videri possit. ingenium et operibus et praemiis operum felix, ipse fortunae diu prosperae; sed in longo tenore felicitatis magnis interim ictus vulneribus, exilio, ruina partium, pro quibus steterat, filiae amatae exitu tam tristi atque acerbo, omnium adversorum nihil, ut viro dignum erat, tulit praeter mortem, quae vere aestimanti minus indigna videri potuit, quod a victore inimico (nihil) crudelius passus erat quam quod eiusdem fortunae compos victo fecisset. si quis tamen virtutibus vitia pensarit, vir magnus ac memorabilis fuit, et in cuius laudes (ex)equendas Cicerone laudatore opus fuerit.

**English Translation:**

[17] Marcus Cicero had left the city as the triumvirs approached, regarding it as certain, as indeed it was, that he could no more be rescued from Antony than Cassius and Brutus could be from Caesar (that is, Octavian). He had first fled to his villa at Tusculum; from there he sets out by cross-country roads for his villa at Formiae, meaning to take ship from Caieta. Several times he put out to sea from there, but now contrary winds drove him back, now he himself could not bear the tossing of the ship as the dark swell rolled it; and at last weariness both of flight and of life took hold of him. Going back to his villa further inland, which is a little more than a mile from the sea, he said: "I shall die in the country I have so often saved." It is well established that his slaves were ready to fight bravely and loyally, and that he ordered them to set down the litter and to bear calmly whatever unjust fate compelled. As he leaned out of the litter and offered his neck without flinching, his head was cut off. Nor was that enough for the brutish cruelty of the soldiers: they cut off his hands as well, taunting them for having written against Antony. So the head was brought back to Antony, and on his orders it was placed between the two hands on the Rostra, where he had been heard as consul, where he had been heard so often as a former consul, where that very year he had been heard speaking against Antony with such admiration for his eloquence as no human voice had ever received. People could hardly raise their eyes, wet with tears, to look upon the remains of their murdered fellow citizen.

[22] He lived sixty-three years, so that, had there been no violence, his death could not even have seemed premature. His genius was fortunate both in its works and in the rewards of its works; he himself long enjoyed prosperous fortune; but in that long run of good fortune he was struck from time to time by great wounds: exile, the ruin of the party he had stood for, and the end, so sad and bitter, of his beloved daughter. Of all his misfortunes he bore none as a man should, except his death, which, to anyone who judges truly, may seem less undeserved, because he suffered nothing crueller at the hands of a victorious enemy than he would himself have done to the defeated, had he been master of the same fortune. Yet if one weighs his virtues against his faults, he was a great and memorable man, and one whose praises would need a Cicero to tell them in full.

**Analysis:**

These two paragraphs are the closest we can get to the lost Livy, and they show him at his best. The narrative of the death is built in the classic Livian way: a sequence of pluperfects and perfects that move the story forward (*cesserat*, *fugerat*, *proficiscitur*, a historic present, *cepit*), a *cum* clause of circumstance with a balanced *modo... modo* ("now... now"), and then, at the moment of decision, Cicero's only words, direct speech, short and solemn: *moriar in patria saepe servata*. The death itself is told with brutal economy, a pair of present participles in the dative (*prominenti... praebentique*, "to him as he leaned out and offered") and a passive verb: *caput praecisum est*. Then the scene moves to the Rostra in Rome, and the triple anaphora *ubi... ubi... ubi* turns the place of the display into a summary of his whole career. It is one of the most powerful pieces of prose to survive from the age of Augustus.

The obituary is even more interesting, because it is a judgement, and an honest one. Seneca the Elder, who quotes it, explains that Thucydides had done this once or twice and Sallust for a very few people, but that Livy generously gave every great man such a closing summary, a kind of funeral speech; and he calls Livy "by nature the fairest judge of all great talents". The judgement is fair, but it is not flattering. Livy praises Cicero's genius and his long good fortune, lists the blows he suffered, and then says something sharp: of all his misfortunes, Cicero bore none of them *ut viro dignum erat*, "as a man should", except his death. Anyone who has read Cicero's letters from exile, full of despair and self-pity, will know what Livy means. Then, even more sharply: his death was less undeserved than it might seem, because Antony did nothing to him that Cicero would not have done to Antony if he had won. And then, finally, the balance: *si quis tamen virtutibus vitia pensarit, vir magnus ac memorabilis fuit*. The last clause is a graceful compliment: only Cicero himself would have been eloquent enough to praise Cicero properly.

Grammar to watch: *non magis... quam*, "no more... than"; the future participle of intention *conscensurus*, "intending to board"; the perfect subjunctive *pensarit* (for *pensaverit*) in the condition; *compos* with the genitive, "master of"; and *opus fuerit* with the ablative, *Cicerone laudatore*, "there would have been need of Cicero as eulogist". One more note on the text. The Latin Library prints *filiae amatae exitu tam tristi atque acerbo*, which makes "the end, so sad and bitter" refer to Cicero's daughter, Tullia, who died in 45 BC after childbirth. Other editions read *filiae morte, exitu tam tristi atque acerbo*, in which "an end so sad and bitter" would be Cicero's own death, as the last of the blows. The translation follows the text as printed here.

## Legacy and Impact

Livy's legacy is legendary. Within a generation of his death he had become *the* history of Rome: later Romans did not need to write the story of the Republic again, because Livy had done it, and almost every later account, Greek or Latin, depends on him. Quintilian ranked him with Herodotus; schoolboys read him; epitomes and summaries were made so that those who could not face one hundred and forty-two books could at least know what was in them. The poet Martial wrote a joke inscription for a pocket edition of "huge Livy, whom my whole library cannot hold", squeezed into a few small sheets of parchment.

That size is also why most of him is lost. In late antiquity and the Middle Ages, a work of one hundred and forty-two long books was simply too big to keep copying in full. It circulated in separate blocks of ten books, the decades, and some decades were copied while others were not, until, one by one, they disappeared. The monks and scholars who preserved classical literature saved what they could. We have about a quarter of the work: Books I-X and XXI-XLV, thirty-five books, which is still comfortably longer than all of Caesar, Sallust and Tacitus put together, and quite enough to understand his greatness. Every so often a scrap of the rest reappears: a page of Book XCI was found in a Vatican palimpsest in the eighteenth century. The dream of finding a complete Livy has haunted scholars for six centuries.

The Renaissance made him a hero. Dante, in the *Inferno*, calls him "Livy, who does not err". Petrarch wrote him a letter, complaining that he had been born in the wrong age, and personally assembled one of the most complete manuscripts of the surviving decades. And in the early 1500s Niccolò Machiavelli wrote his *Discourses on the First Ten Books of Titus Livy*, using the early Roman Republic as described by Livy to work out how free states are founded, how they decline and how they can be saved. Through Machiavelli, Livy's Republic became one of the models of modern political thought, read by the founders of the English and American republics. And his stories, Romulus and Remus, the rape of the Sabine women, Horatius at the bridge, Lucretia, Cincinnatus at his plough, Hannibal crossing the Alps, became the shared imagery of European painting, drama and opera for centuries.

## Final Rating

**Tier: B (Difficult)**

Livy is prose, with standard vocabulary and few literary allusions, so he is in a different league from Virgil and Horace. But he is not easy. His long periods, packed with participles, ablatives absolute and subordinate clauses, demand that the reader hold the whole structure in mind until the main verb arrives; his speeches in indirect discourse can run for paragraphs; the historic infinitive, the poetic colouring of the first decade and his love of *variatio* all catch out the unwary. And then there is the sheer patience required: Livy rewards the reader who keeps going, and punishes the one who loses concentration halfway through a sentence. He is clearly harder than Caesar and Nepos, and at the level of Cicero's speeches: demanding but approachable with preparation, which is exactly what a B means. The later decades, with their long military narratives, sit closer to C; the first decade and the big speeches touch the upper range of B.

# ALBIUS TIBULLUS (c. 55 -- 19 BC)

## Biography

Tibullus is the least famous of the great Augustan poets today, and the one the Romans themselves considered the most elegant. He wrote love elegies, like Gallus before him and Propertius and Ovid after him, but his are unlike anyone else's: dreamy, gentle and melancholy, full of the countryside, of simple rustic gods, of peace, and of a constant, quiet awareness of death. If Propertius is the passionate one and Ovid the clever one, Tibullus is the daydreamer: the poet who, in the middle of a love poem, drifts off into a fantasy of living on a farm with his girlfriend, and then wakes up to find that she has left him for someone richer.

**A handsome knight from the Latin countryside.** We know very little about his life, and his dates are approximate: he was born around 55 BC, probably on his family's estate at Pedum, in the hills between Tibur (Tivoli) and Praeneste (Palestrina), east of Rome. His family was of equestrian rank and had been rich, but lost much of its land, probably in the confiscations of the civil wars: in his first poem he calls himself the guardian of a field "once prosperous, now poor". A short ancient biography adds two details that tell us a lot: he was "remarkable for his good looks and for the care he took of his appearance". Unlike his friend Horace, who was short, fat and proud of it, Tibullus was the handsome one.

**Messalla's man.** Instead of joining the circle of Maecenas, Tibullus attached himself to Marcus Valerius Messalla Corvinus, a great aristocrat, general and orator who had fought for Brutus at Philippi and later for Octavian at Actium, and who kept a literary circle of his own, more independent of Augustus than Maecenas's. It is through Messalla that we meet Tibullus's other surprise: the poet who wrote that he hated war actually went to war. He served on Messalla's staff in his campaign in Aquitania, in southwestern Gaul, around 28-27 BC, and won military decorations, which he mentions with some pride. A little earlier, around 30 BC, he had set out with Messalla for the East, and fell ill on the way, on the island of Corcyra (Corfu), the land of the Phaeacians in the *Odyssey*. Messalla sailed on without him. Tibullus, convinced he was dying, wrote one of his most famous elegies about it (it is the first excerpt below). He recovered, and lived to complain about it.

**Horace's gloomy friend.** Horace wrote two poems to a friend called Albius, and almost everyone agrees that this is Tibullus. In one ode Horace tells him to stop grieving so much over "cruel Glycera", a girl who left him for a younger man, and to stop "chanting pitiful elegies": the most affectionate possible way of telling a friend that his poetry is a little repetitive. In a verse letter Horace imagines him wandering silently through the healthy woods of his estate at Pedum, thinking about what is worthy of a wise and good man, and reminds him that the gods have given him beauty, wealth and the art of enjoying them. And then he signs off with the line we already know: if you want a laugh, come and see me, "fat and sleek, with well-cared-for skin, a pig from Epicurus's herd". It is the perfect portrait of the two of them: the melancholy, handsome Tibullus, and Horace trying to cheer him up.

**Delia, Marathus and Nemesis.** Tibullus's two books are built around three loves. In Book I the woman is **Delia**, a Greek pseudonym, like Gallus's Lycoris; according to Apuleius her real name was Plania, and *Delia* is a learned pun on it. She is apparently married, or at any rate guarded by someone, and in one poem Tibullus teaches her all the tricks for deceiving a husband or a doorkeeper, only to discover in a later poem that she is now using the same tricks against him. Ovid, defending his own *Ars Amatoria* from exile, would later point out that Tibullus had written exactly the same kind of advice and had never been punished for it. In Book I there is also **Marathus**, a beautiful boy whose love affairs Tibullus follows with jealousy, and on whose behalf the rustic god Priapus delivers a comic lecture on the art of courtship. In Book II the woman is **Nemesis**, whose name means "Retribution", and who lives up to it: she is greedy, and wants presents, and Tibullus, the poet of simple country poverty, ends up declaring that he would even sell his ancestral estate to keep her happy.

**Death with Virgil.** He died young, in 19 BC, the same year as Virgil. A contemporary poet, Domitius Marsus, wrote an epitaph for the two of them: "Unjust death sent you too, Tibullus, as a young man, to be Virgil's companion in the Elysian fields." Ovid, who was barely twenty and never had the chance to become his friend, wrote a long lament for him, in which both of his mistresses turn up at the funeral. Delia says that he was happier while he loved her; Nemesis answers that it was her hand he held as he died. Years later, Ovid remembered with regret that "greedy fate did not give Tibullus time for my friendship". Quintilian, a century after his death, still thought him the most polished and elegant of the Roman elegists.

## Most Important Works

- **Elegiae, Book I** (c. 27-26 BC) -- ten elegies, mostly about Delia and the boy Marathus, but also about Messalla's triumph, the countryside and death. The first poem sets out Tibullus's whole programme: let others pile up gold and fight wars; he wants a small farm, a quiet life, the old gods of the countryside, and Delia beside him at the end. [Complete]
- **Elegiae, Book II** (c. 19 BC) -- six elegies, published shortly before or after his death: a country festival, a birthday poem, a celebration of Messalla's son, and the poems for Nemesis, the greedy mistress. [Complete]
- **The Corpus Tibullianum, Book III** -- a collection of poems by other members of Messalla's circle, transmitted with Tibullus's two books and long attributed to him: the elegies of a poet calling himself Lygdamus, a long and rather clumsy *Panegyric of Messalla* in hexameters, a cycle of poems about the love of Sulpicia for Cerinthus, and Sulpicia's own six short elegies (which have their own entry in this chapter). Many editions split this material into Books III and IV. [Complete; mostly not by Tibullus]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Tibullus is the purest, smoothest and least showy of the elegists. His vocabulary is clean and classical, with very few Greek words and very few rare ones. He almost never uses mythology, which sets him apart from Propertius and Ovid: where they compare their mistresses to half the heroines of Greek legend, Tibullus talks about vines, sheep, rustic altars, the household gods and the harvest. His sentences are mostly short and clear, and his elegiac couplets are regular and musical: Quintilian's *tersus atque elegans*, "polished and elegant", is exactly right. **Lexicon: LOW to MEDIUM; Allusion density: LOW.**

The difficulty of Tibullus lies elsewhere, in the way his poems move. They do not follow a logical argument. They drift, from one image to another, by association, like a daydream: a poem that begins with the poet sick on Corfu moves to his memories of leaving Delia, to the Golden Age, to his own death, to the Elysian Fields, to Tartarus, and finally to a fantasy of coming home unexpectedly and finding Delia waiting for him. Each sentence is clear; the problem is understanding why it follows the previous one. Add the usual poetic word order of the couplet, some enjambment, and the conventions of the elegiac genre (the locked door, the guardian, the rival, the lover as slave), and Tibullus is not trivial. But compared with what comes next, Propertius, he is a gentle walk in the country. **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM; Stylistic figures: MEDIUM.**

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Elegiae I.3.1-10 and 53-58 -- Sick on Corfu

**Source:** Tibullus, *Elegiae*, Book I, poem 3, lines 1-10 and 53-58, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/tibullus1.html). The Latin Library capitalises the first word of every line, as many printed editions do; this has been kept.

**Context:** Around 30 BC, Messalla set off for the East, and Tibullus went with him, but fell ill on Corcyra (Corfu), which he calls Phaeacia, after the island of King Alcinous in the *Odyssey*. Messalla and his staff sailed on. Alone and feverish, Tibullus writes to his patron, and then to Death itself, convinced that he is about to die far from home. In the lines between the two parts of the excerpt, he remembers how reluctant he was to leave Delia in Rome, and confesses that he invented every excuse to delay the departure: bad omens, birds, and even "the sacred day of Saturn", one of the earliest references in Latin literature to the Jewish Sabbath. Then he imagines his own death and writes his own epitaph.

**Latin Text:**

> Ibitis Aegaeas sine me, Messalla, per undas,
> O utinam memores ipse cohorsque mei.
> Me tenet ignotis aegrum Phaeacia terris,
> Abstineas avidas, Mors, modo, nigra, manus.
> Abstineas, Mors atra, precor: non hic mihi mater
> Quae legat in maestos ossa perusta sinus,
> Non soror, Assyrios cineri quae dedat odores
> Et fleat effusis ante sepulcra comis,
> Delia non usquam; quae me cum mitteret urbe,
> Dicitur ante omnes consuluisse deos.
>
> ...
>
> Quodsi fatales iam nunc explevimus annos,
> Fac lapis inscriptis stet super ossa notis:
> 'Hic iacet inmiti consumptus morte Tibullus,
> Messallam terra dum sequiturque mari.'
> Sed me, quod facilis tenero sum semper Amori,
> Ipsa Venus campos ducet in Elysios.

**English Translation:**

You will sail across the Aegean waves without me, Messalla:
oh, if only you and your staff would remember me!
Phaeacia holds me here, sick, in an unknown land.
Keep your greedy hands away from me, black Death, I beg you;
keep them away, dark Death, I pray. I have no mother here
to gather my burnt bones into her grieving lap,
no sister to pour Assyrian perfumes on my ashes
and weep before my tomb with her hair let loose,
and nowhere is Delia, who, when she sent me off from the city,
is said to have consulted every god first.

...

But if I have already completed the years that fate allotted me,
let a stone stand over my bones, with these words carved on it:
"Here lies Tibullus, taken by cruel death
while he was following Messalla over land and sea."
But because I have always been an easy prey for tender Love,
Venus herself will lead me to the Elysian Fields.

**Analysis:**

The opening is a send-off poem in reverse, like Horace's Epode 10, but in a completely different key: here it is the poet who stays behind, and the one in danger. The first line already sets the tone: *Ibitis... sine me*, "you will go without me", with *sine me* placed right in the middle, between the Aegean and the waves. Then comes the plea to Death, repeated twice with anaphora, *Abstineas... abstineas*, a jussive subjunctive softened into a prayer by *modo* and *precor*. The image of Death with "greedy hands" (*avidas... manus*) is typical Tibullus: concrete, physical, almost a figure from a painting.

Then the heart of the passage: what frightens him is not death itself but dying alone, without the women who would perform his funeral rites. A Roman funeral needed family: a mother to gather the bones from the pyre, a sister to pour perfumes and mourn with her hair unbound. The list builds up in a series of negatives, *non hic mihi mater... non soror... Delia non usquam*, each clause longer and more detailed than the last, until the climax: the woman he loves is nowhere near. Notice the relative clauses of purpose with the subjunctive (*quae legat*, *quae dedat... et fleat*): "no mother who might gather", "no sister who might give". *Assyrios... odores* is a typical poetic touch: Eastern perfumes, the most precious kind.

The second part, after the gap, is Tibullus's epitaph for himself, written in the form of a real Roman tomb inscription, beginning with the formula *Hic iacet*, "here lies". It is a moving and slightly self-pitying piece of self-promotion: the poet dies in the service of his patron, *Messallam terra dum sequiturque mari* (with *-que* attached, as poets often do, to the second word of the clause rather than the first). And then the typical Tibullan turn: because he has always been "easy" (*facilis*) for Love, Venus herself will guide him to the Elysian Fields. Love has a paradise of its own, and he has earned his place in it. Grammatically, note *fatales... annos*, "the years given by fate", *explevimus*, a poetic plural for "I have completed", and *Fac... stet*, an imperative of *facio* followed by a subjunctive without *ut*: "make a stone stand".

He did not die, of course. He went home, and wrote the poem.

### Excerpt 2: Elegiae I.10.1-14 -- Who Invented the Sword?

**Source:** Tibullus, *Elegiae*, Book I, poem 10, lines 1-14, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/tibullus1.html)

**Context:** The last poem of Book I, and one of the most unusual poems in Latin: an elegy against war. Tibullus has apparently been called up for military service, and does not want to go. He begins by asking who invented the sword, blames gold for the end of the peaceful Golden Age, and prays to the household gods of his childhood to protect him. Coming from a man who had actually served with distinction in Messalla's campaigns, and written in an age that celebrated Actium and the conquests of Augustus, it is a quietly remarkable piece of writing.

**Latin Text:**

> Quis fuit, horrendos primus qui protulit enses?
> Quam ferus et vere ferreus ille fuit!
> Tum caedes hominum generi, tum proelia nata,
> Tum brevior dirae mortis aperta via est.
> An nihil ille miser meruit, nos ad mala nostra
> Vertimus, in saevas quod dedit ille feras?
> Divitis hoc vitium est auri, nec bella fuerunt,
> Faginus adstabat cum scyphus ante dapes.
> Non arces, non vallus erat, somnumque petebat
> Securus sparsas dux gregis inter oves.
> Tunc mihi vita foret, volgi nec tristia nossem
> Arma nec audissem corde micante tubam;
> Nunc ad bella trahor, et iam quis forsitan hostis
> Haesura in nostro tela gerit latere.

**English Translation:**

Who was the first to bring out the horrifying sword?
How savage he was, how truly made of iron!
Then slaughter was born for the human race, then battles,
then a shorter road was opened to grim death.
Or is that poor wretch not to blame at all, and was it we who turned to our own ruin
what he gave us to use against savage beasts?
This is the fault of rich gold: there were no wars
while a beechwood cup still stood beside the meal.
There were no citadels, no palisades, and the leader of the flock
looked for sleep without a care among his scattered sheep.
That was the time I should have lived, and never known
the grim weapons of the crowd, nor heard the trumpet with a pounding heart.
Now I am being dragged off to war, and perhaps some enemy
is already carrying the weapon that will lodge in my side.

**Analysis:**

The poem opens with a question and a pun. *Quam ferus et vere ferreus ille fuit!*: "how savage, and truly made of iron, he was". *Ferus* ("wild, savage") and *ferreus* ("made of iron") sound almost the same, and the man who invented iron weapons is himself iron-hearted. The pun also hides a myth, without naming it: in the ancient story of the ages of the world, the age of iron was the last and worst, the age of war and greed. Tibullus never says so; he lets the adjective do the work.

The next lines build on the same idea with anaphora (*Tum... tum... tum*, "then... then... then"), and then the poet corrects himself: perhaps the inventor is innocent, and it is we who turned against each other a weapon meant for wild animals. *Nos ad mala nostra / Vertimus*: the guilt moves from one man to all of us. The real culprit is named in line 7: *Divitis hoc vitium est auri*, "this is the fault of rich gold". In the old days, when people drank from a simple beechwood cup (*faginus... scyphus*), there were no wars, no fortresses, and the shepherd slept peacefully among his sheep. This is the Tibullan ideal in four lines: poverty, simplicity and peace, the countryside against the city, the beechwood cup against gold.

Then comes the turn from the general to the personal, which is the emotional key of the poem. *Tunc mihi vita foret*, "that was when I should have lived" (an imperfect subjunctive of wish, *foret* for *esset*), followed by two pluperfect subjunctives of unreal past condition, *nossem* (for *novissem*) and *audissem*: "I would not have known, I would not have heard". And then, with a brutal present tense, reality: *Nunc ad bella trahor*, "now I am being dragged off to war". The last couplet is one of the most striking images in Augustan poetry: somewhere, at this very moment, an enemy is already carrying the weapon that will stick in the poet's side. *Haesura* is a future participle, "destined to stick", placed at the head of the line, so that the weapon is in the reader's mind before the enemy who carries it. Note also *volgi*, the old spelling of *vulgi*, and *quis*, here the indefinite "some", as it often is after *si*, *nisi* and *ne*, and here after *forsitan*.

## Legacy and Impact

In antiquity Tibullus was admired more than he is today. Quintilian ranked him first among the Roman elegists, "most polished and elegant", ahead of Propertius and Ovid. Ovid himself, who loved him, placed him in the line of the great elegists between Gallus and Propertius, mourned him in one of his finest poems, and saw him as the model of the gentle, refined love poet. His influence on the countryside of later Latin poetry, on its rustic festivals, its simple gods and its dream of peace far from war and wealth, was deep and lasting.

Since then, he has suffered the fate of the quiet. In the Renaissance and in the eighteenth century, readers who found Propertius too difficult and Ovid too cynical loved Tibullus for his purity and his gentle melancholy, and he was imitated by poets writing in Latin and in the modern languages. But the nineteenth and twentieth centuries preferred Propertius's passion and Ovid's wit, and Tibullus slipped into the background, read mostly by specialists. He also has the strange distinction of being the poet whose name protected someone else's work: it is only because the poems of Sulpicia were copied as part of the *Corpus Tibullianum* that the only surviving poems by a Roman woman reached us at all.

He deserves better. His anti-war elegy, written in the age of Augustus's triumphs, still reads as one of the most humane poems of antiquity, and his dream of the small farm and the beechwood cup is the ancestor of every poem that has ever preferred a quiet life to a glorious one.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

Tibullus is the most accessible of the Augustan poets. His vocabulary is pure and classical, his sentences are mostly short and clear, he uses almost no mythology, and his couplets are smooth and regular. He is still verse, with the normal poetic word order and the conventions of the elegiac genre, and his habit of drifting from one image to another by association means that the logic of a whole poem can be harder to follow than any single sentence in it. But he never plays games with the reader the way Horace does, and he never buries him under learned allusions the way Propertius will. A solid C: the right place to begin with Latin elegy, before Ovid, and long before Propertius.

# SULPICIA (fl. late I century BC)

## Biography

Sulpicia is a small entry with an enormous distinction: she is almost certainly the only woman of classical Rome whose poetry has survived, and the only female elegist we know. Hundreds of Roman men wrote poetry about women; Sulpicia is the one woman whose own voice comes back to us. We have six short poems by her, about forty lines in all, tucked away in the third book of the collection that goes under the name of Tibullus. That is the whole of her surviving work, and very probably the whole of what she ever published. It is enough to show that she was a poet worth reading, and a woman worth knowing.

**A girl from the best family in Rome.** Unlike most elegists, Sulpicia tells us exactly who she is, and in one poem signs herself, proudly, *Servi filia Sulpicia*, "Sulpicia, daughter of Servius". Her father was Servius Sulpicius Rufus, son of the great jurist of the same name who had been consul in 51 BC and a friend of Cicero (it was this grandfather who wrote Cicero one of the most famous letters of consolation in Latin when his daughter Tullia died). Her mother was Valeria, the sister of Messalla Corvinus, Tibullus's patron. Her father seems to have died early, and her uncle Messalla became her guardian. So Sulpicia grew up at the centre of the most brilliant literary circle in Augustan Rome, surrounded by poets, and she decided to become one herself.

**The overprotective uncle.** Two of her poems are about a birthday, and they are the funniest pages she wrote. In the first she complains that her "hateful birthday" is coming, and that she is going to have to spend it in the boring countryside near Arretium (Arezzo), on her uncle's estate, with a cold river, and without Cerinthus. "What is sweeter than the city? Is a country villa a fit place for a girl?" And then, to Messalla directly: "You are far too anxious about me, uncle. Calm down. Journeys are not always well timed." In the second poem, the trip has been cancelled: "Have you heard? That dreary journey has been lifted from your girl's mind. Now she can be in Rome for your birthday." Every teenager who has ever been dragged to a relative's house on a holiday will recognise the tone.

**Cerinthus.** The man she loves is called Cerinthus, a Greek pseudonym, as elegiac convention required, which may hide a pun on *kerinthos*, the Greek word for "bee-bread", the food bees make from pollen. Who he really was, nobody knows. Her poems to him run through the whole emotional range of elegy in a few lines: the triumphant declaration that love has finally arrived and she does not care who knows it (the first excerpt); jealousy, when Cerinthus apparently prefers a common slave girl "weighed down with her wool basket" to the daughter of Servius; illness, when she lies sick with fever and asks whether he even cares; and regret, when she apologises for having left him alone one night because she wanted to hide how much she felt (the second excerpt). In elegy the man speaks and the woman is spoken about. Sulpicia turns the genre round: she is the one who loves, suffers, burns and writes, and Cerinthus is the silent beloved.

**A scandal, deliberately.** All of this was more daring than it looks. A Roman aristocratic woman was expected above all to have *pudicitia*, sexual modesty, and her reputation was her family's honour. Sulpicia announces in her very first poem that she is glad to have "sinned", and that she is tired of composing her face to protect her reputation. That is not the voice of a woman who expected her poems to stay in a drawer.

**A cycle about her.** Just before her own six poems, the *Corpus Tibullianum* contains five more poems about Sulpicia and Cerinthus, written by someone else: a poet of Messalla's circle, perhaps even Tibullus himself, who tells the same love story from the outside, in a more conventional and polished style. Scholars call him the *amicus Sulpiciae*, "Sulpicia's friend". Reading the two side by side is the best way to see what is special about her: his poems are elegant; hers are alive.

A word of honesty: a few modern scholars have argued that Sulpicia's poems were in fact written by a man, impersonating a woman. Most reject the idea, and the poems themselves, with their very personal details of family, birthdays and names, make it hard to believe. But because the question has been raised, it is fair to say "almost certainly" rather than "certainly".

## Most Important Works

- **Six elegies** (late I century BC), preserved as poems 13-18 of the third book of the *Corpus Tibullianum* (poems IV.7-12 in editions that divide the book in two): the declaration of love (13), the unwanted birthday in the country (14), the cancelled trip (15), the rival (16), the fever (17) and the apology (18). About forty lines in all, the shortest poems in this entire project and almost certainly all she wrote. [Complete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Sulpicia's poems are very short, and that is the best thing about them for a student: a whole poem can be translated in an afternoon, with all the satisfaction of having finished a complete work. They are written in elegiac couplets, with ordinary vocabulary, almost no mythology, and none of the long allusive digressions of Propertius. **Lexicon: LOW; Allusion density: LOW.**

But "short" does not mean "simple". Sulpicia's Latin is famously compressed, and for a long time it was dismissed as clumsy: nineteenth-century scholars, assuming that a woman could not write as well as a man, called her style awkward and amateurish. Today most readers see the compression as deliberate. She packs a great deal into very few words, uses unusual constructions (double negatives, comparisons built inside comparisons, abstract nouns in the dative), and likes to delay the decisive word until the end of a long sentence. Her last poem is a single sentence of six lines in which the actual confession only arrives in the fifth line. Line for line, she can be harder than Tibullus. **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM to HIGH; Stylistic figures: MEDIUM.**

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Corpus Tibullianum III.13 -- Love Has Come at Last

**Source:** Sulpicia, *Corpus Tibullianum* III.13, complete, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/tibullus3.html), where her poems are printed under the heading *Sulpiciae Elegidia*, "the little elegies of Sulpicia". The text writes consonantal *v* as *u* (*uenit*, *uelim*), which has been kept.

**Context:** The first of Sulpicia's poems, and her manifesto. Love has finally arrived, and instead of hiding it, as a well-brought-up Roman girl should, she announces it to the world. The poem works like a prayer of thanksgiving: she prayed to Venus with her poems, Venus answered, and now Sulpicia pays her debt by telling everyone. Behind it stands the most famous poem by a woman in all of Greek literature, Sappho's prayer to Aphrodite, in which the goddess comes down from heaven to help the poet in love.

**Latin Text:**

> Tandem uenit amor, qualem texisse pudori
> quam nudasse alicui sit mihi fama magis.
> Exorata meis illum Cytherea Camenis
> attulit in nostrum deposuitque sinum.
> Exsoluit promissa Venus: mea gaudia narret,
> dicetur si quis non habuisse sua.
> Non ego signatis quicquam mandare tabellis,
> ne legat id nemo quam meus ante, uelim,
> sed peccasse iuuat, uultus componere famae
> taedet: cum digno digna fuisse ferar.

**English Translation:**

At last love has come, and of such a kind that a rumour I had hidden it
would bring me more shame than a rumour I had laid it bare to anyone.
Won over by my Muses, the goddess of Cythera
brought him to me and laid him in my arms.
Venus has kept her promises: let my joys be told
by anyone who is said to have had no joys of their own.
I would not want to entrust anything to sealed tablets,
so that no one could read it before my love does;
no, I am glad to have sinned, and sick of composing my face
for my reputation: let them say I was a worthy woman with a worthy man.

**Analysis:**

The first couplet is the most discussed sentence Sulpicia wrote, and a perfect illustration of her compression. *Qualem texisse pudori / quam nudasse alicui sit mihi fama magis*: literally, "such that the report of having covered it would be to me a cause of shame more than of having bared it to someone". The pieces are *qualem... sit*, a relative clause of result ("such that"); *pudori*, a dative of purpose, "a cause of shame"; and two perfect infinitives, *texisse* and *nudasse* (for *nudavisse*), compared with *magis... quam*. Put together, it means: hiding this love would be more shameful than revealing it. The images are physical: *tegere* is to cover, *nudare* is to strip naked. It is a startling way for a senator's niece to begin.

The second couplet is the story of the prayer: *Cytherea* is Venus (from the island of Cythera, where she came ashore after her birth from the sea), and *Camenae* are the Italian Muses, so "my Muses" are Sulpicia's own poems. Venus was *exorata*, "won over by entreaty", and she brought Cerinthus *in nostrum... sinum*, into the poet's lap or embrace (with a poetic plural, *nostrum* for *meum*). The third couplet turns the prayer into a public thanksgiving: Venus has "paid off" her promises (*exsoluit*, the technical word for fulfilling a vow), so Sulpicia will publish her joy, and anyone who has none of their own can tell it for her. Notice the order: *mea gaudia narret, / dicetur si quis...*, with the *si* clause after the main verb and *si quis* split by *dicetur*.

The fourth couplet is a famous puzzle. *Non ego signatis quicquam mandare tabellis, / ne legat id nemo quam meus ante, uelim*: "I would not want to entrust anything to sealed tablets, so that nobody might read it before my man". The double negative, *ne... nemo*, is the difficulty: she is refusing to write love letters on sealed wax tablets meant only for Cerinthus's eyes. She wants her poems to be read by everyone. And then the climax, in the last couplet: *peccasse iuuat*, "I am glad to have sinned", *uultus componere famae / taedet*, "I am sick of arranging my expression for the sake of my reputation", and finally *cum digno digna fuisse ferar*, a jussive subjunctive: "let me be spoken of as a worthy woman who was with a worthy man". The polyptoton *digno digna*, the same adjective in masculine and feminine side by side, places the two lovers on exactly the same level. In the language of elegy, where the poet is usually the slave of his mistress, this is a declaration of equality.

### Excerpt 2: Corpus Tibullianum III.18 -- The Apology

**Source:** Sulpicia, *Corpus Tibullianum* III.18, complete, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/tibullus3.html)

**Context:** The last of Sulpicia's six poems. The night before, she left Cerinthus alone, probably at a party or a dinner, because she did not want him (or the other guests) to see how much she was in love. Now she regrets it, and writes to tell him so. The whole poem is a single sentence, built like a Roman oath: "may I no longer be... if I have ever done anything I regret more than this".

**Latin Text:**

> Ne tibi sim, mea lux, aeque iam feruida cura
> ac uideor paucos ante fuisse dies,
> si quicquam tota commisi stulta iuuenta
> cuius me fatear paenituisse magis,
> hesterna quam te solum quod nocte reliqui,
> ardorem cupiens dissimulare meum.

**English Translation:**

May I no longer be, my light, as burning a passion for you
as I seem to have been just a few days ago,
if, in all my foolish youth, I have ever done anything
that I would confess I regretted more
than leaving you alone last night,
because I wanted to hide my own desire.

**Analysis:**

This is Sulpicia's compression at its most extreme, and a superb exercise in following a Latin sentence to the end. The structure is an oath, or a curse on oneself: *Ne tibi sim... si quicquam commisi*, "may I not be... if I have done anything". The main clause comes first, a negative wish in the subjunctive (*ne... sim*), with a comparison inside it: *aeque... feruida cura / ac uideor... fuisse*, "as burning a passion (*cura*, literally 'care', the standard elegiac word for the beloved) as I seem to have been". Then the condition, *si quicquam... commisi*, then a relative clause of characteristic inside the condition, *cuius me fatear paenituisse magis*, built on the impersonal verb *paenitet*, which takes the accusative of the person who regrets (*me*) and the genitive of the thing regretted (*cuius*). And only then, in the fifth line, the second half of the comparison, *magis... quam*: what she regrets most is *quod te solum hesterna nocte reliqui*, "that I left you alone last night". The confession is held back until the very end of the sentence, exactly as she held back her feelings the night before.

The final line gives the reason: *ardorem cupiens dissimulare meum*, "wanting to hide my passion". *Ardor*, "burning", picks up *feruida*, "boiling", from the first line, so the poem begins and ends in fire. Notice also the tender address *mea lux*, "my light", a common Roman endearment, and *stulta*, "foolish", attached to Sulpicia herself: she is the one who has been silly. Few poems in Latin say so much, so precisely, in six lines.

## Legacy and Impact

For most of their history, Sulpicia's poems were read as part of Tibullus, and for a long time they were barely noticed. When nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars did notice them, they often treated them with condescension, as the charming but unskilled effusions of a young girl, whose Latin was too awkward to be the work of a real poet. In the last fifty years that view has been turned upside down. Sulpicia is now read as a sophisticated poet who knew the conventions of elegy perfectly and bent them to her own purpose, and her poems are among the most studied texts in the whole *Corpus Tibullianum*.

Her real importance is simple and immense. She is the only woman of classical Rome whose poetry has survived in any quantity, and the only female love elegist we know. Other Roman women wrote: a later Sulpicia, in the time of Domitian, wrote love poems to her husband, of which two lines survive; a few letters attributed to Cornelia, the mother of the Gracchi, are quoted by Nepos; the speech of Hortensia against a tax on women survives only in a Greek paraphrase. Only in late antiquity do we have complete works by Latin-writing women: the prison diary of the martyr Perpetua, the travel journal of the pilgrim Egeria, and Proba's retelling of the Bible in lines borrowed from Virgil. In all the centuries of classical Latin poetry, Sulpicia's forty lines are the one place where a woman speaks for herself, about her own love, in her own words. They survived only because they were copied next to the poems of a man. That is reason enough to read every one of them.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

Sulpicia's poems are short, complete and written in plain vocabulary, with almost no mythology and no learned digressions: with all forty lines in front of you, you can finish her entire works, which is a rare pleasure. That is what pulls her towards D. But her syntax is genuinely compressed, with double negatives, comparisons inside comparisons, abstract datives and decisive words held back until the end of a long sentence, and line for line she can be harder than Tibullus. So C, at the easy end of it: the shortest exam in the project, but not a free pass.

# SEXTUS PROPERTIUS (c. 50 -- after 16 BC)

## Biography

If Tibullus is the daydreamer of Roman elegy, Propertius is its storm. He is passionate, obsessive, jealous, self-pitying, funny, learned, morbid, proud and completely unpredictable, often all within the same poem. He spent five years writing about one woman, Cynthia, and he made her the most vivid woman in Latin poetry: we see her asleep, drunk, furious, sick, beautiful, unfaithful, dead and, in his most extraordinary poem, back from the dead to tell him off. He is also, by common consent, the most difficult of the Roman elegists: a poet so dense, so allusive and so badly preserved that even professional Latinists argue over what he is saying in half his lines. And he is worth every bit of the effort.

**The Umbrian.** Sextus Propertius was born around 50 BC, perhaps a little later, at Asisium, modern Assisi, in Umbria: the same hill town that would later give the world Saint Francis. He was proud of it, and calls Umbria "the homeland of the Roman Callimachus", that is, of himself. His family were local landowners of some standing, but the civil wars hit them hard. His father died when he was young. In 41-40 BC, during the Perusine War, when Octavian besieged Lucius Antonius in nearby Perusia (Perugia), a relative of Propertius was killed and left unburied in the Etruscan hills, and the family lost much of its land in the confiscations that followed: "the grim surveyor's rod took away our well-tilled wealth". Like Virgil and Tibullus, he grew up knowing exactly what the new regime had cost.

**No lawyer.** His mother brought him to Rome and expected him to do the sensible thing: study law, speak in the Forum, start a public career. He tried. Then, as he tells it in his last book, the god Apollo himself forbade him to "thunder out words in the insane Forum", and told him to write elegies instead, adding, helpfully, that he would always be at the mercy of a girl. Apollo was right on both counts.

**Cynthia.** The girl was Cynthia, and she is the first word of his first book: *Cynthia prima suis miserum me cepit ocellis*, "Cynthia was the first to capture me, poor wretch, with her eyes". According to Apuleius her real name was Hostia; *Cynthia* is a learned pseudonym, from Mount Cynthus on Delos, the birthplace of Apollo, the god of poetry. She seems to have been a sophisticated, independent woman of the demi-monde, probably older than he was, beautiful, witty, a reader and even a writer of poetry, and, by his account, magnificently impossible. The affair lasted about five years, broke down several times, and ended for good at the close of Book III, where Propertius bids her a bitter farewell. She then returned in Book IV, twice, once as a ghost.

**Maecenas comes calling.** His first book, published around 29-28 BC and known simply as the *Cynthia* or the *Monobiblos* (the "single book"), was an immediate success, and it brought him an invitation into the circle of Maecenas. His next books are dedicated to him, and they show Propertius trying to stay himself inside the official culture of Augustan Rome. When Maecenas asks him for epic, he answers with the most famous *recusatio* in elegy: he cannot sing of Caesar's wars, because his only war is in bed. When Augustus withdraws a law that would have forced men of his class to marry, Propertius celebrates in verse, and adds the most un-Augustan line of the age: "no son of mine will ever be a soldier". He got away with it. It is a measure of how much freedom the poets of Maecenas still had in the 20s BC.

**Horace's rival?** Horace and Propertius belonged to the same circle and never once mention each other, which, for two poets so fond of naming their friends, is a silence that says a lot. Many scholars think Horace was mocking him in a letter of around 19 BC, where he describes two poets in Rome who flatter each other shamelessly at recitations: "I come away an Alcaeus by his vote; and what is he by mine? Who but Callimachus?" Propertius was the only poet in Rome who called himself the Roman Callimachus. If the jab was meant for him, it is one of the best literary insults of antiquity.

**Ovid's friend.** Ovid, much younger, was his friend and admirer, and remembered him reciting his love poems (his "fires", as Ovid puts it) to their circle. In Ovid's list of Roman elegists, Propertius is the third, between Tibullus and Ovid himself.

**Death, and a descendant.** His last datable poem was written in 16 BC; after that he disappears, and we do not know when he died. But his family went on writing. A century later, Pliny the Younger describes a recitation by a certain Passennus Paulus, a descendant of Propertius who wrote elegies in the family style. He began his first poem, as poets do, with an address to a friend in the audience: *Prisce, iubes...*, "Priscus, you bid me...". Whereupon Priscus, a distinguished jurist sitting in the front row, called out: "I most certainly do not bid you!" The audience collapsed, and the recitation never quite recovered. Pliny adds, in fairness, that Priscus was generally thought to be a little unhinged.

## Most Important Works

- **Elegiae, Book I** (*Cynthia* or *Monobiblos*, c. 29-28 BC) -- twenty-two elegies, almost all about Cynthia: falling in love, jealousy, a night spent outside her locked door, her plan to leave him for a trip abroad, and two final poems on the dead of the Perusine War. [Complete]
- **Elegiae, Book II** (c. 26-25 BC) -- the longest book, and possibly two books run together in the manuscripts: more Cynthia, the *recusatio* to Maecenas, the celebration of the withdrawn marriage law, and a famous poem on the image of Love as a winged boy. [Complete, but the text is in poor condition]
- **Elegiae, Book III** (c. 23-22 BC) -- a more varied book, in which Propertius presents himself as the Roman Callimachus, writes about politics, money, death and the Parthian expedition, and ends with his farewell to Cynthia. [Complete]
- **Elegiae, Book IV** (c. 16 BC) -- a new kind of poetry: alongside the last Cynthia poems, a series of "Roman" elegies explaining the origins of Roman cults, places and names (the god Vertumnus, the traitor Tarpeia, Hercules at the site of Rome, the temple of Jupiter Feretrius, Apollo at Actium), modelled on the *Aetia* of Callimachus, and the speech of the dead noblewoman Cornelia from her tomb, often called the "queen of elegies". [Complete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

The best way to understand Propertius's style is to compare him with Tibullus. Tibullus avoids mythology; Propertius cannot get through ten lines without it. When Cynthia falls asleep, she is like Ariadne abandoned on Naxos, Andromeda freed from her rock and a Bacchant collapsed beside a river, all in the first six lines of the poem. His model is not Tibullus but Callimachus, the most learned poet of Hellenistic Alexandria, and he fills his poems with rare myths, obscure place names, and allusions that are never explained, because explaining them would spoil the pleasure for a learned reader. For a modern student, they are an obstacle course. **Allusion density: HIGH.**

His Latin is just as demanding. Propertius compresses ruthlessly: he leaves out connectives, jumps from one idea to the next without warning, changes the addressee halfway through a poem, and lets speeches begin and end without saying who is speaking. His word order is bold even by the standards of verse, his use of cases stretches the grammar (an ablative can be means, cause, place and manner at once), he uses words in unusual senses, and he loves sudden shifts of tone, from tragedy to farce and back again in a single couplet. Where Tibullus drifts gently from image to image, Propertius leaps. **Syntactic complexity: HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH; Lexicon: MEDIUM to HIGH.**

And then there is the text. Propertius survives in a small number of late medieval manuscripts, the oldest from the twelfth century, and they are full of errors. Words are corrupted, lines are missing, couplets have migrated from one poem to another, and the boundaries between poems are often uncertain. Modern editors disagree on hundreds of readings, move couplets around, and even divide Book II into two. The great Latin scholar A. E. Housman devoted years of his life to Propertius and never finished his edition. So when you are stuck in Propertius, it is not always your fault: sometimes the Latin is genuinely broken.

How much harder is he than Tibullus? Two full tiers. Tibullus is a C, the most accessible poet of the age; Propertius sits in the upper part of A, alongside the hardest parts of Virgil, and only Horace's *Odes* are clearly worse.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Elegiae IV.7.1-18 -- Cynthia's Ghost

**Source:** Propertius, *Elegiae*, Book IV, poem 7, lines 1-18, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/prop4.html). The text writes consonantal *v* as *u* (*uisa*, *uestis*), which has been kept.

**Context:** Years after the farewell at the end of Book III, Cynthia is dead, and has only just been buried. One night, as Propertius lies awake in his cold bed, she appears to him: the same hair, the same eyes, but with her dress scorched by the pyre and her lips worn by the water of Lethe, the river of the underworld. And she is not there to forgive him. In the rest of the poem she accuses him of neglecting her funeral, accuses her slaves of having poisoned her, orders him to burn the poems he wrote about her, tells him which of her maids to reward and which to punish, and finally announces that soon she will have him all to herself again, in the underworld: "Others may have you now; soon I alone will hold you." It is one of the strangest and most powerful poems in Latin, a love poem, a ghost story and a domestic quarrel all at once. These are its first eighteen lines.

**Latin Text:**

> Sunt aliquid Manes: letum non omnia finit,
> luridaque euictos effugit umbra rogos.
> Cynthia namque meo uisa est incumbere fulcro,
> murmur ad extremae nuper humata uiae,
> cum mihi somnus ab exsequiis penderet amoris,
> et quererer lecti frigida regna mei.
> eosdem habuit secum quibus est elata capillos,
> eosdem oculos; lateri uestis adusta fuit,
> et solitum digito beryllon adederat ignis,
> summaque Lethaeus triuerat ora liquor.
> spirantisque animos et uocem misit: at illi
> pollicibus fragiles increpuere manus:
> "perfide nec cuiquam melior sperande puellae,
> in te iam uires somnus habere potest?
> iamne tibi exciderant uigilacis furta Suburae
> et mea nocturnis trita fenestra dolis?
> per quam demisso quotiens tibi fune pependi,
> alterna ueniens in tua colla manu!

**English Translation:**

The spirits of the dead are something real: death does not end everything,
and a pale ghost escapes the pyre that has overcome its body.
For Cynthia seemed to lean over the head of my bed,
Cynthia, buried only lately by the roar at the edge of the road,
when, after my love's funeral, sleep was hanging over me
and I was lamenting the cold kingdom of my bed.
She had the same hair as when she was carried out to burial,
the same eyes; her dress was burnt along her side,
the fire had eaten into the beryl ring she always wore on her finger,
and the water of Lethe had worn away the edges of her lips.
She breathed out a living spirit and a voice; but
her brittle hands cracked their knuckles at me:
"Traitor, from whom no girl should ever hope for anything better,
can sleep already have power over you?
Have you already forgotten our stolen nights in the sleepless Subura,
and my window-sill, worn smooth by our nightly tricks?
How often I let down a rope through that window and hung from it for you,
coming down hand over hand into your arms!

**Analysis:**

The poem opens with a philosophical statement, and a provocative one: *Sunt aliquid Manes*, "the spirits of the dead are something". Any Roman reader would have heard in it a contradiction of Lucretius and the Epicureans, for whom death was simply the end of everything. Propertius is about to prove them wrong with an experience of his own. Notice the emphatic word order: *Sunt* first, then *aliquid*, then *Manes*, and the denial in the second half of the line, *letum non omnia finit*.

Then the vision, and it is here that Propertius shows his genius for the concrete detail. Cynthia is not an idealised ghost but a body that has been burnt: her dress is scorched on one side, the fire has eaten into the beryl ring she always wore, and the water of Lethe, which the dead drink to forget, has worn away her lips. The repetition *eosdem... eosdem*, "the same hair... the same eyes", makes the horror more intimate: she is exactly as he remembers her, except where the fire has touched her. And then the gesture, *pollicibus fragiles increpuere manus*: her brittle hands crack, or snap their fingers, at him, a gesture of anger or contempt, and the word *fragiles* makes it horribly physical: these are the hands of a corpse.

The grammar is classic Propertius, and a good example of his compression. *Murmur ad extremae nuper humata uiae* means "recently buried by the murmur of the furthest stretch of the road": Roman tombs lined the roads out of the city, and the "murmur" is the noise of the traffic on it, a sad and slightly ironic place for a woman who loved the city's nightlife. *Cum mihi somnus ab exsequiis penderet amoris*: literally "when sleep was hanging over me from the funeral of my love", where *ab* means "after" and *amoris* stands for the beloved herself. *Quibus est elata capillos* is a relative clause with the antecedent *capillos* placed inside it, after the verb: "the same hair with which she was carried out". *Lateri*, a dative of place without a preposition, is poetic. *Beryllon* keeps its Greek accusative ending. And *sperande*, in Cynthia's first words, is a vocative gerundive: "you, who are to be hoped for", so *nec cuiquam melior sperande puellae* means "you, from whom no girl should ever hope for anything better". Then she accuses him of falling asleep so soon, and reminds him of their secret nights in the Subura, the noisy, "sleepless" district of Rome where she lived, and of the rope she used to climb down from her window into his arms. Grief, reproach, nostalgia and comedy, in eighteen lines: that is Propertius.

### Excerpt 2: Elegiae IV.8.49-70 -- Cynthia Returns

**Source:** Propertius, *Elegiae*, Book IV, poem 8, lines 49-70, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/prop4.html). The source prints *Lygdame,nil* without a space after the comma; the space has been restored.

**Context:** The very next poem in the book, and the strangest pairing in Latin poetry: in IV.7 Cynthia is a ghost, in IV.8 she is gloriously alive, and the poem is pure comedy. Cynthia has gone out of town to the festival of Juno at Lanuvium, riding in a carriage with a rich rival. Propertius decides to console himself, and invites two girls, Phyllis and Teia, to a little dinner party on the Esquiline, with his slave Lygdamus serving the wine, an Egyptian flute player, Phyllis on the castanets, and a dwarf clapping along to the music. It is a disaster: the lamps keep going out, the table collapses, he keeps throwing the worst possible score at dice, and he cannot stop thinking about Cynthia. "They were singing to a deaf man, baring their breasts to a blind man: I was at the gates of Lanuvium." And then the door opens.

**Latin Text:**

> cum subito rauci sonuerunt cardine postes,
> et leuia ad primos murmura facta Laris.
> nec mora, cum totas resupinat Cynthia ualuas,
> non operosa comis, sed furibunda decens.
> pocula mi digitos inter cecidere remissos,
> palluerantque ipso labra soluta mero.
> fulminat illa oculis et quantum femina saeuit,
> spectaclum capta nec minus urbe fuit.
> Phyllidos iratos in uultum conicit unguis:
> territa uicinas Teia clamat aquas.
> lumina sopitos turbant elata Quiritis,
> omnis et insana semita nocte sonat.
> illas direptisque comis tunicisque solutis
> excipit obscurae prima taberna uiae.
> Cynthia gaudet in exuuiis uictrixque recurrit
> et mea peruersa sauciat ora manu,
> imponitque notam collo morsuque cruentat,
> praecipueque oculos, qui meruere, ferit.
> atque ubi iam nostris lassauit bracchia plagis,
> Lygdamus ad plutei fulcra sinistra latens
> eruitur, geniumque meum protractus adorat.
> Lygdame, nil potui: tecum ego captus eram.

**English Translation:**

when suddenly the doorposts groaned on a creaking hinge,
and faint murmurs were heard at the entrance of the house.
In an instant Cynthia flings both folding doors wide open,
her hair not done, but beautiful in her fury.
The cup fell from between my slackened fingers,
and my lips, loosened by the wine itself, went pale.
Her eyes flash lightning, she rages as only a woman can,
and the sight was no less than a city being sacked.
She hurls her angry nails at Phyllis's face;
Teia, terrified, shouts "Water!" to the neighbours.
Lamps held high wake the sleeping citizens,
and the whole street echoes with the madness of the night.
The two girls, their hair torn and their tunics undone,
find refuge in the first tavern of the dark street.
Cynthia exults in her spoils and runs back in triumph,
and slashes my face with the back of her hand,
leaves her mark on my neck, draws blood with her teeth,
and above all strikes my eyes, which deserved it.
And when she had tired her arms beating me,
Lygdamus, who was hiding behind the left leg of the couch,
is dragged out, and, hauled forward, begs my guardian spirit to save him.
Lygdamus, I could do nothing: I had been taken prisoner along with you.

**Analysis:**

This is Propertius in comic mode, and the comedy is built out of epic. Cynthia's entrance is a military assault: she throws the doors open (*resupinat*, "flings back", a vivid present), her eyes "flash lightning" (*fulminat*, a verb that belongs to Jupiter), and the scene is compared to *capta... urbe*, the sack of a city, the most tragic image in all of epic poetry. Then come the casualties: Phyllis's face, Teia's screams, the neighbours woken up, the two girls fleeing half-dressed to a tavern. And then the victor returns to deal with the real enemy: *Cynthia gaudet in exuuiis*, "Cynthia exults in her spoils", *exuuiae* being the spoils stripped from a defeated enemy, here the hair and clothes torn from her rivals. The whole passage is a mock-epic battle, and Propertius plays the part of the defeated city.

The details are wonderfully precise. *Non operosa comis, sed furibunda decens*: she had not taken any trouble over her hair, but was beautiful in her fury, an oxymoron that sums up Cynthia. Teia does not scream "help" but *uicinas... clamat aquas*, "calls for water to the neighbours": the cry Romans used in case of fire, because fire was the emergency everyone would answer, a very practical piece of Roman street life. The punishment is lovingly catalogued: a backhand to the face (*peruersa... manu*), a bite on the neck, and above all blows to the eyes, *qui meruere*, "which deserved it", because they were the ones that looked at the other girls. And the last couplet turns the whole scene into a joke at the poet's expense: poor Lygdamus, hiding behind the couch, is dragged out and begs his master for protection, and the master can only answer *tecum ego captus eram*, "I had been taken prisoner along with you". The great lover is no more free than his slave.

Grammatically, look at the tenses: Propertius switches between historic presents (*resupinat*, *fulminat*, *conicit*, *clamat*, *turbant*, *excipit*, *gaudet*, *recurrit*, *sauciat*, *ferit*), perfects (*sonuerunt*, *cecidere*, *fuit*, *meruere*, *lassauit*) and pluperfects (*palluerant*, *captus eram*), which give the scene its breathless, jerky rhythm. Note the archaic or poetic forms *cecidere* and *meruere* (for *ceciderunt*, *meruerunt*), the Greek genitive *Phyllidos*, the accusatives plural in *-is* (*unguis*, *Quiritis*), the syncopated *spectaclum* for *spectaculum*, and the ablatives absolute *direptisque comis tunicisque solutis*. And then, after the excerpt, the peace treaty: Cynthia dictates her terms, fumigates the whole house to purify it, changes the sheets, and the two of them make up. In the very next lines, the poem ends with them in bed.

## Legacy and Impact

In antiquity Propertius was read, loved and imitated, especially by Ovid, whose own love elegies would be unthinkable without him. Martial wrote that Cynthia owed her fame to Propertius's poetry, but had given him no less fame in return. Then, for centuries, his difficulty and his damaged text kept him in the background. He was rediscovered by the humanists, and Petrarch read him, but he never became a school author like Virgil or Ovid: he was too hard.

The modern world has been kinder. Goethe, writing his *Roman Elegies* in the 1790s, took the Roman elegists, and Propertius above all, as his models, and made the genre live again in German. And in 1919 the American poet Ezra Pound published *Homage to Sextus Propertius*, a free and inventive adaptation that turned Propertius into a modern poet resisting empire and official culture. Classicists were horrified. One professor of Latin wrote that if Mr. Pound were a professor of Latin, there would be nothing left for him but suicide. Pound was not, and did not; his *Homage* is now considered one of the landmarks of modernist poetry, and it made Propertius famous among readers who would never open a Latin dictionary.

Today he is widely regarded as the most original of the Roman elegists, and in many ways the most modern: a poet of obsession and irony, who writes about love not as a pleasant pastime but as a kind of madness that takes over a whole life, and who laughs at himself even while he suffers.

## Final Rating

**Tier: A (Very Difficult)**

Propertius is high in almost every criterion. His mythological and Alexandrian allusions are the densest of any elegist; his syntax is compressed, abrupt and full of unannounced shifts of speaker, addressee and tone; his use of words and cases is bold; and his text is so damaged that some passages are uncertain even for experts. Compared with Tibullus, he is two full tiers harder: Tibullus drifts, Propertius leaps, and he expects you to leap with him. He stops short of S only because, unlike Horace in the *Odes*, his word order is not built as a deliberate puzzle in every line, and because his narrative poems, like the two excerpts here, carry you along with their energy. Upper A, then, and knocking on S's door.

# PUBLIUS OVIDIUS NASO -- OVID (43 BC -- 17/18 AD)

## Biography

Ovid is the youngest of the great Augustan poets, the only one born after the death of Caesar, and the only one who never knew the civil wars as an adult. That shows. Virgil and Horace had lived through the end of the Republic and wrote about peace as something won at a terrible price; Ovid grew up in the peace, took it for granted, and spent it having fun. For twenty years he was the most fashionable poet in Rome: witty, charming, prolific, endlessly inventive, adored by the public and imitated by everyone. Then, in AD 8, at the height of his fame, Augustus sent him to the end of the world, and he never came back. Few lives in literature have such a sharp turn in the middle.

**Born the year the Republic died.** Publius Ovidius Naso was born on 20 March 43 BC at Sulmo, modern Sulmona, a small town in the mountains of the Abruzzo, "rich in ice-cold streams", as he describes it. He was born in a symbolic year, and he knew it: it was the year, he says, "when both consuls fell by the same fate", that is, Hirtius and Pansa, killed at Mutina. His family was old and of equestrian rank, comfortably off. He had a brother exactly one year older, born on the same day of the year, so that, as Ovid puts it, the family celebrated a single birthday with two cakes; the brother died at twenty. *Naso* is a family cognomen meaning "nose", and Ovid uses it constantly to refer to himself, so that the most fashionable poet in Rome went through life calling himself, more or less, "Big Nose".

**The father who wanted a lawyer.** His father sent both boys to Rome to be trained in rhetoric for a political career. The brother was a natural orator; Ovid was a natural poet, and his father despaired. "Why do you try a useless pursuit?", he would say. "Even Homer left no money." Ovid tried to obey. He gave up verse and attempted to write prose, but, in the most famous line of his autobiography, "whatever I tried to write came out as verse". The elder Seneca, who heard him declaim as a young man in the schools of rhetoric, confirms it: even then, he says, Ovid's prose speeches "seemed to be nothing other than poetry let loose". Seneca also tells a wonderful story about his stubbornness. His friends once asked him to remove three lines from his poems that they considered too much; he agreed, on condition that he could name three lines that were exempt. Each side wrote its three lines secretly on a tablet, and when the tablets were opened, the lines were the same. Ovid used to say that a face is more beautiful with a mole.

**No career, thank you.** He began the career his father wanted: he held a couple of junior judicial posts, and was entitled to stand for the Senate. Then he refused. He did not have the body or the temperament for public life, he says, and he was afraid of ambition. Instead he travelled, to Athens, to the cities of Asia Minor and to Sicily, with the poet Macer, and came back to Rome to do nothing but write.

**The most fashionable man in Rome.** And Rome loved him. He began to recite his poems in public when he was barely past adolescence, with his beard shaved for the first or second time, and his first love poems, about a mistress called Corinna, made him famous almost overnight: everyone in Rome, he says, was trying to guess who Corinna was. He was friendly with Propertius, had heard Horace recite, and had seen Virgil, but only from a distance (*Vergilium vidi tantum*, "I only saw Virgil"); Tibullus died before they could become friends. By the time he was thirty he was the leading poet of the new generation, and the first generation of Romans who had never known anything but Augustus adored him. His poems were sung at parties, quoted in conversation, and even performed as ballets in the theatres. He married three times: the first two marriages ended quickly, the third, to a woman from a distinguished family, lasted the rest of his life, and she stayed loyal to him through everything that followed. He had a daughter, and grandchildren. He had a villa with gardens outside Rome. For twenty years his life was, by any standard, a success.

**A poem and a mistake.** In AD 8, at the age of fifty, he was on the island of Elba with a friend when the news reached him: Augustus had personally ordered him to leave Rome and go to Tomis, a Greek town on the western shore of the Black Sea, modern Constanța in Romania, at the very edge of the empire. There was no trial and no Senate decree: it was the emperor's own decision, and technically a *relegatio*, so Ovid kept his citizenship and his property. He tells us the reason himself, in two words: *carmen et error*, "a poem and a mistake". The poem was the *Ars Amatoria*, published some eight years earlier, a cheerful manual of seduction that sat very badly with Augustus's laws on marriage and adultery. The mistake he never explained, except to say that he had seen something he should not have seen, and to compare himself to Actaeon, who was destroyed for accidentally seeing the goddess Diana naked. It was almost certainly connected to the scandal around Augustus's granddaughter Julia, exiled for adultery that same year. Before leaving, in despair, Ovid burned his own copy of the *Metamorphoses*, which was finished but not yet revised. Fortunately, as with Virgil and the *Aeneid*, his friends already had copies.

**At the end of the world.** Tomis was everything Rome was not. It was cold: Ovid describes the Danube freezing so hard that carts could drive across it, and wine freezing so solid that it kept the shape of the jar after the jar was broken, so that people were served it not by the cup but by the chunk. It was dangerous: the town was regularly raided by the Getae and Sarmatians, horsemen who rode across the frozen river and shot poisoned arrows over the walls. And almost nobody spoke Latin. Ovid, the master of the Latin language, found himself the barbarian, unable to make himself understood: *barbarus hic ego sum, quia non intellegor ulli*, "here I am the barbarian, because nobody understands me". So he learned the local languages, Getic and Sarmatian, and eventually wrote a poem in Getic, in praise of Augustus, which he recited to the townspeople; they listened, murmured their approval, and shook their quivers full of arrows in applause.

**Begging to come home.** For the rest of his life he wrote letters in verse to Rome, to his wife, to his friends, to Augustus himself, begging to be allowed home, or at least to be moved somewhere less terrible. Augustus died in AD 14 without relenting; Tiberius did not relent either. Ovid died in Tomis in AD 17 or 18, around the age of sixty, and was buried there. Today a statue of him stands in the main square of Constanța, looking out towards the sea. And in December 2017, two thousand years late, the city council of Rome passed a motion formally revoking his banishment.

## Most Important Works

- **Amores** (*Love Poems*, c. 20 BC, second edition c. 1 BC) -- three books of love elegies, mostly about a mistress called Corinna. They originally made up five books; Ovid cut them down to three for the second edition, and opens the collection with a little epigram in which the books themselves speak: "We who were once Naso's five little books are now three: he preferred this to the old version. Even if you take no pleasure in reading us, at least the punishment will be two books lighter." The *Amores* take all the conventions of Roman elegy, from Gallus, Tibullus and Propertius (the lover as slave, the locked door, the rival, the jealous husband, the soldier of love) and play with them: Ovid is the first elegist who is clearly having more fun with the genre than he is suffering in it. In one poem he complains that he is in love with two women at once; in another he lectures a doorkeeper; in another he sends a parrot to its grave with a full funeral elegy. [Complete]
- **Heroides** (*Heroines*) -- letters in elegiac couplets written by the abandoned women of mythology to the men who left them: Penelope to Ulysses, Dido to Aeneas, Ariadne to Theseus, Medea to Jason, Sappho to Phaon, and others, plus a later set of paired letters from lovers and their replies. Ovid claimed to have invented the genre. [Complete]
- **Ars Amatoria** (*The Art of Love*, c. 2 BC -- AD 1) -- a parody of a didactic poem, in three books of elegiac couplets: Books I and II teach men how to find a girlfriend and how to keep her, and Book III, added afterwards, teaches women the same. It is written with the full apparatus of a serious instruction manual, like Virgil's *Georgics*, but its subject is where to pick up women in Rome (the theatre, the races, dinner parties, even the law courts), what to say, how to write letters, how to make your partner jealous, and how to behave when you are caught. It is very funny, very clever, and it was published while Augustus was trying to make adultery a criminal offence. It was followed by the **Remedia Amoris** (*Cures for Love*), a poem on how to fall out of love, and the **Medicamina Faciei Femineae** (*Cosmetics for the Female Face*), of which a hundred lines survive, with actual recipes for face packs. [Complete; the Medicamina fragmentary]
- **Metamorphoses** (*Transformations*, c. AD 2-8) -- Ovid's masterpiece, an epic in fifteen books of hexameters, around 12,000 lines, telling some two hundred and fifty myths of transformation, from the creation of the world out of chaos to the transformation of Julius Caesar into a star. It is not a single story but a continuous chain of stories, each running into the next, often told by characters inside other stories, so that tales are nested inside tales. Gods turn humans into trees, animals, stones, birds, springs and stars, usually because of love, jealousy, pride or punishment. Its themes are the instability of everything, the power of desire, the cruelty and caprice of the gods, the dangers of artistic talent (Arachne, Marsyas), and the way bodies change while something of the self survives. And it ends with a proud prophecy that Ovid, through this poem, will live on and be read wherever Rome's power extends. [Complete]
- **Fasti** (*The Calendar*, c. AD 2-8) -- a poem in elegiac couplets on the Roman calendar, explaining the origins of Roman festivals, rituals and anniversaries day by day. It was planned in twelve books, one for each month; only six (January to June) were written before the exile interrupted it. [Incomplete -- 6 of 12 books]
- **Tristia** (*Sorrows*, AD 9-12) and **Epistulae ex Ponto** (*Letters from the Black Sea*, AD 12-16) -- nine books in all of elegies and verse letters written from exile, describing the journey, the cold, the barbarians and his loneliness, defending his poetry, and begging for pardon. Book IV of the *Tristia* contains his autobiography in verse, the source of most of what we know about his life. [Complete]
- **Ibis** -- a long curse poem against an unnamed enemy in Rome, in the tradition of Callimachus, packed with obscure mythological punishments. [Complete]
- **Medea** -- a tragedy, praised by Quintilian and Tacitus as proof of what Ovid could have done if he had controlled his talent. [Lost]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Ovid is the easiest of the three great Augustan poets, and it is not close. The elder Seneca's verdict on his early speeches, "poetry let loose", works just as well in reverse: Ovid's poetry is the closest thing in Latin to prose written in verse. His syntax is light, logical and transparent. His sentences usually follow the natural order of thought, his clauses are short and balanced, and his couplets almost always contain a complete idea, with the hexameter making a point and the pentameter turning or completing it. Where Virgil weighs every word and Horace builds a mosaic, Ovid simply talks, beautifully and very fast. His hexameter in the *Metamorphoses* is the most fluent in Latin, full of dactyls and almost never held back by heavy spondees. **Syntactic complexity: LOW to MEDIUM.**

That does not mean he is without tricks, and the tricks are what make him a B rather than a C. He was trained in the schools of rhetoric, and it shows on every page: antithesis, paradox, wordplay, puns on names, epigrammatic endings, and a love of repeating a word in a slightly different form or sense. His learning is enormous, and the *Metamorphoses* in particular is full of rare Greek names, patronymics, epithets and allusions to myths that are told elsewhere, or not at all. He alludes constantly to Virgil, to the elegists, and to Greek poetry, often in order to parody them. And he is ironic almost everywhere, so that the translator always has to ask whether a line is serious or a joke. Quintilian, who admired him, put his finger on the problem: Ovid was "too much in love with his own talent", and could never resist one more clever turn. **Lexicon: MEDIUM; Stylistic figures: MEDIUM to HIGH; Allusion density: MEDIUM to HIGH.**

The result is a poet who is still very readable. After Virgil and Horace, Ovid feels almost easy, and that is exactly why he is worth reading: because the Latin does not stand in the way, you can actually follow what he is doing, enjoy the wit, and understand the serious things he says under the jokes, about love, about power, about art, and about how fragile everything is.

### Excerpt 1: Amores I.9.1-20 -- Every Lover Is a Soldier

**Source:** Ovid, *Amores*, Book I, poem 9, lines 1-20, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/ovid/ovid.amor1.shtml). The dash after *uterque* in the source has been printed as a colon.

**Context:** One of the great themes of Roman elegy is the *militia amoris*, the idea that love is a kind of military service. Tibullus and Propertius use it as a metaphor in passing; Ovid takes it and builds a whole poem out of it, a mock-philosophical proof, addressed to a friend called Atticus, that the lover and the soldier are exactly the same thing. Point by point, he compares them: their age, their courage, their sleepless nights, their long marches, their sieges and their spies. At the end of the poem he concludes that he himself used to be lazy, but love has made him an active man, and anyone who wants to stop being idle should fall in love. This is the first half of the argument. It is not exactly an unknown poem, but it is the best possible demonstration of how clearly Ovid's Latin is built.

**Latin Text:**

> Militat omnis amans, et habet sua castra Cupido;
> Attice, crede mihi, militat omnis amans.
> quae bello est habilis, Veneri quoque convenit aetas.
> turpe senex miles, turpe senilis amor.
> quos petiere duces animos in milite forti,
> hos petit in socio bella puella viro.
> pervigilant ambo; terra requiescit uterque:
> ille fores dominae servat, at ille ducis.
> militis officium longa est via; mitte puellam,
> strenuus exempto fine sequetur amans.
> ibit in adversos montes duplicataque nimbo
> flumina, congestas exteret ille nives,
> nec freta pressurus tumidos causabitur Euros
> aptaque verrendis sidera quaeret aquis.
> quis nisi vel miles vel amans et frigora noctis
> et denso mixtas perferet imbre nives?
> mittitur infestos alter speculator in hostes;
> in rivale oculos alter, ut hoste, tenet.
> ille graves urbes, hic durae limen amicae
> obsidet; hic portas frangit, at ille fores.

**English Translation:**

Every lover is a soldier, and Cupid has a camp of his own:
believe me, Atticus, every lover is a soldier.
The age that is fit for war is also suited to Venus:
an old soldier is a disgrace, an old lover is a disgrace.
The spirit that generals look for in a brave soldier
is what a pretty girl looks for in the man who shares her bed.
Both stay awake all night; both rest on the bare ground:
one guards his mistress's door, the other his general's.
A soldier's duty is the long march; send the girl away,
and her lover will follow her tirelessly, to the ends of the earth.
He will climb the mountains in his way, cross rivers swollen with rain,
and tramp through the drifted snow;
about to set sail, he will not plead the swollen east winds
as an excuse, or wait for stars suited to skimming the waves.
Who but a soldier or a lover will endure the cold of the night,
and snow mixed with driving rain?
One is sent out as a scout against the hostile enemy;
the other keeps his eyes on his rival, as on an enemy.
The one besieges mighty cities, the other the threshold
of a hard-hearted mistress; one breaks down gates, the other doors.

**Analysis:**

This is the clearest possible illustration of Ovid's syntax. Almost every couplet contains one complete idea, and almost every idea is built on a parallel: soldier and lover, war and Venus, *ille* and *ille*, *alter* and *alter*, *hic* and *ille*. The poem opens and closes its first couplet on the same three words, *militat omnis amans*, a frame that announces the thesis like the title of a lecture. Then each couplet adds a point of comparison, in an order so logical that a student can almost predict the next line: age (3-4), courage (5-6), sleepless nights (7-8), long marches (9-14), endurance of cold (15-16), spying (17-18) and sieges (19-20).

Look at how the parallels are built into the grammar. Line 4 is a perfect balance: *turpe senex miles, turpe senilis amor*, the same adjective twice, with the verb *est* left out both times. Lines 5-6 use a correlative pair, *quos... animos... hos*, "the spirit that... this same". Line 8 uses *ille... ille*, "the one... the other", with a single verb, *servat*, for both halves, and the genitives *dominae* and *ducis* placed in matching positions. And the last couplet is a small masterpiece of symmetry: *ille graves urbes, hic durae limen amicae / obsidet; hic portas frangit, at ille fores*. The verb *obsidet* serves both subjects, and the second half reverses the order of *ille* and *hic*, a chiasmus, ending on the most deflating word of all: *fores*, the door of a girl's house, against *portas*, the gates of a city.

There are a few things to watch. *Petiere* is the short form of *petiverunt*. *Exempto fine*, "with the limit removed", is an ablative absolute: "without end". *Pressurus*, a future participle, means "about to press the sea", that is, to sail. *Causabitur* is a deponent verb, "to plead as an excuse", from *causa*. And *verrendis... aquis*, "for the waters that are to be swept", uses a gerundive in the dative: the stars that sailors wait for are the ones suited to the voyage. But none of this gets in the way. That is the point: the Latin is so clear that the reader is free to enjoy the joke, which is that Ovid is applying the solemn Roman virtues of the soldier, discipline, endurance, courage, to a young man trying to get into his girlfriend's house.

### Excerpt 2: Ars Amatoria I.135-162 -- A Day at the Races

**Source:** Ovid, *Ars Amatoria*, Book I, lines 135-162, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/ovid/ovid.artis1.shtml). The Latin Library capitalises the first word of every line, which has been kept.

**Context:** In the first book of the *Ars Amatoria*, Ovid tells his male readers where in Rome they can find a girlfriend: in the porticoes and temples, in the theatres, at the triumphs, at dinner parties, at the seaside resort of Baiae, and here, at the chariot races in the Circus Maximus. The Circus is the perfect place, he explains, for one simple reason: in the theatre men and women sat in separate sections, but at the Circus they sat together, packed on the same benches. What follows is a step-by-step guide to making the most of it, written with the solemnity of a farming manual.

**Latin Text:**

> Nec te nobilium fugiat certamen equorum;
> Multa capax populi commoda Circus habet.
> Nil opus est digitis, per quos arcana loquaris,
> Nec tibi per nutus accipienda nota est:
> Proximus a domina, nullo prohibente, sedeto,
> Iunge tuum lateri qua potes usque latus;
> Et bene, quod cogit, si nolis, linea iungi,
> Quod tibi tangenda est lege puella loci.
> Hic tibi quaeratur socii sermonis origo,
> Et moveant primos publica verba sonos.
> Cuius equi veniant, facito, studiose, requiras:
> Nec mora, quisquis erit, cui favet illa, fave.
> At cum pompa frequens caelestibus ibit eburnis,
> Tu Veneri dominae plaude favente manu;
> Utque fit, in gremium pulvis si forte puellae
> Deciderit, digitis excutiendus erit:
> Etsi nullus erit pulvis, tamen excute nullum:
> Quaelibet officio causa sit apta tuo.
> Pallia si terra nimium demissa iacebunt,
> Collige, et inmunda sedulus effer humo;
> Protinus, officii pretium, patiente puella
> Contingent oculis crura videnda tuis.
> Respice praeterea, post vos quicumque sedebit,
> Ne premat opposito mollia terga genu.
> Parva leves capiunt animos: fuit utile multis
> Pulvinum facili composuisse manu.
> Profuit et tenui ventos movisse tabella,
> Et cava sub tenerum scamna dedisse pedem.

**English Translation:**

Don't miss the races of the noble horses, either:
the Circus, with room for a vast crowd, offers many advantages.
There is no need for fingers to talk in secret,
nor do you need to catch a signal from her nods:
sit right next to your lady, nobody will stop you,
and press your side against hers as close as you can;
and it is a good thing that the seating line forces you together, even if you didn't want it,
that by the rules of the place you simply have to touch the girl.
Here look for a way to start a friendly conversation,
and let small talk produce your first words.
Make sure you ask, very keenly, whose horses are coming in,
and without a moment's delay, whoever she supports, support him too.
And when the crowded procession of ivory gods goes by,
clap your hands enthusiastically for Lady Venus;
and if, as happens, a speck of dust should fall
on the girl's lap, you must brush it off with your fingers;
and even if there is no dust, brush off the nothing:
any excuse will do to be of service.
If her cloak is trailing too low on the ground,
gather it up and carefully lift it from the dirty earth:
straight away, as the reward for your service, if the girl allows it,
your eyes will get a glimpse of her legs.
Keep an eye, besides, on whoever is sitting behind you,
so that he doesn't press his knee into her soft back.
Little things win light hearts: many have found it useful
to plump up a cushion with a deft hand.
It has helped, too, to fan her with a thin writing tablet,
and to slip a hollow footstool under her dainty foot.

**Analysis:**

This passage is the *Ars Amatoria* in miniature: a didactic poem, with all the grammar of instruction, applied to a completely frivolous subject. Watch the verbs. Ovid uses every form of the imperative and the jussive that Latin possesses: the negative jussive subjunctive (*Nec te... fugiat*, "don't let it escape you"), the future imperative (*sedeto*, "you shall sit", *facito*, "see to it"), the ordinary imperative (*iunge*, *fave*, *plaude*, *excute*, *collige*, *effer*, *respice*), the jussive subjunctive (*quaeratur*, *moveant*, *sit*), and the gerundive of obligation (*accipienda... est*, *tangenda est*, *excutiendus erit*). It is the language of Cato's farming manual and of Virgil's *Georgics*, used to explain how to sit next to a girl.

The comedy is in the details, and the best line in the passage is one of the funniest in Latin: *Etsi nullus erit pulvis, tamen excute nullum*, "even if there is no dust, brush off the nothing". The word *nullum*, used as a noun, "nothing", turns the whole couplet into a joke about pretexts, and the next line states the moral with perfect seriousness: *Quaelibet officio causa sit apta tuo*, "let any reason be good enough for your service". Notice also how the lover is told to support whichever team she supports (*quisquis erit, cui favet illa, fave*, with the play on *favet... fave*), to applaud Venus when the statues of the gods are paraded round the track before the races, and to protect her from the knees of the man behind. The whole passage is a portrait of Roman social life, the noise, the crowd, the dust, the cushions and the footstools, seen through the eyes of a very attentive young man.

Grammatically, it is all clear and straightforward, with just a few points to watch: *capax populi*, "capable of holding the people", with an objective genitive; *Et bene, quod...*, "and it's a good thing that...", an idiom; *linea*, the line marking the seats; the conditional *si... deciderit*, a future perfect, "if it should have fallen"; *officii pretium*, in apposition, "as the price of your service"; and *videnda*, a gerundive used almost as an adjective, "legs to be seen". Note too the perfect infinitives with *fuit utile* and *profuit* (*composuisse*, *movisse*, *dedisse*), which give the advice the air of tried and tested experience.

### Excerpt 3: Metamorphoses VIII.823-842 and 875-878 -- The Hunger of Erysichthon

**Source:** Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, Book VIII, lines 823-842 and 875-878, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/ovid/ovid.met8.shtml). The story is told by a character, and the source opens line 823 with a quotation mark that is closed elsewhere; it has been left out here. The dash at the end of line 878 has been printed as a full stop.

**Context:** Erysichthon was a king of Thessaly who despised the gods. He took an axe to the sacred grove of Ceres, the goddess of grain, and cut down an ancient oak so holy that it was hung with offerings, killing the nymph who lived inside it. Ceres punished him with the cruellest possible sentence for an offence against the goddess of food: she sent Fames, Hunger personified, from the frozen wastes of Scythia, and Hunger breathed herself into him as he slept. The story is told, in Book VIII, by the river-god Achelous, to the hero Theseus. These lines describe what happens next: the dream, the waking, and the hunger that nothing can fill. In the lines between the two parts of the excerpt, Erysichthon eats his way through his whole fortune and then sells his own daughter to buy food; she escapes by changing her shape, so he sells her again and again. The last four lines are the end of the story.

**Latin Text:**

> Lenis adhuc Somnus placidis Erysicthona pennis
> mulcebat: petit ille dapes sub imagine somni,
> oraque vana movet dentemque in dente fatigat,
> exercetque cibo delusum guttur inani
> proque epulis tenues nequiquam devorat auras;
> ut vero est expulsa quies, furit ardor edendi
> perque avidas fauces incensaque viscera regnat.
> nec mora; quod pontus, quod terra, quod educat aer,
> poscit et adpositis queritur ieiunia mensis
> inque epulis epulas quaerit; quodque urbibus esse,
> quodque satis poterat populo, non sufficit uni,
> plusque cupit, quo plura suam demittit in alvum.
> utque fretum recipit de tota flumina terra
> nec satiatur aquis peregrinosque ebibit amnes,
> utque rapax ignis non umquam alimenta recusat
> innumerasque trabes cremat et, quo copia maior
> est data, plura petit turbaque voracior ipsa est:
> sic epulas omnes Erysicthonis ora profani
> accipiunt poscuntque simul. cibus omnis in illo
> causa cibi est, semperque locus fit inanis edendo.
>
> ...
>
> vis tamen illa mali postquam consumpserat omnem
> materiam derantque gravi nova pabula morbo,
> ipse suos artus lacerans divellere morsu
> coepit et infelix minuendo corpus alebat.

**English Translation:**

Gentle Sleep was still soothing Erysichthon with his peaceful wings:
in his dream he reaches for a banquet,
moves his mouth on nothing, grinds tooth against tooth,
works his throat, cheated by imaginary food,
and instead of a feast devours, in vain, the thin air.
But when rest had been driven away, a raging desire to eat
takes over his greedy jaws and his burning entrails.
At once he demands everything that the sea, the earth and the air produce,
complains of hunger with the tables laid before him,
and in the middle of a feast looks for more feasts; what could have been enough
for whole cities, enough for an entire people, is not enough for one man,
and the more he sends down into his belly, the more he wants.
And as the sea receives the rivers of the whole earth
and is never satisfied with water, and drinks up streams from distant lands,
and as a greedy fire never refuses fuel
and burns countless logs, and the more it is given
the more it wants, and grows hungrier from its very abundance:
so the mouth of impious Erysichthon receives every feast
and demands more at the same time. All food, in him,
is a cause for more food, and by eating he is always making empty space.

...

But when the violence of his disease had consumed all
his substance, and there was no new fodder for his grievous sickness,
he began to tear his own limbs apart with his teeth,
and, wretched man, fed his body by consuming it.

**Analysis:**

This is the *Metamorphoses* at its most unsettling, and a good example of how Ovid's clarity can be used for horror as well as for comedy. The dream comes first, and it is a cruel joke: Erysichthon eats in his sleep, moves his empty mouth, grinds his teeth together and swallows air. Every verb is physical (*movet*, *fatigat*, *exercet*, *devorat*), and every object is empty (*ora vana*, *cibo inani*, *tenues... auras*). Then he wakes up, and the hunger "reigns" (*regnat*) in his jaws and entrails, like a tyrant.

The central section is built on two devices Ovid loves. The first is paradox, stated in the most epigrammatic form possible: *inque epulis epulas quaerit*, "in the middle of feasts he looks for feasts", with the same word repeated in two cases (polyptoton); *plusque cupit, quo plura... demittit*, "and he wants more, the more he sends down", a correlative with *quo* and a comparative; and finally *cibus omnis in illo / causa cibi est, semperque locus fit inanis edendo*, "all food in him is the cause of food, and by eating he always makes empty space". The second device is the epic simile, and here Ovid gives us two in a row, the sea that swallows all the rivers of the world and is never full, and the fire that burns more fiercely the more wood you give it, each introduced by *utque*, "and as", and resolved by *sic*, "so". They are borrowed from the grand style of Virgil, and applied to a man who cannot stop eating. Notice also the tricolon *quod pontus, quod terra, quod educat aer*, which runs through the three elements of the world to show that nothing in creation can fill him.

The last four lines are the end of the story, and its most famous paradox. When there is nothing left to eat, Erysichthon begins to eat himself: *ipse suos artus lacerans divellere morsu / coepit*, "he began to tear his own limbs apart, ripping them with his bite". And the final line is a perfect Ovidian epigram: *infelix minuendo corpus alebat*, "the wretched man fed his body by diminishing it". The gerund *minuendo*, "by diminishing", against the verb *alebat*, "he was feeding", is a contradiction in two words. Grammatically the passage is straightforward: note *Erysicthona*, a Greek accusative; *derant*, a contracted form of *deerant*; and the historic presents in the dream (*petit*, *movet*, *fatigat*). The difficulty is not the grammar but the density of wit, which never lets up even in the most horrible moments. That, more than the transformations themselves, is the real Ovid.

## Legacy and Impact

Ovid is one of the clearest examples in this whole project of an author who had every reason to be forgotten. He was a pagan, he wrote poems that taught adultery, he mocked the gods, and he had been condemned by the emperor himself. And yet he became one of the most read, copied and imitated authors of the Christian Middle Ages. The twelfth and thirteenth centuries have even been called the *aetas Ovidiana*, the "age of Ovid". What saved him was reinterpretation. Christian readers learned to read the *Metamorphoses* as an allegory, in which each myth concealed a moral or religious truth: Phaethon falling from the sky could be read as Lucifer, Pyramus and Thisbe as Christ and the soul, and the transformations themselves as images of sin and redemption. In the fourteenth century a huge French poem, the *Ovide moralisé*, retold every myth in the *Metamorphoses* with a Christian moral attached, and a Latin version did the same for the schools. Even the *Ars Amatoria* was copied, studied and imitated, in the courtly love literature of the Middle Ages. A pagan poet of love and transformation became, against all odds, a moral teacher.

His influence on the Renaissance and on Western culture as a whole is almost impossible to exaggerate, and it comes above all from the myths of the *Metamorphoses*, which became the standard handbook of classical mythology for Europe. A few examples among hundreds:

- **Dante** places him among the four great poets of antiquity in Limbo, and in the *Inferno* challenges him directly, telling Ovid to be silent, because Dante's own transformations of sinners outdo his.
- **Chaucer** and **Boccaccio** retold his stories, and the *Heroides* shaped the medieval and Renaissance tradition of the abandoned woman's lament.
- **Shakespeare** knew him intimately, probably through Arthur Golding's English translation of 1567: the comic play of *Pyramus and Thisbe* in *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, the narrative poem *Venus and Adonis*, and the tragedy *Titus Andronicus*, in which a copy of the *Metamorphoses* is actually brought on stage, all come from Ovid, and a contemporary wrote that the "sweet witty soul of Ovid lives in mellifluous and honey-tongued Shakespeare".
- In painting and sculpture, **Titian**'s great mythological canvases for Philip II of Spain, which he called his *poesie*, **Bernini**'s *Apollo and Daphne* in the Galleria Borghese in Rome, and countless works by Botticelli, Correggio, Rubens and Velázquez are illustrations of the *Metamorphoses*.
- In music, the very first operas, including **Monteverdi**'s *Orfeo*, drew on his myths, and so did centuries of opera after them.
- In the modern world, the myth of Pygmalion became George Bernard Shaw's *Pygmalion* and then *My Fair Lady*; **Pushkin**, himself exiled to the shores of the Black Sea, wrote a poem to Ovid; and in 1997 the English poet **Ted Hughes** published *Tales from Ovid*, which became a bestseller.

And yet, as with Virgil, fame has narrowed the man. Today most people who know Ovid's name know him as "the Metamorphoses guy": the poet of people turning into trees and birds. That is a great deal, but it is far from all of him. The *Amores* are the wittiest love poems in Latin; the *Ars Amatoria* is one of the funniest poems ever written and the reason he spent the rest of his life at the end of the world; the *Fasti* are a unique guide to Roman religion; and the *Tristia* and the letters from the Black Sea are among the first great literature of exile, written by a man who had lost everything except his language, and who kept writing in it, beautifully, until the end.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: B (Difficult)**

Ovid is the easiest of the three great Augustan poets, and much the easiest to enjoy. His syntax is light and logical, his couplets make one point at a time, his hexameters run fast, and his sentences follow the natural order of thought: his poetry is the closest Latin comes to prose in verse. What makes him a B rather than a C is everything he adds on top: rhetorical wit, paradox and wordplay on every page, a constant layer of irony, a huge stock of Greek names and mythological allusions, especially in the *Metamorphoses*, and a love of showing off that, as Quintilian said, he could never control. So he sits between Tibullus and Propertius: harder than the first, much easier than the second, and still very readable. And because he is readable, his messages come through: which is exactly why he deserves to be read for much more than the transformations.

# GAIUS JULIUS HYGINUS (c. 64 BC -- 17 AD)

## Biography

Hyginus is the librarian of the Augustan Age, and the most frustrating kind of author for a project like this one: a man whose own books are almost all lost, and whose name is attached to two books he very probably did not write. But his life is a good story, and the books that go under his name are among the most useful and most charming handbooks of mythology to survive from antiquity.

**A boy brought from abroad.** Almost everything we know about the real Hyginus comes from a short entry in Suetonius's *Lives of the Grammarians*. Gaius Julius Hyginus was a freedman of Augustus, which means he had been a slave of the imperial family before being freed; his name, Gaius Julius, is simply the name of his former master. Nobody could agree where he came from: most said he was a Spaniard, but others thought he was from Alexandria, and that Julius Caesar had brought him to Rome as a boy after the capture of the city in 47 BC. It is a fitting start for a man nobody can quite pin down.

**The pupil of a walking encyclopedia.** In Rome he studied with Alexander of Miletus, a Greek scholar so learned that people called him *Polyhistor*, "Know-It-All", and some simply called him *Historia*, "History" itself. Hyginus became a scholar in the same mould: an antiquarian, a grammarian and a collector of facts about everything.

**The emperor's librarian.** Augustus put him in charge of the Palatine Library, the great public library built next to the temple of Apollo on the Palatine Hill, beside the emperor's own house, with one hall for Greek books and one for Latin. It was one of the most prestigious posts a scholar could hold in Rome: imagine being made director of the national library, and having the head of state as your neighbour. He combined it with teaching, and had many pupils.

**The man who corrected Virgil.** Hyginus wrote a commentary on Virgil, one of the very first, and he was not afraid to criticise him. Aulus Gellius reports that Hyginus pointed out historical and geographical mistakes in the *Aeneid*, and that he claimed to have consulted a copy of the *Georgics* that came "from the house and family of Virgil" himself, in order to settle a disputed reading. The first critic of the most perfect poet in Latin was the man who looked after the books.

**Ovid's friend, and a poor man.** Suetonius adds that Hyginus was a very close friend of Ovid, and of the historian Clodius Licinus, who recorded that Hyginus died very poor, and that Licinus had supported him out of his own pocket for as long as he lived. Some scholars think that one of Ovid's letters from exile, addressed to an unnamed "guardian and high priest of learned men" in Rome, asking him to look after his books, was written to Hyginus; it would be exactly the right person to ask. The librarian of the emperor, the friend of poets, the teacher of many, died penniless. Some things about academic life never change.

**The two books with his name.** All of Hyginus's genuine works are lost. What survives under the name of "Hyginus" are two handbooks: the **Fabulae**, a collection of short summaries of Greek myths, and the **De Astronomia**, a guide to the constellations and the myths behind them. Neither sounds like the work of a great Augustan scholar. Their Latin is plain and sometimes plainly wrong, and they contain mistakes that a learned man would hardly make: in the *Fabulae*, for instance, Deucalion and Pyrrha survive the great flood by escaping to Mount Etna in Sicily, rather than to Mount Parnassus. Most scholars think they were written much later, probably in the second century AD, by a schoolmaster or compiler who either was also called Hyginus or borrowed the famous name, perhaps drawing on the real Hyginus's lost works. So the books are "Hyginus" in the way that a modern textbook might carry the name of a famous editor long dead.

**A manuscript cut into pieces.** The *Fabulae* came even closer to disappearing than most ancient texts. They survived in a single manuscript, written around AD 900 in southern Italian script and kept at Freising in Bavaria. In 1535 the German humanist Jacob Micyllus used it to print the first edition. And then, its job apparently done, the manuscript was taken apart, and its parchment leaves were reused to bind other books. Today only a few scraps of it survive, recovered from the bindings of volumes in Munich and Regensburg. For almost everything in the *Fabulae*, the first printed edition is now our only witness.

## Most Important Works

- **Lost works of the real Hyginus** -- a commentary on Virgil; books on the cities of Italy, on the Trojan families from which Roman clans claimed descent, on the nature of the gods, on agriculture and on bees (which Columella still used as authorities a generation later), on famous men, and collections of moral examples. [Lost, apart from quotations]
- **Fabulae** (or **Genealogiae**) -- around 270 very short chapters retelling Greek myths, preceded by genealogies of the gods, and followed by lists, which are the most entertaining part: "those who were nursed by animals", "the most beautiful young men", "women who killed their husbands", "men who killed their fathers", "the seven wonders of the world", "the inventors of things", "the greatest friendships". It is, in effect, an ancient encyclopedia of mythology for students, and one of our most important sources for the plots of lost Greek tragedies. [Survives, through the first printed edition]
- **De Astronomia** (also called the *Poeticon Astronomicon*) -- four books on astronomy and the constellations: their shapes, their stars and above all the myths that explain them, such as how the Great Bear was once the nymph Callisto, or why the Scorpion chases Orion across the sky. [Complete]
- **De Munitionibus Castrorum** -- a technical manual on how to lay out a Roman army camp, attributed to "Hyginus" in the manuscripts but written in the imperial period by a land surveyor, and certainly not by our librarian. [Complete; spurious]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

The Latin of the *Fabulae* is the simplest in this whole chapter, and possibly in the whole project. It is a schoolbook: short sentences, a subject, a verb and an object, one event after another, with almost no ornament and no attempt at style. Most chapters follow the same pattern: "X, son of Y and Z, did this; for this reason the gods did that; therefore he was turned into the other." For a student who has just finished the grammar, it is an ideal first text: the myths are familiar, the sentences are short, and every chapter can be finished in a few minutes. **Syntactic complexity: LOW; Stylistic figures: LOW.**

There are only two catches. The first is that the Latin is not always classical: the compiler sometimes uses forms and constructions a careful Augustan writer would have avoided, such as *Iovis* as the nominative of "Jupiter" instead of *Iuppiter*, and occasionally the sentences are clumsy or the facts are wrong. A student should not take everything in Hyginus as a model of good Latin. The second is the mythological names: hundreds of Greek gods, heroes, nymphs and monsters, often in Greek forms, which the reader needs to recognise or look up. **Lexicon: LOW to MEDIUM; Allusion density: MEDIUM, but every myth is explained.**

## Latin Excerpt

### Excerpt: Fabulae 220 -- Cura

**Source:** Hyginus, *Fabulae* 220, *Cura*, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/hyginus/hyginus5.shtml)

**Context:** Most chapters of the *Fabulae* retell myths that we know from other sources. This one is different: it is a little allegorical fable that survives nowhere else in ancient literature. Cura, "Care" or "Worry" personified, shapes the first human being out of clay, and then has to argue with Jupiter and Earth about who it belongs to. It is only a few lines long, it is written in the simplest Latin, and it has had an extraordinary afterlife: the German philosopher Martin Heidegger quoted it in full in his masterpiece *Being and Time* (1927), as evidence that the ancients already understood human existence as fundamentally a matter of care.

**Latin Text:**

Cura cum quendam fluvium transiret, vidit cretosum lutum, sustulit cogitabunda et coepit fingere hominem. Dum deliberat secum quidnam fecisset, intervenit Iovis; rogat eum Cura, ut ei daret spiritum, quod facile ab Iove impetravit. Cui cum vellet Cura nomen suum imponere, Iovis prohibuit suumque nomen ei dandum esse dixit. Dum de nomine Cura et Iovis disceptarent, surrexit et Tellus suumque nomen ei imponi debere dicebat, quandoquidem corpus suum praebuisset. Sumpserunt Saturnum iudicem; quibus Saturnus aequus videtur iudicasse: "Tu, Iovis, quoniam spiritum dedisti, animam post mortem accipe; Tellus, quoniam corpus praebuit, corpus recipito. Cura quoniam prima eum finxit, quamdiu vixerit, Cura eum possideat; sed quoniam de nomine eius controversia est, homo vocetur, quoniam ex humo videtur esse factus."

**English Translation:**

When Care was crossing a certain river, she saw some clayey mud; deep in thought, she picked it up and began to shape a human being. While she was wondering what she had made, Jupiter came along. Care asked him to give it breath, and she easily obtained this from Jupiter. When Care wanted to give it her own name, Jupiter forbade it, and said that his own name should be given to it. While Care and Jupiter were arguing about the name, Earth rose up too, and kept saying that it should be given her name, since she had provided her own body for it. They took Saturn as their judge, and Saturn, it seems, judged fairly: "You, Jupiter, since you gave it breath, shall receive its soul after death; Earth, since she provided its body, shall take back its body. Since Care was the first to shape it, let Care possess it for as long as it lives; but since there is a dispute about its name, let it be called *homo*, since it seems to be made from *humus*, the earth."

**Analysis:**

This is the *Fabulae* at their best, and almost all the grammar of a first-year Latin course is here, in the simplest possible form. There are *cum* clauses with the subjunctive (*cum... transiret*, *cum vellet*); *dum* clauses, one with the present indicative in the classical way (*dum deliberat*) and one, less classically, with the subjunctive (*dum... disceptarent*); an indirect question (*quidnam fecisset*); an indirect command after *rogat* (*ut ei daret spiritum*); indirect statements with a gerundive (*suumque nomen ei dandum esse dixit*) and with *debere*; a causal clause with *quandoquidem*; and then, in Saturn's verdict, three kinds of command one after the other: an ordinary imperative (*accipe*), a future imperative (*recipito*) and jussive subjunctives (*possideat*, *vocetur*). It is almost a grammar exercise in narrative form.

It also shows the oddities of the *Fabulae*'s Latin. Jupiter is called *Iovis* in the nominative three times, a late and popular form for the classical *Iuppiter*, although the ablative *ab Iove* is normal. Saturn begins by addressing Jupiter as "you" and then talks about Earth in the third person while apparently speaking to her (*Tellus, quoniam corpus praebuit, corpus recipito*). And the tenses wander between present and past (*rogat... impetravit*). None of this makes the passage hard; it just reminds us that this is not Cicero.

The fable itself is a small philosophical myth built on a pun. The human being is a mixture of three things, breath from the sky, a body from the earth, and the anxious care that shapes it, and each part goes back to its owner in the end: the soul to Jupiter, the body to Earth, while Care keeps the human being *quamdiu vixerit*, "for as long as it lives". And it is named *homo* because it is made from *humus*, earth: an etymology that ancient grammarians genuinely believed. Heidegger saw in it a profound idea, that human existence is defined by concern and worry from beginning to end, and he was not the first: Herder had turned it into a poem, and Goethe may have had it in mind for the figure of Care in *Faust*. Not bad for a few lines in a schoolbook.

---

## Legacy and Impact

The real Hyginus's influence survives mostly at second hand, through the later scholars who used his lost works, and through his place in the story of the Palatine Library and of the first criticism of Virgil. The books that go under his name had a much longer life. The *Fabulae* became one of the basic handbooks of mythology for the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, and they remain indispensable for modern scholars, because many of their summaries preserve the plots of Greek tragedies that are otherwise lost. The *De Astronomia* was one of the first classical scientific texts to be printed, in Venice in 1482, with woodcut illustrations of the constellations, and its pictures and myths shaped the way Renaissance artists and astronomers drew the sky: many of the figures in old star atlases go back, through it, to Hyginus. And the little fable of Cura, through Herder, Goethe and Heidegger, has had a career in modern philosophy that its unknown compiler could never have imagined.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: D (Good Exercise)**

The *Fabulae* are as easy as Latin prose gets: short sentences, simple syntax, a limited vocabulary and stories most readers already know. Their only obstacles are the many Greek mythological names and a handful of non-classical forms (*Iovis* for *Iuppiter*) that a student should not imitate. Like Augustus's *Res Gestae* at the other end of this chapter, they are a perfect warm-up text, and a pleasant way to learn mythology and grammar at the same time. A clear D.

# GRATTIUS (fl. late I century BC)

## Biography

Grattius is the most mysterious author in the whole Augustan Age, and possibly in this whole project. We know one fact about his life, and it comes from a single line of Ovid. Everything else we know, we have to read out of his poem.

**One line in Ovid.** In one of his last letters from exile, Ovid lists the poets who were writing in Rome in his day, a roll-call of names, most of whom are now lost. In the middle of the list comes this line: *aptaque venanti Grattius arma daret*, "and Grattius would give the hunter the weapons he needs". That is all. It tells us that Grattius was a contemporary of Ovid, that he was known in Rome, and that he wrote about hunting. It does not tell us when he was born, where he lived or when he died. Even his name is uncertain: the manuscripts and editors spell it both *Grattius* and *Gratius*.

**A Faliscan?** Editors traditionally call him *Grattius Faliscus*, "Grattius the Faliscan", and the nickname comes from his own poem. In a passage on the best flax for making hunting nets, he contrasts the Spanish flax of Saetabis with the flax grown by "our Faliscans" (*nostris... Faliscis*). The Falisci were the people of Falerii, modern Civita Castellana, a town about fifty kilometres north of Rome. So he may have been from there, or simply proud of the local linen. Either way, it is the only personal detail he lets slip in 541 lines.

**The dog expert.** If there is one thing we do know about Grattius, it is that he loved dogs, and knew a great deal about them. His poem lists the hunting breeds of the known world with the enthusiasm of a modern dog-show judge: the Persian and Median hounds, the Gaulish dogs, the "Petronian" dogs, the Sycambrian dogs from the Rhine, the speedy Gaulish greyhound he calls the *Vertraha* (one of the earliest mentions of the Celtic sighthound, ancestor of the modern greyhound), the Umbrian dog with a great nose and no courage, the famous Molossian mastiffs from Greece, and, perhaps most memorably, the dogs of Britain, which are ugly but magnificent in a fight (that is the excerpt). He also explains how to breed them, feed them, train them and cure them of mange and rabies, including a recommendation to take a sick dog to a sulphurous volcanic cave in Sicily sacred to Vulcan.

**A poem that stops mid-sentence.** His *Cynegetica* ("On Hunting") survives in 541 hexameters, and breaks off in the middle of a sentence about horses. It was probably longer, perhaps much longer; we do not know whether he ever finished it. It survived the Middle Ages in a single manuscript, copied around AD 800 in France and now in Vienna, which also contains a fragment of a poem on fishing attributed to Ovid. The Neapolitan poet Jacopo Sannazaro found it in France in the early 1500s and brought it back to Italy, and it was first printed in Venice in 1534. Before that, for some seven centuries, the only Roman hunting poet known to the world was a name in a list.

## Most Important Works

- **Cynegetica** (*On Hunting*, probably late I century BC) -- a didactic poem in hexameters, written in conscious imitation of Virgil's *Georgics*. It opens with an invocation to Diana, goddess of the hunt, and a brief history of how hunting began, when men learned to use reason and skill instead of bare strength. It then describes the hunter's equipment: nets and the different kinds of flax used to make them, snares, the *formido* (a long rope hung with brightly coloured feathers, used to frighten deer towards the nets), and spears. The longest section is on dogs: the breeds, their good and bad qualities, how to choose, breed and raise them, and how to treat their diseases. There is a moral digression against luxury and the excesses of the table, and the surviving text ends during a section on horses. [Fragmentary -- 541 lines, breaks off]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Grattius is not a great poet, and nobody has ever claimed he was. His model is obviously the *Georgics*, and he borrows Virgil's structures, his solemn invocations, his digressions and his tone, but without Virgil's music or his depth. His hexameters are correct but heavy, his transitions are abrupt, and he has a weakness for showing off his learning with obscure geographical and mythological references. And yet he is not easy.

The first difficulty is the **subject matter**. A poem on hunting equipment and dog breeding is full of technical terms: kinds of flax and linen, parts of nets and snares, types of spears, names of breeds, symptoms of diseases and the herbs and remedies used to cure them. Many of these words are rare, some appear only in Grattius, and their exact meaning is sometimes uncertain even to specialists. **Lexicon: HIGH.** The second is the **geography**: his dogs come from all over the world, and he names their homelands with learned poetic adjectives, Greek and Latin, that assume a reader who knows exactly where the Morini, the Sycambri, the Athamanes or the Acarnanians lived. **Allusion density: MEDIUM to HIGH.** The third is the **text**. Because it survives in a single manuscript, the *Cynegetica* is full of corruptions, gaps and doubtful readings; modern editions are dotted with angle brackets, where editors have supplied missing words, and with asterisks, where whole lines are lost. **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM to HIGH; Stylistic figures: MEDIUM.**

## Latin Excerpt

### Excerpt: Cynegetica 171-181 -- The Dogs of Britain

**Source:** Grattius, *Cynegetica*, lines 171-181, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/grattius.html). The source writes *Vmber* with a capital V for *Umber*, and *mavors* with a small m for the god Mars; both are kept as printed.

**Context:** In the middle of his long catalogue of hunting dogs, Grattius has just described the Umbrian hound, famous for its nose, and now he turns his attention to the far north-west. The Morini were a Gallic people on the coast of the Channel, around modern Boulogne, and beyond their dangerous, tidal sea lay Britain, which Caesar had invaded twice only a generation before. Grattius's verdict on the dogs of Britain is one of the earliest descriptions of British dogs in literature. It is also a good example of his style: a slightly pompous Virgilian manner applied to a very practical piece of advice.

**Latin Text:**

> at fugit adversos idem quos repperit hostis
> Vmber: quanta fides, utinam, et sollertia naris,
> tanta foret virtus et tantum vellet in armis!
> quid, freta si Morinum dubio refluentia ponto
> veneris atque ipsos libeat penetrare Britannos?
> o quanta est merces et quantum impendia supra,
> si non ad speciem mentiturosque decores
> (parvis haec una est catulis iactura Britannis),
> at magnum cum venit opus promendaque virtus
> et vocat extremo praeceps discrimine mavors:
> non tunc egregios tantum admirere Molossos,

**English Translation:**

But the Umbrian dog runs away from the very enemies it has tracked down:
if only it had as much courage, and as much will to fight,
as it has loyalty and keenness of scent!
What if you came to the straits of the Morini, where the sea ebbs and flows uncertainly,
and chose to go as far as the Britons themselves?
Oh, how great the reward, how far beyond the cost,
as long as you are not looking for good looks and deceptive charms
(this is the one flaw of the little British dogs);
but when a great task comes, and courage must be shown,
and the War-god calls you headlong into the final danger,
then you would not admire the famous Molossians so much,

**Analysis:**

The passage begins with a wish and a complaint. The Umbrian dog is a superb tracker, but a coward: it runs away from the very animals it has found. *Quanta fides... et sollertia naris, / tanta foret virtus*: a correlative pair, *quanta... tanta*, "as much... so much", with *utinam* and the imperfect subjunctive *foret* (for *esset*) expressing an unreal wish in the present: "if only it had as much courage as it has nose". *Sollertia naris*, "the skill of its nostril", is a nicely technical touch.

Then comes the journey to Britain, introduced with an epic flourish. *Freta... Morinum dubio refluentia ponto*, "the straits of the Morini, flowing back and forth on an uncertain sea", is a high-style description of the tides of the English Channel, which had famously caught Caesar's fleet by surprise. *Morinum* is a contracted genitive plural (for *Morinorum*), and *veneris* is a perfect subjunctive in the condition. The British dogs themselves are introduced with a frank judgement, set apart in parentheses: *parvis haec una est catulis iactura Britannis*, "this is the one drawback of the little British dogs", namely their lack of looks (*speciem mentiturosque decores*, "appearance and charms that would lie to you"). But, says Grattius, when real work begins, when *mavors* (an old poetic name for Mars, the war-god) calls you into the final danger, you will stop admiring the famous Molossians, the giant mastiffs from Epirus that were the luxury dogs of the Greek and Roman world. Note the potential subjunctive *admirere* (for *admireris*), "you would admire". The sentence goes on in the next lines, but the text there has a gap, which is why the excerpt ends with a comma.

The grammar here is not especially hard; what makes the passage demanding is the compression of the thought, the learned geographical vocabulary and the slightly overblown style, as if Grattius were describing a military campaign rather than a trip to buy a dog. It is a small, vivid window onto the ancient world: Roman hunters, a generation after Caesar, already knew that if you wanted a dog with real courage, you went to Britain.

---

## Legacy and Impact

Grattius had very little influence. He is mentioned by Ovid, imitated perhaps by Nemesianus, who wrote another *Cynegetica* in the third century AD, and then forgotten for seven hundred years, until Sannazaro brought his manuscript to Italy. Since then he has been read mostly by specialists in Latin didactic poetry, by historians of hunting, and by historians of dogs, for whom his catalogue of breeds is a precious source. His importance is that of a rare survivor: a minor poet of the Augustan Age who shows what an ordinary, competent imitator of Virgil looked like, and who reminds us how many such poets must have been writing in Rome, all of them lost except for him, and except for the single line in which Ovid remembered that Grattius would give the hunter his weapons.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: B (Difficult)**

Grattius survives in 541 lines, enough for a fair rating. He is not a subtle stylist, but he is a difficult read: dense technical vocabulary on nets, weapons, dog breeds and veterinary remedies, much of it rare or uncertain; learned geographical and mythological references; a heavy, compressed hexameter in imitation of the *Georgics*; and a text that survives in a single, damaged manuscript, with gaps and editorial supplements. He is easier than Virgil, because there is far less depth and far less art to unpack, but considerably harder than Tibullus or Ovid. A B, and an honest one.

# POMPEIUS TROGUS (fl. late I century BC -- early I century AD)

## Biography

Pompeius Trogus is the great historian of the Augustan Age that almost nobody has read, for the simple reason that nobody can: his work is lost. What we have is a summary of it made centuries later by a man called Justin, who will get his own entry in a later era. Reading Trogus is a bit like reading a famous film through the review in a newspaper: the plot is there, the opinions are there, but the director's voice is mostly gone. And yet what survives of his life, and of his ideas, is surprisingly interesting.

**A Gaul with a Roman name.** Trogus is the only Roman historian who tells us about his own family, and he did it, characteristically, at the very end of his last book. Justin summarises it: his ancestors came from the Vocontii, a Gallic people of Gallia Narbonensis, in what is now Provence, around Vaison-la-Romaine. His grandfather had received Roman citizenship from Pompey the Great during the war against Sertorius in Spain, and, as was the custom, took the name of his patron: that is why a Gaul is called *Pompeius*. His uncle commanded squadrons of cavalry under Pompey in the war against Mithridates, and his father served under Julius Caesar, as his secretary for letters and embassies, and keeper of his seal ring. So in three generations the family went from Gallic tribesmen to the private office of the most powerful man in Rome. Not bad for provincials.

**A historian who looked away from Rome.** Everyone else in Rome wrote the history of Rome. Livy, his contemporary, was writing it from the foundation of the city in 142 books. Trogus decided to write the history of everybody else. His *Historiae Philippicae*, in 44 books, start with Ninus, the legendary first king of Assyria, and move through the empires of the Medes and the Persians, Greece, and above all the Macedonians of Philip and Alexander (hence the title, "Philippic Histories") and the Hellenistic kingdoms that came after them. Rome appears mostly from the outside, as the power that eventually swallows them all, and it gets its own narrative only near the end, in the books on its origins, on Gaul and on Spain, where the work ends with Augustus's final conquest of the Spanish tribes. A world history, written by a Gaul in Latin, in which Rome is the last arrival: it is the most original idea in Augustan historiography.

**A bad influence?** Trogus drew heavily on Greek historians, and one of them may have been Timagenes of Alexandria, a brilliant and sharp-tongued Greek who lived at Rome and wrote a history of the world's kings. Timagenes had a famous problem with Augustus: he could not stop making jokes about the emperor and his family. Augustus banned him from his house, and Timagenes, out of spite, publicly burned the books he had written about Augustus's reign. Whether Trogus really used him is debated, but it would explain why his history is so full of the voices of Rome's enemies.

**The man who hated invented speeches.** Ancient historians loved to put long speeches in the mouths of their characters, in direct speech, as if they had taken them down word for word. Livy and Sallust filled their works with them. Trogus thought this was dishonest: Justin tells us that he criticised Livy and Sallust because, by inserting direct speeches and passing off their own eloquence as that of their characters, they had gone beyond the proper limits of history. So when Trogus did include a great speech, he wrote it in indirect speech. Justin admired that one so much that he copied it out almost in full: it is the speech of Mithridates, king of Pontus, to his soldiers before his war against Rome, and it is one of the longest passages of indirect speech in Latin literature, and one of the most violent attacks on Rome ever written by a Roman. That is the excerpt.

**Also an expert on animals.** Trogus also wrote a work on animals and plants, now lost, and Pliny the Elder quotes him several times as an authority on zoology. An historian of empires and a naturalist: the Augustan Age liked its writers versatile.

**Lost, and saved by a summary.** Some time between the second and fourth centuries AD (the date is disputed), Justin produced his *Epitome*, cutting Trogus's 44 books down to a collection of what he himself calls the most pleasant and most instructive parts, a little "body of flowers". It was a huge success, and it killed the original: once readers had the short version, nobody bothered to copy the long one. The *Epitome* was read in every medieval school, and survives in some two hundred manuscripts. Of Trogus himself we have only Justin's text, the *prologi* (short summaries of the contents of each of the 44 books, which may go back to Trogus's own edition), and a few quotations. It is the most successful act of destruction-by-abridgement in Latin literature.

## Most Important Works

- **Historiae Philippicae** (*Philippic Histories*) -- a world history in 44 books, from the Assyrian empire to the age of Augustus, centred on Macedon and the Hellenistic kingdoms, with Rome treated mostly from the outside. Survives through Justin's *Epitome* and the *prologi*. [Lost; abridged]
- **On Animals** (and possibly on plants) -- a work of natural history cited by Pliny the Elder. [Lost]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Here we have to be honest: any rating of Trogus is a rating of Justin. The Latin we read is Justin's, though he often keeps Trogus's sentences, especially in the speeches and the moral comments. The result is clear, readable narrative prose: mostly classical in grammar and vocabulary, with long but well-built periods, frequent moralising sentences in the manner of Sallust, and a fondness for antithesis and neat endings. Some later features creep in, and the condensation sometimes leaves gaps in the logic of the story, but on the whole it is easier than Livy, and much easier than Sallust. **Lexicon: LOW to MEDIUM; Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM.**

The real challenge is the one Trogus chose himself: **indirect speech**. When a whole speech is reported in *oratio obliqua*, every main verb becomes an infinitive, every subordinate verb becomes a subjunctive, every "I" becomes *se* or *sibi* and every "you" becomes *illi* or *ipsi*, and the reader has to rebuild the original speech in his head as he translates. It is one of the best exercises in Latin syntax, and the Mithridates speech is the classic example. **Stylistic figures: MEDIUM; Allusion density: MEDIUM** (the history of half the ancient world, with names to match).

## Latin Excerpt

### Excerpt: Justin, Epitome 38.6.7-8 -- The Sons of the She-Wolf

**Source:** Justin, *Epitome of the Philippic Histories of Pompeius Trogus*, 38.6.7-8, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/justin/38.html). Section number 8 inside the passage has been removed.

**Context:** In 88 BC Mithridates VI, king of Pontus, is about to begin his first war against Rome. He gathers his army and makes a long speech, which Trogus reports in indirect speech and Justin preserves almost entire. Mithridates has just listed the kings that Rome has used and then betrayed (Eumenes of Pergamum, Masinissa of Numidia), and now he explains why the Romans hate all kings: because their own kings were so embarrassing. Every Roman reader knew the seven kings of Rome; Mithridates turns the proud legend of the city upside down, one king at a time.

**Latin Text:**

Hanc illos omnibus regibus legem odiorum dixisse, scilicet quia ipsi tales reges habuerint, quorum etiam nominibus erubescant, aut pastores Aboriginum, aut aruspices Sabinorum, aut exules Corinthiorum, aut servos vernasque Tuscorum, aut, quod honoratissimum nomen fuit inter haec, Superbos; atque ut ipsi ferunt conditores suos lupae uberibus altos, sic omnem illum populum luporum animos inexplebiles sanguinis, atque imperii divitiarumque avidos ac ieiunos habere.

**English Translation:**

(He said that) they had laid down this law of hatred against all kings, obviously because they themselves had had kings whose very names made them blush: either shepherds of the Aborigines, or soothsayers of the Sabines, or exiles from Corinth, or slaves and house-born servants of the Etruscans, or, the most honourable name among them, the Proud; and just as they themselves say that their founders were suckled at the teats of a she-wolf, so that whole people had the souls of wolves, insatiable for blood, and greedy and hungry for power and wealth.

**Analysis:**

This is a perfect example of *oratio obliqua*. There is no verb of saying in the passage, because the *ait* that governs it came several sections earlier: everything depends on it. So the main verbs are infinitives (*dixisse*, *habere*), with their subjects in the accusative (*illos*, *omnem illum populum*), and the verbs of the subordinate clauses are in the subjunctive (*habuerint*, *erubescant*). The pronouns follow the rules too: *illos* and *ipsi* are the Romans, seen from Mithridates' point of view. To translate it well, a student has to supply "he said that" and then decide how much of the indirect construction to keep in Italian or English. One small trap: *quod honoratissimum nomen fuit* is in the indicative, because it is a comment by the narrator, not part of Mithridates' speech.

The list of kings is a little masterpiece of insult, and every item is a real Roman king described in the worst possible way. The "shepherds of the Aborigines" are Romulus and Remus, raised by shepherds; the "soothsayers of the Sabines" is Numa Pompilius, the pious Sabine king; the "exiles from Corinth" is Tarquinius Priscus, son of Demaratus, a Corinthian refugee; the "slaves of the Etruscans" is Servius Tullius, said to be the son of a slave woman; and the last are the Tarquins, whose most famous member was called *Superbus*, the Proud. The sentence then ends with the most famous symbol of Rome, the she-wolf, and turns it into an accusation: a people raised on wolf's milk has the soul of a wolf. Note the *ut... sic* comparison, and the ending, *avidos ac ieiunos*, "greedy and starving", with the genitives *imperii divitiarumque* depending on both adjectives. A Gaul whose family owed everything to Pompey and Caesar gave this speech to Rome's greatest enemy, and gave it his best prose: that is either remarkable fairness or remarkable courage.

---

## Legacy and Impact

Through Justin, Trogus had an enormous influence on the way the Middle Ages and the Renaissance saw ancient history outside Rome. The *Epitome* was the standard handbook for the history of the Assyrians, the Persians, the Greeks and the Macedonians; it was read by Orosius and by Augustine, quoted throughout the Middle Ages, and used by Renaissance writers looking for good stories and moral examples. Many of the most famous anecdotes about Alexander the Great, the Scythians, the Amazons or the foundation of Marseille reached European culture through this book. And the speech of Mithridates, with its picture of Rome as a nation of wolves, has been quoted ever since by everyone who wanted to criticise an empire. The irony is that Trogus is famous through the very book that caused his work to disappear.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

What we read of Trogus is clear, classical narrative prose, filtered and simplified by Justin, and easier than Livy or Sallust. Two things lift it above a D: the condensation, which sometimes makes the story jump or the connections unclear, and the long passages of indirect speech, which are an excellent but demanding test of syntax. A solid C, and one of the best places to practise *oratio obliqua*.

# MARCUS MANILIUS (fl. early I century AD)

## Biography

Manilius is the last author of the Augustan Age, and in some ways the strangest. He wrote the longest surviving poem on astrology in the ancient world, a work of real power and occasional brilliance; and yet no ancient writer ever mentions him. Not Ovid, who mentions everybody; not Quintilian, who lists the Latin poets; not Pliny. He is a man who claimed to read the future in the stars, and could not secure his own.

**A poet without a biography.** We do not even know his name for certain: the manuscripts call him Manilius, Manlius or Mallius, and the first name Marcus is not secure either. Everything we know comes from the poem itself. He addresses an emperor, Caesar, as the "prince of his country" and son of a god; he mentions the disaster of Varus, when three legions were destroyed in Germany in AD 9, as a recent event. So he wrote at the end of Augustus's reign, and probably finished the poem, or failed to finish it, under Tiberius. Some scholars, noting a few un-Roman turns in his Latin, have suggested he was a foreigner; others that he was a freedman, like Hyginus. Nobody knows.

**Astrology, the fashion of the age.** Manilius chose his subject well. Astrology was the great intellectual fashion of the early Empire. Augustus, according to Suetonius, published his own horoscope and struck coins with the sign of Capricorn, which he considered his lucky sign; Manilius duly mentions Capricorn shining on the birth of Augustus. Tiberius, the next emperor, was even more devoted: he lived on Capri with his personal astrologer, Thrasyllus. A poem showing that the stars rule everything, and that the emperor's rule was written in the sky, was exactly what the palace wanted to hear.

**The anti-Lucretius.** Manilius is proud, and he tells us so: he is the first, he says, to bring the knowledge of the heavens into Latin song. His great rival is Lucretius, whom he imitates constantly and contradicts on every point. Lucretius had taught that the universe is made of atoms moving by chance, and that the gods do not interfere; Manilius, a Stoic, teaches that the universe is a single living body governed by divine reason, and that everything, from the fall of empires to the job of the man next door, is decided by fate. If Lucretius wanted to free men from fear of the gods, Manilius wants to free them from anxiety about the future, by telling them that it is already decided anyway. That is the first excerpt.

**Horoscopes as comedy.** The poem's most entertaining parts are the descriptions of the characters of people born under each constellation, which read like a Roman comedy of types. The man born under the Ram becomes a wool merchant; under the Ship, a sailor; under the Charioteer, a circus driver or a trick rider. And whoever is born under Orion will be a busybody, rushing around the city at dawn, all over everyone's doorstep, with the same single word of greeting for everybody, "the friend of all". Every reader in Rome must have recognised someone.

**An unfinished heaven.** The poem has five books, about 4,200 lines. Book I describes the universe and the constellations; Books II and III the technical apparatus of astrology, the signs and their relations, the houses, the lots and the calculation of the moment of birth; Book IV the characters given by the signs of the zodiac; Book V the constellations that rise alongside the zodiac and the people they produce. Manilius promises more than once to treat the planets, which are essential to any horoscope, but never does. Either the poem is unfinished, or a part of it is lost. In both cases, the astrologer ran out of time.

**Found again, and edited by a poet.** Manilius disappeared for most of the Middle Ages, and was found again in 1417 by Poggio Bracciolini, the great Italian manuscript hunter, during the Council of Constance (the same man who rediscovered Lucretius). His text is so corrupt, and so technical, that it became the great proving ground of Latin textual criticism. Joseph Scaliger and Richard Bentley edited him; and then A. E. Housman, the English poet of *A Shropshire Lad*, spent almost thirty years on him, publishing his edition in five volumes from 1903 to 1930. Housman did not think much of Manilius as a poet, but he thought even less of earlier editors, and his prefaces are famous for some of the most savage and funniest insults in the history of scholarship. One of the mildest: an editor with no judgment, faced with two manuscripts, is like a donkey between two bundles of hay. A minor didactic poet thus became the reason for one of the great masterpieces of philology, and the subject of the best-written scholarly rudeness in English.

## Most Important Works

- **Astronomica** (written c. AD 9-20) -- a didactic poem in five books of hexameters on astronomy and astrology: the structure of the universe and the constellations (I), the zodiac and the technical system of the horoscope (II and III), the characters and destinies given by the signs of the zodiac (IV), and the constellations rising with the signs and the people born under them (V), with mythological episodes such as Andromeda and Perseus, and philosophical passages on fate and divine reason. The promised treatment of the planets is missing. [Survives; probably incomplete]

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Manilius is one of the hardest poets in Latin, and the reason is that he tries to do two impossible things at once. The first is to put mathematics into verse. Large parts of Books II and III are calculations: degrees of the signs, divisions of the circle, the rising times of the constellations, all expressed in hexameters, which have no room for numbers. Manilius loves to show off by turning "thirty degrees" into periphrases like "three times ten parts", and the reader has to do the arithmetic and know the astrology at the same time. **Lexicon: HIGH** (technical astrology, Greek terms in Latin dress); **Allusion density: HIGH**.

The second is to be brilliant. When Manilius is not counting, he is writing philosophy, satire or myth, and here he is one of the most pointed poets after Ovid: short, epigrammatic sentences, paradoxes, antitheses, plays on words, chiasmus, a sentence that turns on its last word. It is the style of the coming Silver Age, a generation early. Add a vocabulary stretched to cover a subject it was never made for, a very compressed syntax, frequent hyperbaton, and a text which survives in poor manuscripts and has been emended in hundreds of places, and the result is, frankly, a nightmare. **Syntactic complexity: VERY HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH.** The philosophical and mythological passages are where a student should start; the technical ones are for specialists only.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Astronomica 4.1-16 -- Why Do We Waste Our Lives?

**Source:** Manilius, *Astronomica* IV, lines 1-16, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/manilius4.html)

**Context:** Book IV opens with a philosophical prelude, before the long description of the characters given by the signs of the zodiac. It is the passage in which Manilius explains why astrology should bring peace of mind rather than fear: if everything is already decided by fate, it is pointless to waste our life in anxiety and greed. It reads like a Stoic sermon, and it ends with one of the most famous lines in Latin poetry.

**Latin Text:**

> Quid tam sollicitis vitam consumimus annis
> torquemurque metu caecaque cupidine rerum
> aeternisque senes curis, dum quaerimus, aevum
> perdimus et nullo votorum fine beati
> victuros agimus semper nec vivimus umquam,
> pauperiorque bonis quisque est, quia plura requirit
> nec quod habet numerat, tantum quod non habet optat,
> cumque sibi parvos usus natura reposcat
> materiam struimus magnae per vota ruinae
> luxuriamque lucris emimus luxuque rapinas,
> et summum census pretium est effundere censum?
> solvite, mortales, animos curasque levate
> totque supervacuis vitam deplete querellis.
> fata regunt orbem, certa stant omnia lege
> longaque per certos signantur tempora casus.
> nascentes morimur, finisque ab origine pendet.

**English Translation:**

Why do we consume our lives in such anxious years,
tormented by fear and by blind desire for things,
and, grown old in endless worries, lose our time
while we search for it, and, never happy, since our wishes have no end,
we always behave as if about to live, and never live at all?
Each man is the poorer for his riches, because he wants more,
and does not count what he has, but only longs for what he has not;
and though nature asks for herself only small needs,
we pile up with our wishes the material for a great collapse,
and with our profits we buy luxury, and with luxury, plunder,
and the highest reward of wealth is to squander wealth?
Free your minds, mortals, lighten your cares,
and empty your life of so many useless complaints.
The fates rule the world, all things stand fixed by a sure law,
and the long ages are marked by sure events.
As we are born, we die, and the end hangs from the beginning.

**Analysis:**

The whole first part is a single rhetorical question, eleven lines long, held together by *-que* and *et*, which a student has to follow to the question mark at the end of line 11. Manilius packs it with paradoxes in the Stoic manner: we lose time while we look for it (*dum quaerimus, aevum / perdimus*); we are always "about to live" and never live (*victuros agimus semper nec vivimus umquam*, with *victuros* the future participle of *vivo*, not of *vinco*: a classic trap); each man is poorer because of his riches; the greatest value of wealth is to waste it (*summum census pretium est effundere censum*, with the same word, *census*, in two different cases at the two ends of the sentence). *Nullo votorum fine beati* is the hardest phrase: literally "happy with no end of wishes", that is, people who could be happy but whose wishes never stop; editors and translators still argue about it.

Then the tone changes: the imperatives *solvite*, *levate*, *deplete* give the cure, and lines 14-16 give the reason, in three short, absolute statements. The last line is pure Manilius: *nascentes morimur*, "we die as we are born", two words that contain a whole philosophy, and *finisque ab origine pendet*, "and the end hangs from the beginning", in which the image is of a chain: the moment of birth, read in the stars, already contains the moment of death. It is both a consolation and a rather chilling thought, and that is exactly the ambiguity of astrology.

### Excerpt 2: Astronomica 5.567-576 -- Perseus Sees Andromeda

**Source:** Manilius, *Astronomica* V, lines 567-576, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/manilius5.html)

**Context:** Book V describes the constellations that rise alongside the signs of the zodiac. When he reaches Andromeda, Manilius cannot resist telling her story, and it becomes the longest and most beautiful narrative episode of the poem. Andromeda, daughter of Cepheus and Cassiope, has been chained to a rock as a sacrifice to a sea monster to atone for her parents' guilt. Manilius has just described her, beautiful even in her suffering, with the birds and the sea nymphs weeping for her. Now the hero arrives, flying home after killing Medusa.

**Latin Text:**

> tandem Gorgonei victorem Persea monstri
> felix illa dies redeuntem ad litora duxit.
> isque, ubi pendentem vidit de rupe puellam,
> deriguit, facie quem non stupefecerat hostis,
> vixque manu spolium tenuit, victorque Medusae
> victus in Andromeda est. iam cautibus invidet ipsis
> felicisque vocat, teneant quae membra, catenas;
> et, postquam poenae causam cognovit ab ipsa,
> destinat in thalamos per bellum vadere ponti,
> altera si Gorgo veniat, non territus illa.

**English Translation:**

At last that happy day brought Perseus, the conqueror
of the Gorgon monster, back to those shores.
And when he saw the girl hanging from the rock,
he froze, he whom the face of his enemy had not turned to stone,
and he could hardly hold his trophy in his hand: the conqueror of Medusa
was conquered by Andromeda. Now he envies the very rocks,
and calls the chains happy, because they hold her limbs;
and when he has learned from her the reason for her punishment,
he resolves to make his way to the marriage bed through a war with the sea,
not afraid even if a second Gorgon were to come.

**Analysis:**

This is Manilius in his Ovidian mode, and it is one of the wittiest passages in Latin poetry. The whole joke is about stone and petrification. Medusa turned to stone everyone who looked at her, but not Perseus; now one look at a girl makes him freeze (*deriguit*, "he grew stiff"), "he whom the face of the enemy had not stupefied" (*facie quem non stupefecerat hostis*, with the relative clause placed before its antecedent, a typical poetic inversion). The trophy he can hardly hold is the head of Medusa itself. Then the famous paradox: *victorque Medusae / victus in Andromeda est*, "the conqueror of Medusa was conquered in Andromeda", with the same root (*victor... victus*) set across the line break, a figure called polyptoton.

The next lines continue the conceit. Perseus envies the rocks and calls the chains lucky (*felicis*, accusative plural in *-is*, the archaic form for *felices*, agreeing with *catenas*), because they get to touch her: the relative clause *teneant quae membra* has the subjunctive because it gives his reason, and *quae* is postponed after *teneant*. The final line returns to the Gorgon: he would face a second one for her (*altera si Gorgo veniat*, a hypothetical subjunctive, with *illa* in the ablative of cause). Grammatically, the passage is not as hard as the technical books; what makes it demanding is the word order, the pointed style, and the need to see the joke. Manilius wrote the dullest pages of Augustan poetry, and some of the cleverest.

## Legacy and Impact

Manilius had almost no readers in antiquity, as far as we can tell, and was nearly lost in the Middle Ages. His rediscovery by Poggio in 1417 made him a key text for Renaissance astrologers and astronomers, and the first printed edition appeared around 1473, edited by the astronomer Regiomontanus. Since then his legacy has belonged above all to scholarship: Scaliger, Bentley and Housman made him the most famous battlefield of textual criticism, and Housman's edition, with its prefaces, is still read today, partly for the method and partly for the insults. He is also an important source for the history of ancient astrology, and his poetic passages, on fate, on the universe as a living god, on human reason climbing to the sky, influenced later Stoic writing. And one line has escaped from the poem entirely: *nascentes morimur*, quoted on sundials, gravestones and in books of quotations by people who have no idea who wrote it.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: S (GOD PLEASE HELP)**

Manilius is the last author of the Augustan Age, and one of the hardest of all. Technical astrology turned into hexameters, with numbers and calculations disguised as poetic periphrasis; a highly compressed, pointed style full of paradox, hyperbaton and wordplay; a vocabulary stretched beyond its natural limits; and a text so corrupt that it took Scaliger, Bentley and Housman to make sense of it. The philosophical and mythological passages are difficult but rewarding; the technical books are a real ordeal. Alongside Horace, he is the peak of difficulty of this era, and he takes it to the end of the Augustan Age in a firm S.
