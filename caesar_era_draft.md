# CAESAR'S ERA (100--44 BC)

The first century BC is Rome's most turbulent, and the turbulence is inseparable from its literature. The Republic spent the century destroying itself: Sulla's march on Rome, the Catilinarian conspiracy, the First Triumvirate, Caesar's civil war, and finally his assassination in 44 BC. For the authors of this era, this is not background noise -- it is the subject. Cicero was executed on Antony's orders. Sallust wrote history as a post-mortem on a republic he had watched corrupt itself from the inside. Caesar narrated his own conquests in the third person, as calmly as if someone else had done them. The political crisis and the literary output are the same phenomenon.

Greek philosophy arrived in Rome in this era with the force of a delayed import finally cleared through customs. Epicureanism, Stoicism, and Academic skepticism were no longer foreign curiosities: they were the intellectual air educated Romans breathed. Lucretius built his entire epic on Epicurean physics, arguing in verse that the gods do not intervene in human affairs and that death is simply nothing to worry about. Cicero, who was skeptical of certainty in all its forms, ransacked the Academy and the Stoa alike to produce Latin versions of Greek philosophy -- coining words like *qualitas* and *essentia* because Latin had no equivalents, and in doing so permanently shaped the vocabulary of Western thought.

The most consequential development of this era is the stabilization of Latin itself. The archaic language of Plautus and Ennius -- irregular, exuberant, full of forms that later readers would find barbaric -- gave way to the *sermo urbanus*, the polished urban prose that schoolchildren still parse today. This transition was not accidental: it was argued over. The *Atticists*, led by the orator Calvus, advocated a plain, spare style modeled on classical Greek, stripped of ornament. Against them, Cicero defended a fuller, rhythmical prose -- neither Attic austerity nor Asiatic excess, but a middle road that used periodic sentences, rhetorical figures, and careful cadence to make Latin a pleasure to read aloud. Cicero won, in the sense that his prose became the model for the next two millennia.

The Big 5 of this era -- Cicero, Caesar, Lucretius, Sallust, Catullus -- shaped not just Roman literature but the entire Western tradition. Cicero defined what an orator and a philosophical writer could do in Latin. Caesar demonstrated that absolute clarity could be its own kind of power. Lucretius proved that a technical argument about physics could also be great poetry. Sallust invented the compressed, moralistic historical monograph. Catullus made Roman lyric poetry personal in a way it had never been before. Any of the five, taken alone, would be enough to make this era remarkable. Together, they represent a convergence that has no real parallel in the history of Latin literature.

The works produced in Caesar's era fall into strikingly different categories, reflecting how many things Latin could now do. At one end, Varro and Lucretius write *didactic* works -- encyclopaedic and technical, one about farming and language, the other about the nature of the universe. At the other, Catullus writes lyric poetry of radical brevity and emotional directness. Between them: Caesar and Sallust in historical prose; Cicero oscillating between oratory (the *Verrine* orations, the *Philippics*), philosophical dialogue (*De Re Publica*, *Tusculanae Disputationes*), and deeply personal letters that were never meant to be published but were. The era has no single literary mode. It has, instead, an embarrassment of them.

# MARCUS TERENTIUS VARRO (116--27 BC)

## Biography

Marcus Terentius Varro was born in 116 BC in Reate, a Sabine town in the hills northeast of Rome (modern Rieti), and died in 27 BC at eighty-nine. What he did with those years staggers the imagination: according to Jerome's count, seventy-four separate works comprising approximately six hundred and twenty books. No other Latin prose author in antiquity comes close. The man was not merely prolific -- he was encyclopaedic in the literal sense, filling volumes the way other Romans filled storehouses. Cicero dedicated the *Academica* to him and wrote that Romans had been wandering as strangers in their own city until Varro's books showed them their history, their customs, and who they actually were. That is not a dedication. It is a civic debt notice.

He was educated in Rome under the distinguished philologist Aelius Stilo, and later in Athens under the Academic philosopher Antiochus of Ascalon -- the same Antiochus whose lectures Cicero attended, a detail that places both men in the same intellectual current even as they pursued very different literary careers. Varro's practical side was equally developed: he served as a naval commander under Pompey during the campaign against the pirates (67 BC), earning the *corona rostrata*, the naval crown, one of the rarest military decorations in Roman history, awarded for victory in a sea battle. A scholar who could also run a fleet is an unusual combination, but Varro managed it without apparent difficulty.

In the civil war he sided with Pompey, commanding troops in Spain, where things went badly and he eventually surrendered to Caesar. Caesar, who was shrewder than his enemies about when to be generous, pardoned him completely -- and then gave him one of the most remarkable assignments in Roman cultural history: planning Rome's first great public library. For a man who had spent his life producing books, the prospect of building a place to house them must have seemed like the logical culmination of everything. It was not to be. Caesar was assassinated in 44 BC, the library project died with him, and Varro lost his most powerful patron.

Worse followed. Mark Antony proscribed him after the formation of the Second Triumvirate -- Varro's name went on the list, his estates were seized, and soldiers ransacked his private library. He survived, reportedly hiding in the villa of Calpurnius Piso, and was eventually removed from the proscription list, possibly through Octavian's intervention. He got back enough of his property and peace of mind to keep writing. He was still completing new works at the age of eighty-eight.

Petrarch called him *"tertium Romanae lucis lumen"* -- the third great light of Rome, placing him alongside Cicero and Virgil as one of antiquity's supreme luminaries. Augustine, who quarried Varro's *Antiquitates* exhaustively for the *City of God* (disagreeing with it so passionately that he cited it on almost every page), called him *"vir doctissimus"* -- the most learned of men. The joke -- because there is always a joke with Varro -- is that he opens *De Re Rustica* by explaining to his wife that he is writing in a hurry because his eightieth year is reminding him to pack his bags before departing life. He then lived nine more years and kept writing through all of them.

## Main Works

- **De Re Rustica** (*On Agriculture*, 37 BC) -- three books on farming, animal husbandry, and villa management, cast in the form of a philosophical dialogue. The most readable of Varro's surviving works, partly because the dialogue frame provides movement and the occasional dry joke. [Complete]
- **De Lingua Latina** (*On the Latin Language*) -- originally twenty-five books; a systematic investigation of Latin etymology, inflection, and word-formation. Books V through X survive, with gaps. Essential for the history of Latin linguistics, and frequently maddening. [Fragmentary -- Books V-X of XXV]
- **Saturae Menippeae** (*Menippean Satires*, c. 80--67 BC) -- roughly one hundred and fifty books of Cynic-style satirical writing in a mixture of prose and verse, modelled on the philosopher Menippus. Survive only as approximately six hundred fragments preserved by later grammarians. Were apparently very funny and unlike anything else in Latin literature. Their loss is one of antiquity's most lamented. [Fragmentary -- c. 600 fragments from 150 books]
- **Antiquitates Rerum Humanarum et Divinarum** (*Antiquities of Human and Divine Matters*) -- forty-one books. A systematic account of Roman history, institutions, religious rites, and theology. The most important work Varro ever wrote and arguably the most important work of Roman cultural history ever attempted. Known primarily through Augustine's exhaustive and combative citations in the *City of God*. [Lost]
- **Disciplinarum Libri IX** (*Nine Books of Disciplines*) -- an encyclopaedia of the nine liberal arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric, geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, music, medicine, architecture). The work that gave the Middle Ages most of its educational framework, including the distinction between trivium and quadrivium. [Lost]
- **Imagines** (*Portraits*, also called *Hebdomades*) -- seven hundred paired portraits of famous Greeks and Romans, each accompanied by a verse epigram. Possibly the first illustrated book in Western history. [Lost]

The pattern should by now be clear. Varro is the most widely influential author in the history of Latin prose and among the most thoroughly lost.

## Style and Difficulty

*De Re Rustica* is the entry point into Varro, and in some ways the most deceptive. It looks easy: dialogue form, named interlocutors, a conversational frame, a subject (farming) whose vocabulary is at least predictable. A reader with solid intermediate Latin and a good agricultural glossary can navigate it without being overwhelmed. The periodic sentences are real -- Varro was a classically trained orator and it shows -- but they are controlled rather than labyrinthine, and the dialogue structure provides constant contextual anchoring. **Lexicon: MEDIUM** (technical agricultural vocabulary, but repetitive and learnable); **Syntactic complexity: LOW-MEDIUM**; **Allusion density: LOW**.

*De Lingua Latina* is a different creature. The subject is Latin itself, which means the vocabulary is abstract and meta-linguistic: Varro discusses inflection, word-formation, etymology, and grammatical category using philosophical terms borrowed from Greek that he himself is not entirely consistent in applying. Reading it requires not just Latin but the ability to think analytically *about* Latin while reading Latin -- a recursive demand that wrong-foots even experienced readers. His etymologies, furthermore, are frequently wrong by modern standards (he derives *lucus*, "sacred grove," from *lucere*, "to shine," on the grounds that it is shaded and therefore dark: the opposite of shining, as he himself acknowledges, which he considers etymologically significant). This requires a degree of patient detachment. **Lexicon: HIGH**; **Syntactic complexity: MEDIUM**.

The *Saturae Menippeae* present a third mode entirely: the mixture of prose and verse, the Cynic satirical register, the rapid code-switching between philosophical abstraction and street-level humor. What survives is fragments of fragments, which means the most allusive and elliptical passages tend to survive while the explanatory tissue around them is gone. Based on what can be read, they were linguistically adventurous -- mixing archaic Latin, Greek loanwords, colloquialisms, neologisms, and technical vocabulary as the satirical mood demanded. **Lexicon: HIGH; Stylistic figures: HIGH** (for what survives).

Across his surviving work, the main obstacles are: (1) technical vocabulary that shifts register between works, requiring different preparation for each; (2) an organizational style that privileges completeness over argument -- Varro lists, catalogues, and enumerates where a more rhetorically minded author would synthesize; (3) in *De Lingua Latina* specifically, the abstractness of the subject. **Genre burden: MEDIUM-HIGH** (encyclopaedic and didactic, with philosophical elements in the linguistic work). As a composite assessment, *De Re Rustica* sits squarely at Tier C; *De Lingua Latina* pushes into Tier B. The overall rating reflects the bulk of surviving material.

> Otium si essem consecutus, Fundania, commodius tibi haec scriberem, quae nunc, ut potero, exponam cogitans esse properandum, quod, ut dicitur, si est homo bulla, eo magis senex. > Nec, ut Homerus et Ennius, Musas, sed duodecim deos Consentis [...] illos XII deos, qui maxime agricolarum duces sunt. Primum, qui omnis fructos agri culturae caelo et terra continent, Iovem et Tellurem: itaque, quod ii parentes, magni dicuntur, Iuppiter pater appellatur, Tellus terra mater. Secundo Solem et Lunam, quorum tempora observantur, cum quaedam seruntur et conduntur. Tertio Cererem et Liberum, quod horum fructus maxime necessari ad victum: ab his enim cibus et potio venit e fundo. Quarto Robigum ac Floram, quibus propitiis neque robigo frumenta atque arbores corrumpit, neque non tempestive florent. Itaque publice Robigo feriae Robigalia, Florae ludi Floralia instituti. Item adveneror Minervam et Venerem, quarum unius procuratio oliveti, alterius hortorum; quo nomine rustica Vinalia instituta. Nec non etiam precor Lympham ac Bonum Eventum, quoniam sine aqua omnis arida ac misera agri cultura, sine successu ac bono eventu frustratio est, non cultura.

*(De Re Rustica I.1 -- opening dedication and invocation)*

**English translation** *(original)*

> If I had managed to find some leisure, Fundania, I would have written these things for you more comfortably; as it stands, I will set them out as best I can, bearing in mind that haste is necessary -- because, as they say, if a man is a bubble, an old man is all the more so. My eightieth year is reminding me to pack my bags before I set out from life. Since, then, you have bought an estate that you wish to make productive by farming it well, and you are asking me to take responsibility for advising you on it, I will try; and not only will I advise you on what should be done for as long as I live, but also after my death.

> Not the Muses, as Homer and Ennius did, but the twelve Consentes gods [...] those twelve gods who are above all the guides of farmers. First, Jupiter and Tellus, who hold together all the fruits of agriculture through sky and earth: and because they are parents and are called great ones, Jupiter is called Father, Tellus is called Earth Mother. Second, Sol and Luna, whose seasons are watched when certain things are sown and stored. Third, Ceres and Liber, because their fruits are most necessary for sustenance: from them come the food and drink that the farm provides. Fourth, Robigus and Flora, by whose favour neither does mildew ruin the grain and the trees, nor do they fail to flower in season - and so the state founded the Robigalia festival for Robigus and the Floralia games for Flora. Likewise I revere Minerva and Venus, the care of the olive grove belonging to one and of the gardens to the other - on whose account the rustic Vinalia was founded. And I pray too to Lympha and Bonus Eventus, since without water all farming is dry and wretched, and without success and a good outcome there is only frustration, not cultivation.

**Analysis**

The opening of *De Re Rustica* sets Varro's tone immediately: dry wit inside a frame of genuine erudition. Fundania is his wife, so his first move in a farming manual is to apologize to her for writing it in a hurry because he is about to die. The *bulla* -- the amulet-bubble worn by Roman boys -- is applied to human life in general, old age in particular. *Sarcinas conligam, antequam proficiscar e vita* ("pack my bags before setting out from life") is probably a proverbial phrase, but Varro anchors it to his eightieth year with the precision of a man who kept meticulous records of everything.

The invocation that follows is learned wit in action: Varro bypasses Homer's Muses and Ennius's Muses in favor of a full enumeration of agricultural deities -- Jupiter and Tellus, Sol and Luna, Ceres and Liber, and on down the list. A man who cannot open a farming manual without first cataloguing the correct gods for the occasion is a man whose encyclopaedic compulsion is structural, not ornamental. Note also the hyperbaton in *quem bene colendo fructuosum cum facere velis*: the subordinate clause wraps around its own object in a way that demands the reader hold the syntax in suspension -- characteristic of Varro's prose at its denser moments.

## Proposed Tier

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

The core of Varro's accessible prose -- *De Re Rustica* in particular -- sits at the manageable end of the difficulty scale. The language is classical, the vocabulary, though technical, is concrete, and the dialogue form provides structural clarity that prevents the denser passages from becoming truly opaque. *De Lingua Latina* pushes toward Tier B in its more analytical sections, due to abstract vocabulary and compressed argumentation. As a composite rating for what survives, Tier C reflects the fact that a well-prepared reader with good vocabulary support can navigate Varro's work without being overwhelmed. The NC designation does not apply: what survives is substantial enough to sustain a meaningful rating.

# CORNELIUS NEPOS (c. 110--24 BC)

![Cornelius Nepos](./images/cornelius_nepos.jpg)
*Cornelius Nepos (imaginary reconstruction)*

## Biography

Cornelius Nepos was born around 110 BC somewhere in Cisalpine Gaul -- the ancient sources are vague on the exact town, and Nepos himself apparently saw no reason to clear the matter up. What is certain is that he came to Rome, moved in the highest literary circles of the late Republic, and managed to die in his bed in 24 BC at a respectable old age, which in the first century BC was no mean achievement. He knew Cicero, he knew Catullus, and he was one of the closest friends of Titus Pomponius Atticus, the man who maintained intimate correspondence with virtually every literate Roman of the era. To be a friend of Atticus was to be connected to everyone. Nepos played his cards quietly and well.

The most famous thing about Nepos is not something he wrote -- it is something written about him. Catullus opens his collected poems with a dedication to Cornelio, his fellow Cisalpine Gaul, describing him as the man who "used to think my trifles were something" (*meas esse aliquid putare nugas*) and who had dared, alone among Italians, to set out all of history in three scholarly and laborious little books (*omne aevum tribus explicare chartis / doctis, Iuppiter, et laboriosis*). The affection is real, the teasing is gentle, and the reference to the three-book *Chronica* tells us something important: Nepos was known to his contemporaries not as a biographer but as a chronographer, a man who had tried to organize universal history into a compact framework. The biographical works came later and outlasted everything else.

Nepos was also the biographer of Atticus -- and this is the genuinely unusual part: he wrote the life while Atticus was still alive. Atticus died in 32 BC; sections of the surviving *Vita Attici* clearly refer to events before his death and were apparently read by the subject himself. Writing a panegyric biography of a living friend and submitting it to him for his approval is an act that requires either great tact or great friendship, and Nepos and Atticus had enough of both. The life of Atticus is the most personal thing Nepos wrote: the portrait of a man he admired deeply, written for no occasion except admiration. After Atticus's death, Nepos apparently added a final chapter bringing the account to its close.

His life was spent at a careful distance from the violence of his era. He did not hold magistracies, did not command armies, and was not assassinated. While Cicero was being hunted down by Antony's soldiers, while Caesar was crossing rubicons and being stabbed in theatre lobbies, while Sallust was getting expelled from the Senate on morality charges, Nepos was writing biographies of dead Greek generals and keeping his head down. This is not cowardice -- it is the entirely defensible position of a man who understood that literature survives better than politics. He was right. His biographies are still read. Most of his contemporaries' political careers are footnotes.

## Main Works

- **De Viris Illustribus** (*On Famous Men*) -- originally at least sixteen books covering generals, historians, kings, orators, poets, and philosophers, comparing Greek and Roman exemplars in parallel. The vast majority is lost. What survives: the complete (or nearly complete) *Liber de Excellentibus Ducibus Exterarum Gentium* (twenty-three lives of foreign, mainly Greek, commanders, including Miltiades, Themistocles, Alcibiades, Hannibal, and Epaminondas); the *Vita Catonis* and *Vita Attici* from the book on Latin historians. [Fragmentary -- roughly 25 lives survive of what was originally a much larger work]
- **Chronica** (*Chronicles*) -- three books of universal history from earliest times to Nepos's own era, the work Catullus mentions in his dedication. The first sustained attempt in Latin to place Roman history in a global chronological framework. [Lost]
- **Exempla** (*Examples*) -- at least five books of collected anecdotes illustrating moral and practical lessons, probably similar in spirit to *De Viris Illustribus*. Cited by later authors. [Lost]
- **Love poems** -- mentioned by Pliny the Elder, who seems to have found them rather racy. [Lost]

## Style and Difficulty

Nepos is one of the most accessible Latin prose authors that survives, which is partly why he has been a school text for centuries and partly why literary critics have never known quite what to do with him. His sentences are short, his vocabulary is standard classical, his syntax is paratactic: subject, verb, object, next sentence. There is almost no periodic architecture, almost no subordination for its own sake, very little of the rhetorical elaboration that makes Cicero demanding. A student who has worked through Caesar and wants something with more biographical interest and less military logistics will find Nepos a comfortable next step. **Lexicon: LOW; Syntactic complexity: LOW; Stylistic figures: LOW; Allusion density: LOW.**

This does not mean Nepos is entirely without surprises. His Latin has been described by ancient and modern critics alike as occasionally provincial -- a slight Gallic tinge, some non-classical constructions, choices that classical purists would flag. Quintilian notes his usage without enthusiasm. None of this rises to the level of genuine difficulty; it is more a reminder that "correct" classical Latin was a prestige standard centered on Rome, and that writers from the provinces sometimes diverged from it in ways that were not yet fully ironed out. For a modern reader, the oddities are minor and rarely impede comprehension.

What makes Nepos genuinely worth reading -- and what the tier system does not fully capture -- is the quality of his subject matter. The lives are short, sometimes frustratingly so (he is aware of this and apologizes for it in the preface), but they are carefully chosen and economically told. The life of Epaminondas is the clearest example: Nepos's Latin is as transparent as glass, and what you see through it is one of the most attractive characters in all of ancient biography -- a man of complete integrity, no money, no wife, no children, whose only legacy was a single battle that freed Greece. The simplicity of the prose suits the simplicity of the man. It is not clear whether that is art or luck, but the effect is real.

## Latin Excerpt, Translation and Analysis

**Latin text** *(The Latin Library, thelatinlibrary.com)*

> At Epaminondas cum animadverteret mortiferum se vulnus accepisse simulque, si ferrum, quod ex hastili in corpore remanserat, extraxisset, animam statim emissurum, usque eo retinuit, quoad renuntiatum est vicisse Boeotios. Id postquam audivit, 'Satis' inquit 'vixi; invictus enim morior.' Tum ferro extracto confestim exanimatus est.
>
> Hic uxorem numquam duxit. In quo cum reprehenderetur, quod liberos non relinqueret, a Pelopida, qui filium habebat infamem, maleque eum in eo patriae consulere diceret, 'Vide', inquit 'ne tu peius consulas, qui talem ex te natum relicturus sis. Neque vero stirps potest mihi deesse. Namque ex me natam relinquo pugnam Leuctricam, quae non modo mihi superstes, sed etiam immortalis sit necesse est.'

*(Epaminondas IX.3--4 and X.1--2)*

**English translation** *(original)*

> But Epaminondas, realizing he had received a mortal wound and knowing that if he drew out the iron from the spear-shaft that remained lodged in his body he would immediately breathe his last, held it in place until word was brought that the Boeotians had won. When he heard this, he said: "I have lived long enough; for I die undefeated." Then, the iron drawn out, he died at once.
>
> He never married. When he was reproached for this on the grounds that he left no children -- by Pelopidas, who had a son of bad reputation and said that Epaminondas was failing his duty to his country -- he replied: "Take care that it is not you who fail it worse, since you will leave behind a son like that. Nor can I lack for descendants. I leave behind me, born of me, the Battle of Leuctra, which will not only outlast me but must of necessity be immortal."

**Analysis**

These two passages contain the most quoted lines in Nepos and together illustrate both what he does well and how he does it. *Satis vixi; invictus enim morior* -- "I have lived long enough; for I die undefeated" -- is one of the great exits in ancient literature, and Nepos delivers it in six words of utterly plain Latin. No rhetorical figures, no subordination, no period: a subject, a verb, a connective, another subject, an adverb, another verb. The power is entirely in the content. Nepos understood, better than many more ambitious writers, that some moments do not need ornamentation.

The second passage shows a different skill: the reporting of a witty comeback. *Ex me natam relinquo pugnam Leuctricam* -- "I leave behind me, born of me, the Battle of Leuctra" -- is a brilliant retort to Pelopidas, whose own son was notorious enough to make the reproach somewhat reckless. Nepos sets up the exchange economically: the charge, the parry, the counter-blow. The *necesse est* closing the period is the only moment the syntax reaches toward something more elaborate, and it earns its place: immortality needs a logical connective. Note also the gentle sting buried in the setup -- Nepos identifies Pelopidas's son as *infamem* in a single word and moves on, letting the reader do the arithmetic.

For the reader studying Latin, these passages offer a useful counterpoint to Caesar: the same clarity, the same economy, a completely different subject matter. The main analytical tool needed here is not grammatical but contextual: knowing that Leuctra (371 BC) was the moment Thebes broke Sparta's military dominance for good changes the weight of *pugnam Leuctricam* from a proper noun to a monument. Nepos's prose gives you the words; you supply the history.

## Final Rating

**Tier: D (Good Exercise)**

Nepos is Latin without obstacles: standard classical vocabulary, short paratactic sentences, no archaisms, no compressed philosophical argument, no allusion requiring specialist knowledge to unpack. He is the author you assign when the goal is confident reading rather than demanding translation. The biographical genre is the most accessible prose register in the ancient world, and Nepos inhabits it with complete ease. The occasional provincial oddity noted by ancient critics is a footnote, not a difficulty. Pair him with Caesar for a course on clean, unpretentious Latin -- the contrast in subject matter (campaigns vs. character portraits) keeps both authors interesting.

# QUINTUS HORTENSIUS HORTALUS (114--50 BC)

## Biography

Quintus Hortensius Hortalus was born in 114 BC, probably in Rome, into an equestrian family of no exceptional distinction. That did not slow him down. He delivered his first speech in the Forum at the age of nineteen, and it caused an immediate sensation. Cicero, who was fifteen at the time and still collecting cases for his rhetorical education, would later recall that Hortensius debuted to the unanimous admiration of everyone present -- not a promising beginning, but a fully formed performance. For the next two decades, Hortensius was the undisputed leading advocate at the Roman bar. No one could compete with him. This is the context that makes his later rivalry with Cicero worth understanding: when Cicero arrived, Hortensius was not a champion being challenged. He was an institution being dismantled.

The style that made Hortensius dominant was what Rome called *Asianism* -- the florid, theatrical, rhythmically elaborate school of Greek rhetoric developed in the cities of Asia Minor and imported into Latin advocacy. Asianist oratory was everything that spare Attic prose was not: long periodic sentences with carefully wrought endings, elaborate word-coloring, vivid metaphor, and emotional intensity calibrated to maximum effect. Hortensius handled all of this with a performer's instinct. He modulated his voice like a musician moving through registers, arranged his toga before each appearance with the deliberate care of an actor, and gestured so expressively that contemporaries compared the experience of watching him to watching a theatrical dance. This last quality provoked the most famous insult ever directed at a Roman advocate.

During shared oratorical exercises in their youth, Lucius Torquatus, watching Hortensius perform yet another sweeping gesture, called out that he was not an orator at all but "Dionysia" -- the stage name of a famous female dancer of the time. It was meant to be devastating. Hortensius's reply was instant: he said he would far rather be Dionysia than what Torquatus was -- a man utterly unknown to the Muses, to Venus, and to Bacchus. In other words: uncultivated, loveless, and sober. The audience apparently loved this, and the anecdote circulated for generations. Cicero included it in the *Brutus*. The nickname "Dionysia" stuck to Hortensius in Roman gossip, though whether as a compliment or an insult seems to have depended on who was telling the story.

The collapse of his supremacy came in 70 BC, during the trial of Gaius Verres, the former governor of Sicily, who had spent his tenure looting the island with systematic thoroughness -- its temples, its private collections, its treasury. Cicero was brought in as prosecutor; Hortensius, the finest advocate money could retain, appeared for the defense. Hortensius was confident, reasonably so: the trial was set to run late in the year, when courts would close, forcing a retrial with a potentially more favorable jury. His plan was to talk. Cicero did not let him. Instead of opening with a long prosecutorial oration -- which would have eaten time and given Hortensius his stage -- Cicero spent five days presenting nothing but evidence: witnesses from Sicily, ledger books, sworn inventories of looted goods, one devastating document after another. Hortensius found himself with nothing to argue against. Verres read the room, did the arithmetic, and fled into voluntary exile before the second actio could begin. Cicero published the second actio speeches anyway -- five enormous orations on art theft and provincial corruption that Hortensius never had to answer, which may be why they survive in such good condition. The career of the most celebrated orator of his generation had been ended not by a more brilliant argument but by more efficient preparation.

What followed is, in its way, more interesting than the rivalry itself. Hortensius accepted the verdict gracefully, and the two men became genuine friends. They appeared on the same side in subsequent cases, exchanged books, and maintained what Cicero in the *Brutus* describes as a relationship of mutual support and encouragement rather than competitive antagonism. Hortensius even turned to writing erotic poetry in his later years, presumably under the influence of the neoteric poets whose aesthetic he had no business sharing. A few fragments survive -- barely. One imagines Catullus reading them and being diplomatically silent. He died in 50 BC, two years before the civil war that would kill so many of his contemporaries, and was mourned by Cicero with what seems to have been genuine grief. He was also mourned, it is reported, far less sincerely at home, where he had been known to weep at the death of one of his ornamental fish without displaying comparable emotion at the death of his wife. He kept elaborate *piscinae* -- fish ponds -- at his Bauli villa near Baiae, and Cicero, who found this combination of luxury and emotional misalignment reliably absurd, gave him and his fellow fish-pond enthusiasts the collective nickname *piscinarii*.

The most remarkable chapter in the family history came after his death. In 43 BC, the Second Triumvirate -- Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus -- needed funds to prosecute the war against Brutus and Cassius, and decreed that the fourteen hundred wealthiest women in Rome would be subject to a special war tax. The women organized. They sent delegations to Octavia and Livia, who received them politely; they then tried Antony's mother Fulvia, who turned them away from the door. At that point Hortensia, daughter of Hortensius, walked to the Rostra in the Forum and gave a speech. She argued that women could not be made to fund a civil war in which they had no vote, no political role, and no voice -- that if the state wanted their property, it owed them, at minimum, the right to object. The triumvirs ordered their lictors to remove her. The crowd reacted badly enough that the lictors backed off. The next day the tax was reduced from fourteen hundred women to four hundred. Quintilian read Hortensia's speech and praised it not as a curiosity but as genuinely worth reading on its own merits. The speech has not survived. Her father, who made his reputation by speaking for clients who could pay, might have found something to admire in a daughter who spoke for herself.

## Main Works

- **Speeches** [Lost]: Hortensius argued hundreds of cases across a forty-year career, but no speech survived antiquity in complete form. Cicero notes in the *Brutus* that his written texts were disappointing compared to his performances -- the Asianist style depended so heavily on delivery, voice, and gesture that the words on the page lost most of their effect when the man was not there to animate them. This is the most eloquent verdict possible on both his talent and his limitations.
- **Erotic poetry** [Fragmentary, c. three fragments]: In his later years Hortensius wrote verse in the neoteric manner. Ovid mentions it in the *Tristia*, apparently aware of it. What survives is almost nothing -- a few lines cited by grammarians for linguistic rather than literary reasons.

## Style and Difficulty

**Lexicon and Medium:** The problem with assessing Hortensius's style is immediately obvious: we cannot assess Hortensius's style. No complete text survives. What we have is Cicero's description of it in the *Brutus*, and Cicero was both his most sympathetic critic and his successful replacement, which makes him a complicated witness. What Cicero tells us is that Hortensius worked in the Asianist tradition: elaborate sentence-structures with studied rhythmic endings (*clausulae*), rich *colores* of verbal ornament, and periodic architecture designed to build emotional pressure and release it with precisely timed cadences. This was technically demanding oratory, and readers who encountered his texts without him apparently found them less compelling than audiences who had watched him perform.

**The delivery problem:** Cicero identifies this directly and without malice. Some orators, he writes, depend on the inflaming of the judge rather than the mere instruction of the judge -- their power is essentially performative. When the fire of delivery dies down, what remains is cold. Hortensius was, by this analysis, the most extreme case of a great advocate whose words only half-existed on the page. His poetry, similarly, exists as a rumor. NC is not a rating of difficulty but of inaccessibility: what we cannot read, we cannot rank.

## Latin Excerpt

The passage below is not by Hortensius -- nothing by him survives. It is from the opening of Cicero's *Brutus* (6), a dialogue on Roman oratory written in 46 BC as a deliberate memorial to his dead rival and colleague. Cicero wrote this line to explain why Hortensius, had he lived, would have grieved more than anyone else at the state of Rome.

**Source:** Cicero, *Brutus* 6, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/brut.shtml)

**Latin text**

> Etenim si viveret Q. Hortensius, cetera fortasse desideraret una cum reliquis bonis et fortibus civibus, hunc autem aut praeter ceteros aut cum paucis sustineret dolorem, cum forum populi Romani, quod fuisset quasi theatrum illius ingeni, voce erudita et Romanis Graecisque auribus digna spoliatum atque orbatum videret.

**English translation** *(original)*

> For indeed, if Quintus Hortensius were still alive, he would perhaps share all the other losses with the rest of Rome's good and brave citizens -- but this grief he would bear more than all the others, or alongside very few: to see the Forum of the Roman people, which had been, as it were, the theater of his genius, stripped and bereft of an educated voice worthy of Roman and Greek ears.

**Analysis**

Cicero opens the *Brutus* with this line for a reason: the entire dialogue is an act of grief for Hortensius, combined with a sustained argument that the tradition of Roman eloquence cannot die with him. The phrase *quasi theatrum illius ingeni* -- "as it were, the theater of his genius" -- is the most elegant tribute in Latin critical prose to an orator's relationship with his stage. The Forum was not merely where Hortensius worked; it was the space that gave his talent meaning and scale. Without an audience, without the physical space of advocacy, his kind of oratory could not exist at all. Cicero understood this, because he had beaten him in that same theater.

The sentence is a single period built on a conditional (*si viveret*) that immediately modulates: not a simple protasis-apodosis, but a conditional that becomes increasingly specific as it unfolds, adding *praeter ceteros aut cum paucis* (more than the rest, or with only a few) as a refinement of the pain, then the *cum*-clause that drives to the actual grief. The ablative of separation -- *spoliatum atque orbatum voce erudita* -- is emphatic and final. The doubling of the participles (*spoliatum atque orbatum*) is Ciceronian symmetry, reinforcing the totality of the loss. For the student of Latin, this sentence is a model of classical periodic style operating at full power with no room for misunderstanding: every clause is clear, every transition is marked, and the emotional weight is distributed precisely across the structure.

The point of including it here is that this is the closest we can get to Hortensius -- through the most famous pair of eyes that ever watched him speak.

## Final Rating

**Tier: NC (Not Comparable)**

No complete work by Hortensius survives in any genre. His speeches existed in written form in the early Empire -- Cicero quotes from some of them in the *Brutus* -- but none reached us. His poetry is a handful of lines cited for grammatical purposes. The NC rating is not a comment on his ability, which by ancient testimony was exceptional, but on the impossibility of reading him: you cannot rank what you cannot read. What we can say is that the intrinsic difficulty of Asianist oratory -- complex periodic structure, elaborate rhythmic endings, dense verbal ornament -- would have placed him somewhere in the B-A range, had any full text survived. As it is, Hortensius exists in Latin literary history as an absence: the voice that made the Forum its theater, and left no transcript.

# PUBLIUS NIGIDIUS FIGULUS (c. 98--45 BC)

## Biography

Publius Nigidius Figulus was born around 98 BC and died in exile around 45 BC. He was an equestrian from a respectable but not distinguished family, who rose to the praetorship in 58 BC and became one of the most learned men in Rome -- or, depending on your point of view, one of the most alarming. Aulus Gellius, writing in the second century AD, called him "second in learning only to Varro," and if you know how Gellius felt about Varro that is not a minor compliment. What set Nigidius apart from other learned Romans of his era was not the breadth of his knowledge -- Varro had more of that -- but its texture. Where Varro catalogued, Nigidius divined. Where Varro explained institutions, Nigidius explained the hidden relationships between words and things, the religious functions of Etruscan lightning, the astrological significance of the positions of stars, and the philosophical implications of the Pythagorean tradition that he was determined to revive in Rome. He was a scholar the way some people are prophets: with conviction that knowledge is not merely useful but dangerous.

The range of his intellectual interests was staggering and, in polite Roman society, slightly unsettling. He wrote extensively on Latin grammar -- a major work in at least twenty-eight books of *Commentarii grammatici*, still being mined by grammarians in the second century AD. He wrote on Etruscan religious discipline (*disciplina Etrusca*): the interpretation of lightning, of prodigies, of haruspicy. He wrote on astronomy and astrology, producing works on the Greek and "barbarian" (non-Greek) celestial spheres. He wrote on theology, on animals, and on Pythagorean philosophy. This combination -- rigorous grammar alongside astrological prediction and Pythagorean mysticism -- would have seemed perfectly coherent to Nigidius himself and deeply eccentric to almost everyone else. Cicero, who was his friend and respected his learning, nevertheless clearly found him somewhat bewildering.

The Pythagorean angle deserves its own paragraph. Pythagoreanism had been a force in southern Italy since the sixth century BC, but by Nigidius's time it had dwindled in Rome to an exotic curiosity. Nigidius tried to revive it as a living philosophical tradition, which in practice meant advocating for things that struck ordinary Romans as deeply strange: vegetarianism (or at minimum dietary restrictions), belief in the transmigration of souls, mathematical mysticism, and a set of ritual practices and taboos that Pythagorean communities observed. Cicero's dialogue *Hortensius* (now lost) has Nigidius as a character, and later writers referred to him as the founder of a neo-Pythagorean movement in Rome. Whether his attempt at a genuine revival gained any real following is doubtful. What is certain is that it gave him a reputation as a man who took the occult seriously, at a time when educated Romans were supposed to treat divination as a public institution useful for civic purposes, not as a genuine means of accessing hidden knowledge. Nigidius appears to have believed the latter.

The most famous anecdote about him concerns not grammar or Pythagoreanism but a birth. In 63 BC, during the Catilinarian crisis -- the most fraught moment in Cicero's consulship -- the senator Gaius Octavius arrived late to a Senate meeting because his wife had just given birth. The future emperor Augustus had entered the world. When the lateness of Octavius was noted and the cause explained, Nigidius Figulus, who was present and who had made something of a specialty of astrological calculation, asked for the exact hour of the birth. He received it, did his calculations, and announced to the assembled senators that a master of the world had been born. Suetonius, who preserves this story in the *Life of Augustus*, adds that the alarm this caused was considerable -- Octavius apparently attempted to expose the infant, and Nigidius had to dissuade him by pointing out that the child's destiny was already fixed and could not be undone by human intervention. Augustus eventually heard the story and, reportedly, was pleased by it. Whether Nigidius actually said this or whether the story was retroactively attributed to the most conveniently prophetic scholar present is impossible to determine, but the fact that it attached to Nigidius at all tells us something about his reputation.

His political end was the mirror image of Varro's. Both were learned polymaths, both Pompeian in sympathy, both caught on the wrong side of the civil war. Varro survived: he surrendered to Caesar in Spain, was pardoned, was given the library commission, outlived Caesar, outlived the proscriptions, and wrote into his nineties. Nigidius did not. He was exiled after Pharsalus, attempted through various intermediaries to obtain Caesar's clemency, and received no answer that was helpful. Cicero wrote to Caesar on his behalf -- a surviving letter (*Ad Familiares* IV.13) attests to the friendship and to the effort -- but Caesar did not recall him. He died in exile around 45 BC, a year before Caesar's assassination and decades before the master of the world whose birth he had supposedly predicted took power. It is one of history's more pointed ironies. He predicted Augustus and was killed, in effect, by the civil war that Augustus eventually ended.

## Main Works

- ***Commentarii grammatici*** [Fragmentary, c. 28 books]: The major grammatical work; extensively quoted by Gellius, Priscian, and other grammarians. Dealt with etymology, phonetics, morphology, and the meaning of words with a distinctively philosophical bent -- Nigidius was interested not just in how words worked but in why they worked that way.
- **Works on Etruscan discipline** [Lost or fragmentary]: Covered haruspicy, the interpretation of lightning (*fulgurales libri*), and prodigies. He was considered one of Rome's leading authorities on Etruscan religious knowledge.
- **Works on divination and astrology** (*Sphaera Graecanica*, *Sphaera Barbarica*) [Lost]: Astronomical and astrological works on the Greek and non-Greek celestial spheres; probably the basis for his reputation as an astrologer.
- **Philosophical and religious works** [Lost]: Pythagorean philosophy and theology; also works on animals (*De animalibus*) and on the gods (*De diis*).

## Style and Difficulty

**Lexicon and archaic interest:** What the Gellius quotations show us is a mind consistently drawn to the archaic layer of Latin -- old words, old forms, old distinctions that classical usage had smoothed over. In the passage on *religiosus*, Nigidius does not simply explain the word; he finds it in an ancient verse no one else bothered to preserve, then unpacks the morphological pattern (*vinosus*, *mulierosus*, *religiosus*) to show that the suffix carries a meaning that standard usage had forgotten. His grammar is not descriptive but archaeological: he digs into language looking for what it used to mean.

**The overall situation:** NC is the only honest rating. The fragments we have are almost all preserved by grammarians who quoted Nigidius for a specific point and moved on; we have no continuous text, no complete argument, no sense of how a full Nigidian exposition actually unfolded over pages rather than sentences. The *Commentarii grammatici* in particular would have been demanding -- the philosophical weight behind individual grammatical observations suggests a dense, allusion-rich prose -- but this is inference, not reading.

## Latin Excerpt

The passage below is from Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* X.9.1--2, where Gellius discusses the word *religiosus* and quotes Nigidius directly: first a verse from an old poem that Nigidius had preserved in his *Commentarii grammatici*, then Nigidius's own explanation of the morphological pattern.

**Source:** Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* X.9.1--2, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/gellius/gellius10.shtml)

**Latin text**

> Nigidius Figulus, homo, ut ego arbitror, iuxta M. Varronem doctissimus, in undecimo commentariorum grammaticorum versum ex antiquo carmine refert memoria hercle dignum:
>
> *religentem esse oportet, religiosus ne fuas,*
>
> cuius autem id carmen sit, non scribit. Atque in eodem loco Nigidius: "Hoc" inquit "inclinamentum semper huiuscemodi verborum, ut 'vinosus', 'mulierosus', 'religiosus', significat copiam quandam inmodicam rei, super qua dicitur. Quocirca 'religiosus' is appellabatur, qui nimia et superstitiosa religione sese alligaverat, eaque res vitio assignabatur."

**English translation** *(original)*

> Nigidius Figulus, a man who was, in my judgment, second in learning only to Marcus Varro, records in the eleventh book of his *Grammatical Commentaries* a verse from an ancient poem that is, by Hercules, worth remembering:
>
> *one ought to be scrupulous in religion, but must not become overly religious,*
>
> though he does not write whose poem it is. And in the same passage Nigidius says: "This suffix, in words of this kind -- such as 'vinosus', 'mulierosus', 'religiosus' -- always signifies a kind of immoderate excess of the thing in question. For this reason, the person called 'religiosus' was one who had bound himself to religion with excessive and superstitious devotion, and this was counted as a fault."

**Analysis**

Gellius introduces Nigidius with one of the most pointed descriptions in ancient scholarship: *iuxta M. Varronem doctissimus* -- "second only to Varro in learning." This is the ancient world's equivalent of a Wikipedia "See also." Then Nigidius does something that perfectly illustrates why he was remarkable: he preserves a line of ancient Latin verse (*religentem esse oportet, religiosus ne fuas*) that appears nowhere else in surviving literature, and he uses it to make a grammatical and theological argument simultaneously. The verse distinguishes *religens* (the participle: one who is observant, scrupulous, careful in religious duties) from *religiosus* (the adjective with the suffix *-osus*: one who is excessively, dangerously devoted). The suffix pattern -- *vinosus* is addicted to wine, *mulierosus* is obsessed with women, *religiosus* is a religious fanatic -- is Nigidius's key observation, and it is entirely correct.

The theological implication is quietly significant. To a Roman reader, the idea that *religiosus* was a pejorative -- that being excessively pious was a *vitium*, a fault, not a virtue -- was genuinely counterintuitive in one direction (the word had already begun shifting toward its later neutral or positive meaning, as Gellius himself notes) and utterly Roman in another. Roman religion was civic and practical; excess in either direction was suspect. Nigidius, the Pythagorean mystic who took the occult more seriously than any respectable Roman was supposed to, was here arguing on behalf of religious moderation. The irony is its own small portrait.

For the student encountering this passage, the main work is syntactic: the *ut ego arbitror* parenthetical, the *versum refert* construction with the verse itself, and the indirect statement in Nigidius's quoted words (*significat copiam... quocirca... eaque res vitio assignabatur*). The vocabulary is classical throughout, with the single archaic touch of *religens* itself -- which, as Nigidius would have noted with satisfaction, is exactly the point.

## Final Rating

**Tier: NC (Not Comparable)**

Nothing by Nigidius survives in complete form. His *Commentarii grammatici* existed in late antiquity and was mined by grammarians until it eventually disappeared; the fragments preserved in Gellius and Priscian are tantalizing but discontinuous. His other works are known only by title and reputation. Per-line difficulty of what survives in Gellius suggests a B range -- archaizing vocabulary, philosophical density, awareness of older Latin forms -- but there is simply not enough to rank. The NC rating is also appropriate for a more fundamental reason: Nigidius's most characteristic work was probably his religious and astrological writing, which combined Latin prose with Etruscan technical terminology and Pythagorean philosophical language in ways we cannot reconstruct at all. What we are rating, if we were to rate him, would be a shadow of a corner of the actual text.

# MARCUS TULLIUS CICERO (106--43 BC)

## Biography

Marcus Tullius Cicero was born on January 3, 106 BC in Arpinum -- a small hill town in Latium, also (as he enjoyed pointing out) the birthplace of Gaius Marius, the general who had saved Rome from the Cimbri. Whether Cicero enjoyed the comparison because it gave him a distinguished local precedent for ambition, or because it allowed him to associate himself with a genuine military hero in an era when he had none of his own, is a question his biographers have debated. The answer is probably: both.

The family was equestrian, respectable, not distinguished. No consuls in his ancestry, no famous generals, no one who had held power in Rome. Cicero would be the first, and he never forgot it, and he never let anyone else forget it either. He was what Romans called a *homo novus* -- a new man, someone who had reached the consulship on talent alone with no family name to open doors. He found this a genuine source of pride and an inexhaustible source of anxiety, and the two emotions never quite separated in him.

His cognomen "Cicero" came, according to Plutarch, from an ancestor who had a wart-like protrusion on the tip of his nose shaped like a chickpea (*cicer*). When Cicero entered public life, his friends advised him to change the name -- a chickpea was no kind of name for a Roman statesman. Cicero declined. He said he would make the name Cicero more famous than Scaurus or Catulus (two of the most distinguished names in Roman politics). He was right. This is the first piece of evidence that Marcus Tullius Cicero had an ego the size of the Capitoline hill.

He was educated in Rome alongside Titus Pomponius (later known as Atticus, his lifelong friend) under the poet Archias, whose citizenship Cicero would later defend in a famous oration. He studied rhetoric and philosophy under the best teachers available: Philo of Larissa, head of the Platonic Academy, who gave him his lifelong attachment to Academic skepticism; Apollonius Molon of Rhodes, the most famous rhetorical teacher of the age; and the great jurist Quintus Mucius Scaevola, for Roman law. Cicero was not just diligent -- he was compulsive. He studied the way some people eat: constantly, with no apparent sense of being full.

His first major case, *Pro Roscio Amerino* (80 BC), was an act of courage that easily could have been an act of suicide. A man named Sextus Roscius of Ameria was accused of murdering his own father -- the charge backed by Chrysogonus, a freedman of Sulla, who had his own reasons for wanting the conviction (he had purchased Roscius's estate during the proscriptions and needed the son out of the way). Defending anyone against a Sullan freedman while Sulla was still dictator was genuinely dangerous. Cicero was twenty-six years old and took the case anyway. He won. Shortly thereafter, perhaps for health reasons, certainly also for safety reasons, he left for Athens and Rhodes to study for two years with Apollonius Molon and others.

He served as quaestor in Sicily in 75 BC and was, by his own account, an outstanding administrator of the grain supply -- fair, energetic, admired. He was so pleased with his performance that he expected Rome to be talking about him when he returned. He arrived at Puteoli and met a Roman acquaintance, who asked him cheerfully where he had been. "Sicily," said Cicero. The man seemed confused. "Oh -- Sicily? No one in Rome mentioned it." Cicero recorded his own mortification, and Plutarch preserved it for us, because sometimes the gods are kind. The experience humbled him. For a while.

Sicily came back productively four years later. In 70 BC, the Sicilians hired Cicero to prosecute Gaius Verres, the former governor who had spent three years stripping the island of everything portable and several things that weren't. Verres's defense counsel was Hortensius, the greatest orator in Rome. Cicero was supposed to lose. Instead, he prepared his evidence so thoroughly and presented it with such efficiency that Verres went into voluntary exile before Hortensius even had a chance to speak at the first session. Cicero published the five books of the *Actio Secunda* anyway, even though they were never delivered. There was already a visible pattern in his relationship with posterity.

He also discovered, during his time as quaestor in Sicily, the tomb of Archimedes in Syracuse. The Syracusans themselves had forgotten where it was. Cicero identified it by the sphere and cylinder carved on the stone -- the symbols of the mathematician's greatest theorem. The tomb was completely overgrown. He cleared it, read the inscription, and was very pleased with himself. He recounted this story in his *Tusculanae Disputationes* (V.64) with the air of a man who has found something the locals should have taken better care of.

The consulship of 63 BC was the peak of his public life, and he knew it, and he wrote about it for the next twenty years in a way that eventually began to irritate even his friends. When the Catilinarian conspiracy was revealed -- Lucius Sergius Catilina plotting to overthrow the Republic with an armed insurrection -- Cicero had Catiline denounced in the Senate four times, in four of the most famous speeches ever delivered in Latin. He executed the arrested conspirators without trial, which was legally questionable (they were Roman citizens) but politically popular at the time. He was hailed as *pater patriae*, father of the fatherland, in the Senate. He was forty-three years old and this was the greatest moment of his life.

To mark the occasion, he wrote a poem. He wrote a poem about his own consulship, which included the line *O fortunatam natam me consule Romam!* -- "O fortunate Rome, born in my consulship!" -- a verse so bad that it was mocked even in antiquity. It rhymes *natam* with *Romam*, which is the Latin equivalent of rhyming "nation" with "station" in a poem you have composed about yourself. The man who would write some of the finest Latin prose ever committed to papyrus also wrote some of the worst Latin verse, and chose to write it about himself. He apparently composed an epic poem about his own life as well, now lost. Its loss is one of antiquity's few mercies.

The political downfall came through a scandal that was not his fault but for which he paid anyway. In December 62 BC, the tribune Publius Clodius Pulcher was caught infiltrating the women-only festival of the Bona Dea, held at Caesar's house, disguised as a female lute-player. This was a religious sacrilege and a social scandal simultaneously. Clodius was put on trial and claimed to have been in Interamna at the time. Cicero testified that Clodius had dined with him in Rome just hours before the festival. The jury acquitted Clodius anyway: they had been bribed. Cicero was furious and made no secret of it. Clodius was furious and made even less secret of it.

In 58 BC, Clodius (now a tribune) passed a law targeting Cicero retroactively for the execution of the Catilinarians without trial. Cicero went into exile. His Palatine mansion was demolished and a temple to Liberty built on the site. He wrote letters from exile that were so painfully self-pitying that his friends eventually had to tell him to stop. He was recalled in 57 BC with public demonstrations of popular support that he described, with his usual restraint, as the greatest reception any Roman had ever received. His house was rebuilt at public expense.

He was now fifty years old and had survived Sulla's dictatorship, the Catilinarian crisis, and exile. The decade of the fifties was difficult: Caesar's rise, the First Triumvirate, the growing sense that the Republic was not going to survive. He wrote the *De Oratore*, which is about his own craft rather than about how to live, and he wrote philosophy: the *De Re Publica*, the *De Legibus*. He wrote orations. He wrote letters to Atticus that are among the most intimate and revealing documents to survive from the ancient world. He governed Cilicia (as proconsul, 51-50 BC) and did an honorable job; he wanted a triumph but never got one.
Underneath all of it lay a single idea, held with a consistency his enemies mistook for vanity: that Rome was held together not by written law but by the *mos maiorum*, the custom of the ancestors. This was an unwritten constitution, accumulated by practice rather than voted into existence, and it governed how a magistrate behaved, how the Senate was consulted, how a man treated his clients and his opponents. In the *De Re Publica* Cicero built his argument around a line of Ennius, *moribus antiquis res stat Romana virisque*, "the Roman state stands on its ancient customs and its men," and then made the uncomfortable observation that Rome was running short of both. The difficulty was structural. The *mos maiorum* had been shaped by a city-state that governed itself, and it was now being asked to govern an empire, in a world of armies loyal to their generals rather than to the Senate, of provincial fortunes large enough to buy an election, and of street violence used as an ordinary political instrument. Cicero spent his career insisting that the old rules still applied to a game whose pieces had changed.

There is an obvious oddity in a man from Arpinum appointing himself the guardian of ancestral tradition, since he had no ancestors inside it: no consuls, no triumphs, no wax masks in the atrium. He knew it, and he had a model. Cato the Elder had also come from a country town, had also been a *novus homo*, and had made himself into the living definition of the ancestral virtue that the old families assumed they owned. Cicero admired him enough to list him in the *Pro Archia* among the Romans worth imitating, and later to hand him the leading role in the *De Senectute*, which is the literary equivalent of letting the *mos maiorum* speak in its own voice. The irony is the one that ended his career. In 63 BC, in defence of that tradition, he had Roman citizens executed without trial, for which the tradition offered no clear precedent, and five years later a tribune used the tradition's own protections to drive him out of Italy for doing it.


Caesar's victory in the civil war left Cicero stranded: too implicated with Pompey to be welcome at Caesar's court, too prudent to resist openly. He retreated to philosophy again. Then, in 46 BC, he divorced Terentia -- his wife of thirty years -- under disputed circumstances (some ancient sources blame financial mismanagement during the exile, some blame his desire for a younger woman). He married Publilia almost immediately: a much younger woman, reportedly his former ward. His daughter Tullia died in February 45 BC. The blow was devastating. Cicero was inconsolable. He proposed building a temple to Tullia to deify her -- a project that his friends, carefully and persistently, talked him out of. When Publilia seemed insufficiently moved by Tullia's death, he divorced her too. He was fifty-nine years old, had been married twice and divorced twice in less than a year, and was channeling his grief into an enormous outpouring of philosophical writing. The period 46-44 BC produced the *De Finibus*, the *Tusculanae Disputationes*, the *De Natura Deorum*, the *De Amicitia*, the *De Senectute*, and several other works. If Cicero had not lost his daughter, Latin philosophy would be much poorer.

The obvious question about those two years is how anybody writes that much that fast, and the answer has a name. Marcus Tullius Tiro was Cicero's slave, freed in 53 BC, and he ran the whole literary machinery of the household: he took dictation, kept the archive, corrected the drafts, and is credited with devising the shorthand that made it possible. The *notae Tironianae* were signs standing for whole words and endings rather than for letters, which meant a writer could keep up with a speaking voice. Plutarch says that in December 63 BC, while the Senate was debating what to do with the Catilinarian conspirators, Cicero stationed clerks trained in such signs around the chamber to take down Cato's speech -- the earliest recorded use of shorthand at Rome, and the reason anyone knows what was actually said. Cicero worked by talking. He dictated, Tiro wrote, and the philosophical works of 45 and 44 BC were produced at a rate that only that arrangement makes plausible. Book XVI of the *Ad Familiares* is the file of letters to Tiro, and it contains the warmest things Cicero ever wrote to anybody.

Caesar's assassination in 44 BC briefly reignited his political life. He had not been part of the conspiracy -- Brutus and Cassius knew he would be unreliable with a secret -- but he supported it enthusiastically afterward. He then delivered the fourteen Philippic orations against Mark Antony: the most sustained campaign of personal rhetorical destruction in Latin literature. The Second Philippic, never delivered but published as a pamphlet, is probably the most annihilating personal attack ever written in Latin prose. Antony reciprocated in the only way available to him: when the Second Triumvirate was formed in 43 BC, Cicero's name went on the proscription list.

He tried to flee by sea. The winds turned against him. He returned to his Formian villa and met the soldiers on December 7, 43 BC. His head and hands were cut off and displayed on the Rostra -- the speaker's platform from which he had addressed Rome for decades. Antony's wife Fulvia (formerly Clodius's wife, a small circle of revenge) reportedly drove a pin through his tongue. The story may not be true. It is the kind of story that sticks.

Twenty-three years later, Cicero's son -- also named Marcus -- was consul. He announced the defeat of Antony and Cleopatra to the Senate. The man who had ordered his father's death was dead, and it was Cicero's son who said so. The gods, occasionally, have a sense of structure.

## Main Works

Cicero wrote in four modes: orator, philosopher, teacher of rhetoric, and correspondent. He was excellent at all four, and the scope of his output is staggering. What follows is selective. The philosophical works and the rhetorical works are listed apart because they are different projects: the first ask what a good life and a good state are, the second ask how a Roman learns to speak, and only the second are about the craft Cicero actually practised for a living.

**Orations** (more than 50 survive of perhaps 100 or more delivered)

- *Verrine Orations* (70 BC): seven speeches against the corrupt Sicilian governor Gaius Verres. Only the first was delivered in court; the vast *Actio Secunda* in five books was published after Verres fled into exile. A monument of invective and evidence-marshaling. [Complete]
- *In Catilinam I-IV* (63 BC): the four orations against Catiline. The most famous speeches in Latin literature. "Quo usque tandem..." [Complete]
- *Pro Milone* (52 BC): defense of Titus Annius Milo for the killing of Clodius on the Appian Way. Milo was convicted anyway; the published version (significantly revised) is considered Cicero's finest forensic achievement. [Complete]
- *Philippics I-XIV* (44-43 BC): against Mark Antony. The Second Philippic is the most ferocious personal attack in Latin literature. [Complete]

**Philosophical Works** (a selection from a very large catalogue, almost all of it written in two frantic years)

- *De Re Publica* (54-51 BC): on the ideal state. Badly damaged in transmission -- most of what we have was recovered in 1819 from a palimpsest, a manuscript scraped clean and written over with a commentary on the Psalms. Its sixth and final book, the *Somnium Scipionis*, survived by a completely different route, quoted whole inside Macrobius's commentary, and is usually read on its own.
- *Paradoxa Stoicorum* (46 BC): six Stoic paradoxes ("only the wise man is free", "only the good man is rich") argued as rhetorical set-pieces. Short, and the easiest philosophical Latin he wrote.
- *Tusculanae Disputationes* (45 BC): five dialogues on how to achieve happiness, written in the weeks after Tullia's death. Book I is on the fear of death, and it is not a detached performance.
- *De Finibus Bonorum et Malorum* (45 BC): a comprehensive survey of Epicurean, Stoic, and Peripatetic ethics. Dense and demanding.
- *De Natura Deorum* (45 BC): three books presenting Epicurean, Stoic, and Academic views on the gods, and refusing at the end to say which is right.
- *De Divinatione* (44 BC): whether prophecy, omens and dreams mean anything. Cicero the augur argues, in his own voice, that they do not.
- *De Fato* (44 BC): the sequel to *De Divinatione* and the last panel of the theological trilogy, on fate, free will and what is left up to us. Damaged at both ends. Its interlocutor is Aulus Hirtius, who has his own entry here.
- *De Senectute* (44 BC): on old age. Warm, witty, and genuinely felt.
- *De Amicitia* (44 BC): on friendship. Contains the famous definition of friendship as *consensio* of all divine and human things.
- *De Officiis* (44 BC): on duty, written for his son and finished as Antony was destroying him. The most influential prose work of antiquity in later Europe.

**Rhetorical Works** (the craft he actually practised, described from the inside)

- *De Oratore* (55 BC): on the ideal orator, in three books, and the best thing he wrote in any genre. A conversation set in 91 BC among the great orators of his youth. The most sophisticated treatment of rhetoric in Latin.
- *Brutus* (46 BC): a history of Roman oratory from its beginnings to Cicero himself, in the form of a dialogue. Our single most important source for the early Latin orators, most of whom survive nowhere else.
- *Orator* (46 BC): the portrait of the perfect orator, addressed to Brutus. Contains Cicero's fullest account of prose rhythm, the thing his own sentences are built on.
- *De Optimo Genere Oratorum* (46 BC): a short preface to a translation, and the origin of the argument about translating sense rather than words -- *non verbum pro verbo*.
- *Topica* (44 BC): on where to find arguments. Written from memory on a sea voyage, for a lawyer who had asked.

**Letters** (about 900 survive in four collections, most of them never meant for publication)

- *Epistulae ad Atticum* (16 books): to his closest friend Titus Pomponius Atticus. The most personal and candid body of correspondence in all of Latin.
- *Epistulae ad Familiares* (16 books): to various friends, associates, and political figures.
- *Epistulae ad Quintum Fratrem* (3 books): to his brother Quintus.
- *Epistulae ad Brutum* (2 books): to Marcus Brutus; the letters from the final months of Cicero's life.

## Style and Difficulty

**The Periodic Sentence**

Cicero is the master of the *oratio perpetua*: sustained periodic style in which a sentence builds through successive subordinate clauses until the final main verb resolves the accumulated tension. Think of it as musical composition. The period opens a theme, adds complications, creates counterpoint through relative clauses and participial phrases, and eventually lands on a *clausula* -- a rhythmically patterned ending (*esse videatur*, *posse videri*, and their relatives) that was the audible signature of elevated Latin prose. Educated Romans could hear these endings the way musicians hear a perfect cadence.

This commitment to hypotaxis -- the architectural principle of subordination -- is what most defines Cicero against his contemporaries. Where Caesar coordinates (*venit, vidit, vicit*: three main verbs, three separate actions), Cicero subordinates. Causal clauses, temporal clauses, indirect statement, purpose clauses, result clauses all nest within each other, creating a hierarchy of information the reader must hold suspended until the main clause arrives and organizes everything.

Cicero also practices *amplificatio*: saying the same thing two, three, or four times in different words. *Synonymia* (accumulation of near-synonyms) and *anaphora* (repetition of the same word or phrase at the start of successive clauses) are the most visible signs of his rhetorical training, and they are everywhere in the orations.

**The Difficulty Question**

Students approach Cicero with fear, and this is not entirely unreasonable. The sentences are long, the vocabulary is enormous, and his frequent use of indirect discourse and legal technicality can feel like a circular highway with no exits.

But here is the essential insight: Cicero's prose is one of the most *learnable* in all of Latin, once you understand how his sentences work. Translating Cicero is like assembling a puzzle where every piece is guaranteed to fit and fall into the correct position -- if you are slow and careful. The periodic structure, far from being arbitrary complexity, is a system. The subordinate clauses set up the main clause. The participial phrases attach to the nearest appropriate noun. The relative *qui* clause refers back to the nearest plausible antecedent. Patience and a solid command of Latin subordination are 80% of what you need. The remaining 20% is vocabulary.

By genre:

The **letters** -- especially the *Ad Atticum* -- are the most accessible: colloquial, often short-sentenced, full of Greek loan-words signaling informal register, written at conversational pace. Many of them are effectively text messages from antiquity. Tier C.

The **orations** are where most students encounter Cicero, and the Catilinarians and *Pro Caelio* are standard entry points for good reason. Hypotactic complexity is real but tractable: Cicero wants to persuade, which means he wants to be understood. Longer, denser speeches (*Verrine* set-pieces, *Second Philippic*) push higher. Overall: B to low A.

The **rhetorical works** sit between the two. They are dialogues, so the give and take keeps the sentences shorter than a treatise would, and the subject is one the reader is already inside: this is Cicero describing the job he did every day. The technical vocabulary is real but finite and worth learning, since it is the vocabulary these notes use. *De Oratore* is the most demanding of them, *Topica* the least. Overall: B to low A.

The **philosophical works** are a different register entirely. Cicero had to invent Latin philosophical vocabulary -- *qualitas*, *essentia*, *beatitudo*, *moralis*, *humanitas* are Ciceronian coinages or adaptations -- meaning that even an advanced reader will encounter lexicon that cannot be found in the usual dictionaries. The argumentation requires tracking multiple Greek philosophical schools simultaneously, and the allusion density to Greek originals is high and increasing. *De Finibus* and *Tusculanae Disputationes* are genuinely difficult. Overall: A.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: Oration -- *In Catilinam* I.1-2

**Source:** Cicero, *Oratio in L. Catilinam Prima*, sections [1]-[2], from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/cat1.shtml)

**Context:** November 8, 63 BC. Cicero as consul has uncovered the details of the Catilinarian conspiracy. He summons Catiline to appear before the Senate in the Temple of Jupiter Stator -- and Catiline actually shows up. Cicero addresses the conspirator to his face, in front of the entire Senate.

**Latin text**

> [1] Quo usque tandem abutere, Catilina, patientia nostra? quam diu etiam furor iste tuus nos eludet? quem ad finem sese effrenata iactabit audacia? Nihilne te nocturnum praesidium Palati, nihil urbis vigiliae, nihil timor populi, nihil concursus bonorum omnium, nihil hic munitissimus habendi senatus locus, nihil horum ora voltusque moverunt? Patere tua consilia non sentis, constrictam iam horum omnium scientia teneri coniurationem tuam non vides? Quid proxima, quid superiore nocte egeris, ubi fueris, quos convocaveris, quid consilii ceperis, quem nostrum ignorare arbitraris? [2] O tempora, o mores! Senatus haec intellegit. Consul videt; hic tamen vivit. Vivit? immo vero etiam in senatum venit, fit publici consilii particeps, notat et designat oculis ad caedem unum quemque nostrum. Nos autem fortes viri satis facere rei publicae videmur, si istius furorem ac tela vitemus. Ad mortem te, Catilina, duci iussu consulis iam pridem oportebat, in te conferri pestem, quam tu in nos machinaris.

**English translation** *(original)*

> [1] How long, Catilina, will you go on abusing our patience? How much longer will your madness mock us? Where will your unbridled arrogance push itself? Has nothing moved you -- not the nightly guard on the Palatine, not the city watches, not the people's fear, not the assembly of all good citizens, not this most heavily fortified place of the Senate's meeting, not the faces and expressions of these men? Do you not feel that your plans have been exposed, do you not see that your conspiracy is already gripped and held fast by the knowledge of everyone here? What you did last night and the night before, where you were, who you summoned, what decision you took: is there anyone among us you think does not know? [2] O what times, O what customs! The Senate knows this. The consul sees it; and yet this man lives. Lives? He does more -- he comes to the Senate, he takes part in public deliberations, he marks out each one of us with his eyes for murder. We, brave men, seem to do enough for the Republic if we dodge his rage and his weapons. Long since, Catilina, you should have been led to death by order of the consul; the ruin you are plotting against us should by now have fallen on you.

**Analysis**

The three opening words -- *Quo usque tandem* -- are among the most famous in Latin prose. *Quo usque* means "how far" or "how long"; *tandem*, usually "at last" or "finally," here functions as an intensifier implying that the patience of Rome has been stretched past any reasonable limit. The question is not really a question; it is an accusation in interrogative clothing.

The sentence builds through triple anaphora: *quo usque... quam diu... quem ad finem* -- three parallel questions, each synonymous, each escalating in emotional temperature. This is Cicero's characteristic technique of *tricolon crescens*, saying the same thing three ways so that the meaning approaches from multiple angles and lands harder.

What follows is the rhetorical climax of the opening: the seven *nihil* clauses. *Nihilne te... nihil... nihil... nihil... nihil... nihil... nihil moverunt?* Seven things that should have moved Catiline, listed in strict parallel series, each a further witness to the conspirator's brazenness. No pause, no explanatory gloss, seven consecutive items hammered home. Then: *moverunt?* -- the single verb and the question mark. Cicero's deadpan shrug.

*O tempora, o mores!* -- after the controlled anaphora of the opening, this sudden shift to a two-part exclamation is a shock of simplicity. Two brief exclamations, both vocative, addressing not Catiline but the age itself. In context, the change of register -- from hammering accumulation to a two-item outcry -- is a genuine rhetorical stroke. The sentence has been rubbed smooth by repetition, but it lands differently when you have just waded through the seven *nihil* clauses before it.

For vocabulary: *abutere* (from *abutor*, to misuse, abuse; a deponent taking the ablative: "you abuse our patience"); *effrenata* (unbridled, unrestrained); *praesidium* (guard, garrison, protection); *voltus* (archaic variant of *vultus*, face, expression); *constrictam... teneri* (held gripped, held fast; *teneri* is passive infinitive of *teneo*); *coniurationem* (conspiracy, from *coniuro*, to swear together); *particeps* (participant, partner; takes the genitive: *publici consilii*); *pestem* (plague, ruin, destruction -- loaded political vocabulary).

The sentence lengths themselves teach the strategy: the three opening questions are each a few words; the seven *nihil* clauses are medium length and strictly parallel; the *Patere... non vides* section switches to statements; then *O tempora, o mores!* is tiny; then the periodic climax. Length and structure are rhetoric.

### Excerpt 2: Letter -- *Ad Atticum* I.16 (excerpt)

**Source:** Cicero, *Epistulae ad Atticum* I.16, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/att1.shtml)

**Context:** June/July 61 BC. Clodius has just been acquitted of the charge of sacrilege -- entering the Bona Dea festival disguised as a woman -- despite Cicero having testified against his alibi. The acquittal was obtained by bribery. Cicero writes to Atticus, furious and sardonically amused at the same time. The passage is one of the sharpest accounts of judicial corruption in ancient literature, and also one of the funniest.

**Latin text**

> Biduo per unum servum et eum ex ludo gladiatorio confecit totum negotium; arcessivit ad se, promisit, intercessit, dedit. Iam vero (o di boni, rem perditam!) etiam noctes certarum mulierum atque adulescentulorum nobilium introductiones non nullis iudicibus pro mercedis cumulo fuerunt. Ita summo discessu bonorum, pleno foro servorum, XXV iudices ita fortes tamen fuerunt, ut summo proposito periculo vel perire maluerint quam perdere omnia. XXXI fuerunt, quos fames magis quam fama commoverit. Quorum Catulus cum vidisset quendam, "Quid vos," inquit, "praesidium a nobis postulabatis? an, ne nummi vobis eriperentur, timebatis?"

**English translation** *(original)*

> In two days, through a single slave -- and one from a gladiatorial school, at that -- he wrapped up the whole business: summoned them to him, made promises, interceded, paid out. And what is more -- O good gods, what a wretched affair! -- even evenings spent with certain women and with noble young men were laid on for some of the jurors as a bonus on top of their fee. And so, with every decent man gone and the Forum full of slaves, twenty-five jurors were nonetheless brave enough to prefer, at the gravest personal risk, to die rather than sell everything out. Thirty-one were moved more by hunger than by reputation. When Catulus caught sight of one of them afterward, he said: "Why did you ask us for a guard? Were you afraid someone would steal your money?"

**Analysis**

The contrast with the oration excerpt is almost violent in its completeness. This is Cicero writing to his closest friend, at speed, with no performance in mind. The sentences are short and punchy. The word order is colloquial. The parenthetical interjection -- *o di boni, rem perditam!* -- is not rhetoric; it is a private cry. "O good gods, a ruined affair!" There is no periodic structure, no anaphora, no clausula. This is a man in his study telling his friend what happened, using the vocabulary of controlled outrage.

*Biduo per unum servum et eum ex ludo gladiatorio*: the compression is itself the indignation. In two days. Through a single slave. A slave from a *ludus gladiatorius* -- a gladiatorial training school. The moral quality of the agent is encoded in the noun: this is who you use when you need to buy a jury. *Confecit totum negotium*: wrapped up the whole business. The bureaucratic phrase (*negotium conficere*) applied to judicial corruption is Ciceronian irony at its most understated.

The vote count -- XXV and XXXI -- is exact and sour. Twenty-five honest jurors, thirty-one corrupt ones. *Fames magis quam fama commoverit*: hunger moved them more than reputation. The alliterative pair (*fames/fama*) is both phonetically precise and morally devastating. The word *fama* is almost self-satirizing here from a man famously obsessed with his own reputation: these thirty-one judges simply did not care about theirs.

Catulus's parting remark -- "Were you afraid someone would steal your money?" -- is the kind of line you can only write in a private letter when you are still shaking with fury. It is the exact rejoinder to a jury that had requested a guard: the guard was presumably to protect their bribe money, since their judicial integrity was evidently not the thing at risk. Two days, one gladiatorial-school slave, and the justice system of the Roman Republic was for sale.

Grammar notes: *arcessivit* (from *arcesso*, to summon; perfect indicative); *pro mercedis cumulo* (as a supplement to the fee -- *cumulus* literally means a heap or addition piled on top); *summo discessu bonorum* (ablative absolute: with the complete departure of good men); *vel perire maluerint quam perdere omnia* (they preferred to die rather than to lose everything -- *malo + infinitive* construction); *quos fames... commoverit* (relative clause with perfect subjunctive, characterizing the thirty-one: people whom hunger moved more than reputation).

### Excerpt 3: Philosophical Work -- *De Amicitia* 20

**Source:** Cicero, *Laelius de Amicitia* [20], from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/cicero/amic.shtml)

**Context:** Written in 44 BC, the final year of Cicero's life. The dialogue is presented as Laelius's reflections on friendship, set after the death of Scipio Aemilianus. This passage contains the most famous definition of friendship in Western literature. It is worth noting that Cicero wrote it at sixty-two years old, recently widowed of his daughter, twice divorced in a year, and watching the Republic disintegrate. Under the circumstances, what he says about friendship is not just philosophy.

**Latin text**

> Est enim amicitia nihil aliud nisi omnium divinarum humanarumque rerum cum benevolentia et caritate consensio; qua quidem haud scio an excepta sapientia nihil melius homini sit a dis immortalibus datum.

**English translation** *(original)*

> Friendship is nothing other than agreement on all divine and human things, bound together by goodwill and affection; and, with wisdom perhaps excepted, I am not sure whether anything better has been given to mankind by the immortal gods.

**Analysis**

One sentence. Thirty-four Latin words. Cicero needed exactly one sentence, and this is what he did with it.

*Est enim amicitia nihil aliud nisi...* is the classical definition formula: friendship is nothing other than X. What follows the *nisi* is one of the most carefully balanced formulations in Cicero's philosophical prose.

*Omnium divinarum humanarumque rerum*: of all divine and human things. The chiastic double genitive (*divinarum humanarumque*) gestures at totality -- everything above and below, everything in the cosmos and in daily life. *Cum benevolentia et caritate consensio*: agreement, or harmony, reached through goodwill (*benevolentia*) and deep affection (*caritas*). The word *consensio* deserves close attention: it is not merely "agreement" in the propositional sense, but a deeper resonance -- a co-feeling, a harmony of minds responding to the same things.

The second clause performs the Ciceronian Academic hedge: *haud scio an* (I do not know whether -- a formula that typically introduces a confident claim in polite disguise) *excepta sapientia* (with wisdom perhaps excepted -- an ablative absolute doing the quiet work of qualification) *nihil melius homini sit a dis immortalibus datum*. Nothing better has been given to mankind by the immortal gods. The effect places friendship just below wisdom in the entire catalogue of divine gifts: second in the universe, with a caveat that even first place is not quite certain.

The difficulty of this sentence lies not in its length but in its compression: thirty-four words carry a complete ethical argument. Students accustomed to long periodic sentences sometimes find the compressed philosophical aphorism harder, because the syntactic machinery is less visible. The work here is: recognizing *nisi* as introducing an exception after *nihil aliud*; understanding *consensio* as the predicate noun completing *amicitia est*; reading *cum benevolentia et caritate* as the ablative of accompaniment defining how the *consensio* holds; and then unpacking the relative clause (*qua*) that follows the definition.

Vocabulary: *nisi* here = "other than," after *nihil aliud*; *consensio* (harmony, resonance, agreement of souls -- stronger than *consensus*); *benevolentia* (goodwill, warm stable inclination toward another's good); *caritas* (deep affection, regard -- not romantic love but warm, stable regard); *haud scio an* (I am inclined to think, I suspect -- a softening formula preceding what is actually a confident claim); *excepta sapientia* (ablative absolute: with wisdom excepted); *a dis immortalibus datum* (given by the immortal gods).

## Legacy and Impact

Cicero was dead before he had any idea what he had made. His letters, published after his execution by his freedman Tiro, were the first private correspondence ever presented as literature in the Western tradition. They created a genre: the epistle as self-revelation, the letter as the truest version of the person who wrote it. And in creating it, they preserved Cicero whole -- not the polished orator of the Catilinarians, but the frightened exile, the grieving father, the man who divorced two wives in a year, the writer who could not stop talking about his consulship, the politician who was vain, terrified, brilliant, petty, courageous, and calculating sometimes in the same letter on the same day.

Tiro's shorthand outlasted everything else that came out of that household. The *notae* were expanded by later hands -- Isidore of Seville reports that the collection eventually reached some five thousand signs -- and they became the working script first of the imperial chancery and then of the medieval scriptorium, where Merovingian and Carolingian scribes used them for notes, glosses and subscriptions; the surviving lexica of them run to thousands of entries. They faded after the eleventh century, by which time scribes who could no longer read them had started to suspect they were a cipher, or magic. Two fossils are still in daily use. One is the word *notarius*, which originally meant not a man of law but a shorthand writer, a man who wrote *notae*, and which drifted across the Middle Ages into the modern notary. The other is a single sign: the Tironian *et*, written as ⁊, which is still printed on Irish road signs in place of *agus*, "and". Modern shorthand -- Bright in 1588, Willis in 1602, then Pitman in 1837 and Gregg in 1888 -- was invented afresh rather than inherited, but its authors knew exactly whose precedent they were claiming. Tiro is the reason the idea of writing at the speed of a voice was thinkable in the first place.

The philosophical works performed a different miracle: they became the primary channel through which Greek philosophy entered the Latin-speaking world. The vocabulary problem was severe -- Greek had a rich philosophical language; Latin had almost none, because the Romans had not traditionally done Greek-style abstract philosophy. Cicero invented the missing words. *Qualitas* (quality), *quantitas* (quantity), *essentia* (essence), *moralis* (moral), *humanitas* (a characteristically Ciceronian concept: the cultivation of our human qualities through education and culture), *beatitudo* (happiness, blessedness) -- all Ciceronian coinages or adaptations. The vocabulary of Western philosophy and ethics, in Latin and in all the languages derived from it, is substantially Ciceronian. When Thomas Aquinas writes about virtue, when the founders of the American Republic write about *res publica*, they are using Cicero's language whether they know it or not.

Then there is Petrarch. In 1345, the humanist poet Francesco Petrarch was searching the cathedral library in Verona when he found a manuscript of Cicero's *Epistulae ad Atticum*. He had known the orations and some of the philosophical works. He had not known the letters. He read them and found a Cicero he had not expected: anxious, emotional, obsessively engaged with day-to-day politics, deeply human. He wrote Cicero a letter in response -- in Latin, addressed to the dead man -- both praising his genius and gently reproving him for having sacrificed philosophical retirement to political entanglement. Petrarch was not just reading a dead author; he was having a conversation with him.

This act is often taken as one of the founding moments of Renaissance humanism. What Petrarch discovered in the *Ad Atticum* was that the past was not a museum; it was a conversation interrupted by death. Cicero had written those letters to a specific friend about specific events, and they could still be read, still argued with, still felt. The discovery energized Renaissance scholarship and Latin writing for the next century and a half. Erasmus wrote a dialogue mocking the most extreme Ciceronians (*Ciceronianus*, 1528) for refusing to use any word not found in Cicero. Even the mockery is a testament to how central Cicero had become.

Even today, the standard school trajectory in classical Latin passes through Caesar and Cicero in sequence. Caesar teaches the learner to read Latin that is radically clear. Cicero teaches the learner what Latin can do when someone is trying to move an audience, define a concept, or tell a friend what happened. In the most literal sense, Cicero is still the author who tells most readers what Latin is for.

## Final Rating

**Tier: A (Very Difficult) -- Low End**

The placement reflects the aggregate of all Cicero wrote, with the philosophical works pulling the rating upward from where the orations alone would place him.

The **letters** are C tier: colloquial, often short-sentenced, immediate, and accessible to an intermediate reader. The *Ad Atticum* is the most candid and in many ways the most human text in the entire Latin corpus, and also one of the easier ones.

The **orations** sit in the B to low A range. Hypotactic complexity is real but tractable: Cicero wants to be understood, which means the structure, however elaborate, always serves comprehension rather than defeating it. The major Catilinarians and *Pro Caelio* are manageable entry points. Longer, denser speeches push higher.

The **philosophical works** justify the A rating decisively. Cicero's coined philosophical vocabulary is non-standard and cannot be easily looked up. The argumentation requires tracking multiple philosophical schools simultaneously. Allusion density to Greek originals is high. *De Finibus* and *Tusculanae Disputationes* are A-tier texts by any measure.

The "low A" modifier matters: Cicero, even at his most demanding, is never gratuitously difficult. He is always trying to reach the reader, always signposting his structure, always placing his clausulae to signal where the sentence is going. The puzzle always has the right number of pieces. The work is learning to see them.

# GAIUS JULIUS CAESAR (100--44 BC)

## Biography

Born 100 BC in Rome to a patrician family of the gens Julia, which traced its lineage back to Aeneas and, through him, to the goddess Venus herself. Caesar was not born a god, but he spent his career making a reasonable case for the upgrade.

His family was aristocratic but not particularly wealthy or powerful. His aunt Julia was married to Gaius Marius, the great populist general and six-time consul; his father was a praetor who died suddenly when Caesar was sixteen. These connections -- to the popular faction, to the reform tradition -- shaped Caesar's politics for life.

**Pirates, ransoms, and revenge:** Around 75 BC, on his way to study rhetoric in Rhodes, Caesar was captured by Cilician pirates. They demanded a ransom of twenty talents. Caesar laughed at them. He told them they had dramatically underestimated his worth and insisted they demand fifty. While waiting to be ransomed, he mingled with his captors like a friend, recited his own poetry to them (complaining when they did not applaud), and told them, cheerfully, that once he was free he would come back and have them all crucified. The pirates thought he was joking. He was not. He returned with a fleet, captured them, and had them crucified -- though, as Plutarch notes, he did first cut their throats, "out of consideration for them," since he had grown rather fond of them. This story is not allegory. This is just what happened.

**The Nicomedes problem:** In his youth Caesar served in Asia and spent considerable time at the court of Nicomedes IV of Bithynia. Rumors circulated immediately and never entirely died: his enemies whispered for decades that there had been something between them. His soldiers, who adored him but maintained a cheerful tradition of insulting their general, sang during his Gallic triumph: *Gallias Caesar subegit, Nicomedes Caesarem* ("Caesar conquered the Gauls, Nicomedes conquered Caesar"). Caesar apparently took this with good humor, as a man who had successfully conquered Gaul can afford to do.

**Baldness:** Caesar was deeply self-conscious about his receding hairline, which is extremely relatable. He reportedly combed his thin hair forward to cover it and was so grateful for the privilege of wearing the laurel wreath at all times -- granted as an honor -- that he wore it everywhere, on every possible occasion. Ancient sources are united on one point: he would have preferred the wreath.

**Climbing the ladder:** Caesar's political career followed the standard Roman *cursus honorum*, though with more borrowing than usual. He was consistently in debt -- his campaigns to win popular support were expensive -- but his ability to attract financial backers, notably Crassus, compensated for personal finances that were always spectacular in their negative numbers. As aedile he staged games of such extravagance that Plutarch says the Roman people considered giving him the consulship on the spot, which would have saved everyone a great deal of time.

**"Caesar's wife must be above suspicion":** In 62 BC, during the rites of the Bona Dea festival -- a ceremony exclusively for women, from which all men were excluded -- a young nobleman named Clodius Pulcher was discovered inside the house where the ceremony was being held, reportedly disguised as a woman. The house belonged to Caesar. Clodius was allegedly there to see Caesar's wife Pompeia. Caesar himself was not present. Nevertheless, he divorced Pompeia immediately. When asked why, since there was no proof of wrongdoing, he gave the answer that has survived ever since: "Caesar's wife must be above suspicion." Whether this was genuine high-mindedness or convenient political cover has been debated ever since. Cicero, who testified against Clodius at his trial, sided firmly with the second interpretation.

**The First Triumvirate:** Around 60 BC, Caesar formed the political alliance known today as the First Triumvirate with Pompey and Crassus. It was not a formal institution -- Rome had no such thing -- but a private arrangement of mutual support among the three most powerful men in Rome. It got Caesar his consulship in 59 BC and, from there, the proconsular command in Gaul that would make him the most famous man in Western history.

**Nine years in Gaul:** Caesar's Gallic campaigns (58-50 BC) are the subject of the *Bellum Gallicum*. In brief: he conquered most of what is now France, Belgium, and parts of Switzerland and Germany, made two expeditions to Britain, crossed the Rhine twice to intimidate the Germans, and suppressed a massive Gallic revolt led by Vercingetorix in 52 BC. The campaigns resulted in roughly one million dead and one million enslaved Gauls, by ancient estimates. They also produced an incomparable military reputation, enormous wealth, and the undying loyalty of a professional army that had spent nine years under his command. Pompey, watching from Rome, grew increasingly nervous.

**The Rubicon:** In 49 BC, the Senate ordered Caesar to disband his army before returning to Rome. The tribunes who tried to veto the motion were physically expelled from the Senate. Caesar's response was not a written complaint. On the night of January 10-11, 49 BC, he led the XIII Legion across the Rubicon, the small river that marked the boundary between Roman Italy and his province. To cross armed was to commit an act of war against the state. The moment was legally simple and politically incandescent. What Caesar actually said at the crossing is disputed; the most famous version -- *iacta alea esto*, "let the die be cast," a line from the Greek comedian Menander -- comes from Suetonius. What all sources agree on is that he crossed.

**Clemency and "Veni, vidi, vici":** The civil war against Pompey (49-48 BC) saw Caesar consistently pursue a policy of *clementia* -- mercy -- toward defeated enemies, releasing Pompeian officers rather than executing them. It was good propaganda and also, apparently, sincere. Pompey fled to Greece, then to Egypt, where he was assassinated by agents of Ptolemy XIII before Caesar arrived. Caesar, by all accounts, was genuinely distressed. In 47 BC, after defeating Pharnaces II of Pontus at the battle of Zela -- a campaign so quick it barely qualified as a campaign -- Caesar sent Rome the three-word dispatch that ranks among history's most economical communiques: *veni, vidi, vici*. I came, I saw, I conquered. Each word is a perfect first-person singular past tense verb. The war had lasted five days. The dispatch required three words.

**Not an emperor -- this needs to be said clearly:** Caesar was not an emperor. The Roman Empire did not yet exist; it was created by Augustus after Caesar's death. Caesar's constitutional position was that of *dictator* -- an extraordinary magistracy with sweeping powers, legally recognized but always understood to be temporary. In February 44 BC, just weeks before his assassination, Caesar was appointed *dictator perpetuus*: dictator for life. This was the crossing of another line. The dictatorship had always been a magistracy with a time limit; a permanent dictatorship was kingship by another name. It was this appointment, more than anything else, that hardened the conspiracy against him.

The distinction matters. "Emperor" is the title of the *princeps*, a position created by Augustus after the civil wars, carefully designed to look like a restored republic. Caesar gave his name to the whole enterprise -- *Kaiser*, *Czar*, and "Caesar" in a dozen other languages all descend from him -- but he died before the thing he was building had a name.

**The Ides of March and his will:** On March 15, 44 BC, Caesar was stabbed twenty-three times in the Theatre of Pompey by senators calling themselves *Liberatores*. Medical examination established that only one wound was fatal. Whether he said anything as he fell is uncertain; "Et tu, Brute?" is Shakespeare's, not history's.

Three days later, his will was read publicly by Mark Antony. Two clauses shook Rome. First: Caesar left his private gardens on the Tiber (*Horti Caesaris*) to the Roman people as a public park, and bequeathed 75 denarii to every Roman citizen. Second: his great-nephew Gaius Octavius was posthumously adopted as his son and primary heir. Octavius would become Gaius Julius Caesar Octavianus and eventually, as Augustus, the first Roman emperor. Caesar's last political act was to designate his successor in a world that did not yet know it was going to need one.

## Most Important Works

**The Corpus Caesarianum**

Caesar's surviving works all fall under the umbrella of the *Corpus Caesarianum*, a collection of military memoirs covering his campaigns in Gaul and his civil war against Pompey, along with subsequent campaigns in Alexandria, Africa, and Spain written by his officers. Not all of these are by Caesar himself.

***De Bello Gallico*** ("On the Gallic War") is Caesar's account of his campaigns in Gaul from 58 to 52 BC. It is the primary Latin text encountered by first- and second-year students almost everywhere Latin is taught. The work consists of **eight books**, but Caesar wrote only **the first seven**, covering the campaigns year by year. **Book VIII was written by Aulus Hirtius**, one of Caesar's officers, who completed the account after Caesar's death up to 50 BC. Hirtius says so openly in his preface. His style -- somewhat more ornate and slower -- is noticeably different from Caesar's; the seam is visible to a trained reader.

One thing textbooks often leave implicit must be said explicitly: the *Bellum Gallicum* is propaganda. Extremely well-crafted, strategically shaped propaganda. Caesar wrote it while the campaigns were still in progress, releasing books roughly year by year, and its purpose was political: to justify his actions in Gaul to a Roman audience, to maintain his name and military reputation before the public, and to counter his enemies' accusations that he was exceeding his mandate. Every campaign is presented as a response to a provocation or a defensive necessity. The Gauls are always the aggressors, or the troublemakers, or the treacherous. Caesar is always the reasonable party who offers fair terms before resorting to force. The third-person narration -- "Caesar did this," "Caesar ordered that" -- is not humility. It is the prose equivalent of wearing a toga to your own trial: it signals objectivity, distance, the voice of a man calmly reporting what happened rather than an interested party justifying himself. Do not be fooled.

***De Bello Civili*** ("On the Civil War") is Caesar's account of the civil war against Pompey, covering 49-48 BC in three books. It is almost certainly **unfinished** -- it breaks off in the middle of the Alexandrian campaign -- and was completed by others. The *Bellum Alexandrinum* is generally attributed to Hirtius; the *Bellum Africum* and *Bellum Hispaniense* are by unknown hands.

The BC is a different experience from the BG. Where the BG takes time to explain who the Gallic tribes are, what their social structure looks like, and what the geography of the Rhine means for military operations, the BC assumes you already know who Pompey is, what the tribunes of the plebs are, what the *Senatus Consultum Ultimum* means, and when it has been invoked before. Caesar is writing for Romans who lived through these events, not for posterity.

The propaganda is, if anything, more visible in the BC than in the BG, because the stakes are higher. In the BG Caesar justifies conquest. In the BC he justifies civil war. His line of defense is threefold: he tried every diplomatic channel and Pompey refused each time; the constitutional order was violated not by him but by the Pompeians, who illegally silenced the tribunes; and he is fighting not for personal power but for his *dignitas* and for the freedom of the Roman people from "the faction of the few." Meanwhile, in Caesar's telling, Pompey consistently refuses to negotiate, consistently avoids pitched battle, and consistently runs away when pressed. Caesar presents himself as the responsible adult in a room full of panicking optimates. The verdict of history, such as it is, may vary.

**Lost Works**

Caesar wrote far more than survives. Among the attested losses:

*Anticato*: a prose response to Cicero's laudation of Cato the Younger after his suicide in 46 BC. Caesar clearly felt that Cato -- who died refusing to accept his clemency -- deserved less of the last word than he was being given. Lost.

*De Analogia*: a grammatical treatise in two books, dedicated to Cicero, arguing for principled and regular Latin usage. Caesar wrote it during a crossing of the Alps in 54 BC. Writing grammar textbooks while crossing the Alps is, again, very Caesar.

*Iter*: a poem about his journey from Rome to Spain in 46 BC. Lost. Augustus apparently suppressed the *Oedipus* tragedy Caesar had written, reportedly saying that Caesar's genius was better contemplated in the twelve months of his dictatorship than in his poetry. Whether this was diplomacy or aesthetic judgment is unclear.

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Caesar's style is, at its core, a weapon. He developed the most radically transparent Latin prose in the entire tradition -- not because he could not do more, but because clarity was exactly what the political situation required.

The dominant technical feature of Caesarian prose is *brevitas*: compression, the refusal of ornament. Where Cicero builds a period across five subordinate clauses and lands on a resonant clausula, Caesar coordinates. *Veni, vidi, vici*: three main verbs, three completed actions, nothing between them. He uses parataxis far more than hypotaxis. The result reads like military dispatches because that is, in essence, what the *Bellum Gallicum* is. This is not accidental. Caesar wrote the lost *De Analogia* arguing for precise and regular Latin, and he practiced what he preached: *tamquam scopulum sic fugias inauditum atque insolens verbum* -- "flee the unusual and foreign word as you would a reef."

The third-person narration deserves particular notice. Caesar never writes "I." He writes "Caesar." "Caesar ordered," "Caesar decided," "Caesar advanced." The *commentarius* genre permitted this convention, and Caesar adopted it so completely that it seems inseparable from his work. The effect positions Caesar as an object of history rather than a subject of advocacy. He is not telling you what he did and why; he is reporting, in measured impersonal terms, what happened. The person who happens to be called Caesar happens to be the protagonist, but that is simply how events unfolded. The technique is so effective that generations of students have read the BG as a historical document and only later been told that it is political advocacy by the advocate himself.

**De Bello Gallico** is the starting point for almost every Latin student, and the choice is well made. The Latin is principled in its simplicity. Sentences are structured logically, with clear subordination and minimal ambiguity. The vocabulary is military and administrative -- not wide, not rare. There are no lyric meters, no philosophical abstractions, no dense allusive texture. The main challenge for the beginning student is not Caesar's Latin but Latin itself: the ablative absolute, indirect statement, the gerundive, the sequence of tenses. Caesar uses all of these, but he uses them in clean, logical contexts, as if he designed the sentences to teach the constructions. He did not, of course; but the effect is the same.

**De Bello Civili** is a step up, and the step is more significant than it might appear. The language itself is not dramatically harder -- same clarity, same compression, same measured pace. The difficulty is contextual. The BC was not finished and not revised to the same degree as the BG; there are passages that feel raw and hurried, as if Caesar meant to come back and polish later. More critically, it assumes a politically literate Roman audience that needs no explanation of the SCU, the tribunes' constitutional history, or the events of the Sullan proscriptions. For a modern reader, this demands heavy annotation. You can parse every word of BC I.7 and still not understand what Caesar is arguing unless you know what the *tribunicia intercessio* was and what happened to the Gracchi. The Latin is manageable; the political subtext is dense.

There is also the propaganda dimension to hold in mind, which changes the reading once you are aware of it. Caesar's careful framing, his consistent presentation of himself as the reasonable party, his portrait of Pompey as a man who talked big and ran -- these are not neutral features of the text. Reading the BC well means reading it against itself: noticing what Caesar does not explain, what he glides over quickly, what he frames with particular care. That is a more sophisticated exercise than it looks.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: De Bello Gallico VI.13-14 -- The Druids

**Source:** Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book VI, chapters 13-14, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/caesar/gall6.shtml)

**Context:** Book VI contains one of Caesar's most famous ethnographic digressions -- an extended description of Gaulish and German society inserted between two years of military campaigning. Chapters 13-20 describe the social structure of Gaul: two privileged classes (druids and knights), their customs, their religion, their relationship to the common people. The digression is typical of the BG's strategy: by presenting Gaul as a coherent civilization with its own institutions, Caesar implicitly positions Rome as the superior civilization whose conquest of these lands is a natural and legitimate process. What appears to be anthropology is also, quietly, justification.

**Latin Text:**

[13] In omni Gallia eorum hominum, qui aliquo sunt numero atque honore, genera sunt duo. Nam plebes paene servorum habetur loco, quae nihil audet per se, nullo adhibetur consilio. Plerique, cum aut aere alieno aut magnitudine tributorum aut iniuria potentiorum premuntur, sese in servitutem dicant nobilibus: in hos eadem omnia sunt iura, quae dominis in servos. Sed de his duobus generibus alterum est druidum, alterum equitum. Illi rebus divinis intersunt, sacrificia publica ac privata procurant, religiones interpretantur: ad hos magnus adulescentium numerus disciplinae causa concurrit, magnoque hi sunt apud eos honore. Nam fere de omnibus controversiis publicis privatisque constituunt, et, si quod est admissum facinus, si caedes facta, si de hereditate, de finibus controversia est, idem decernunt, praemia poenasque constituunt; si qui aut privatus aut populus eorum decreto non stetit, sacrificiis interdicunt. Haec poena apud eos est gravissima. Quibus ita est interdictum, hi numero impiorum ac sceleratorum habentur, his omnes decedunt, aditum sermonemque defugiunt, ne quid ex contagione incommodi accipiant, neque his petentibus ius redditur neque honos ullus communicatur. His autem omnibus druidibus praeest unus, qui summam inter eos habet auctoritatem. Hoc mortuo aut si qui ex reliquis excellit dignitate succedit, aut, si sunt plures pares, suffragio druidum, nonnumquam etiam armis de principatu contendunt. Hi certo anni tempore in finibus Carnutum, quae regio totius Galliae media habetur, considunt in loco consecrato. Huc omnes undique, qui controversias habent, conveniunt eorumque decretis iudiciisque parent. Disciplina in Britannia reperta atque inde in Galliam translata esse existimatur, et nunc, qui diligentius eam rem cognoscere volunt, plerumque illo discendi causa proficiscuntur.

[14] Druides a bello abesse consuerunt neque tributa una cum reliquis pendunt; militiae vacationem omniumque rerum habent immunitatem. Tantis excitati praemiis et sua sponte multi in disciplinam conveniunt et a parentibus propinquisque mittuntur. Magnum ibi numerum versuum ediscere dicuntur. Itaque annos nonnulli vicenos in disciplina permanent. Neque fas esse existimant ea litteris mandare, cum in reliquis fere rebus, publicis privatisque rationibus Graecis litteris utantur. Id mihi duabus de causis instituisse videntur, quod neque in vulgum disciplinam efferri velint neque eos, qui discunt, litteris confisos minus memoriae studere: quod fere plerisque accidit, ut praesidio litterarum diligentiam in perdiscendo ac memoriam remittant. In primis hoc volunt persuadere, non interire animas, sed ab aliis post mortem transire ad alios, atque hoc maxime ad virtutem excitari putant metu mortis neglecto. Multa praeterea de sideribus atque eorum motu, de mundi ac terrarum magnitudine, de rerum natura, de deorum immortalium vi ac potestate disputant et iuventuti tradunt.

**English Translation:**

In all of Gaul, among those men who are of some rank and prestige, there are two classes. For the common people are regarded as almost the equivalent of slaves: they dare nothing on their own authority and are called into no council. Most of them, pressed down by debt or the weight of taxes or the injustice of the more powerful, commit themselves into the service of the nobility; over these men, the nobles hold the same rights as masters over slaves. Of these two classes, one is that of the druids, the other that of the knights. The druids take part in divine matters, perform public and private sacrifices, and interpret religious observances; a great number of young men come to them for instruction, and they are held in great honor. For they decide on almost all public and private disputes: if any crime has been committed, if a murder has been done, if there is a dispute over an inheritance or a boundary, it is these same men who decide and set out rewards and punishments; if any private person or community does not abide by their decree, they forbid them from sacrifice. This is the heaviest punishment among them. Those on whom this has been imposed are counted among the number of the impious and the criminal; all men give way before them and shun their approach and conversation, lest they incur some harm from the contact; neither is justice granted to them when they seek it, nor is any honor shared with them. Over all these druids there presides a single man, who holds the highest authority among them. When he dies, whoever stands out above the rest in dignity succeeds him; if several are equal, the succession is settled by vote of the druids, though sometimes they contend for the leadership with arms. At a fixed time of year they assemble in a consecrated place in the territory of the Carnutes, which is held to be the center of all Gaul. From every direction all who have disputes come together and submit to their decrees and judgments. The system is believed to have been discovered in Britain and imported from there into Gaul; and today, those who wish to study it more carefully generally travel there for the purpose of learning.

The druids are accustomed to abstain from war and do not pay taxes alongside the rest; they enjoy exemption from military service and immunity from all obligations. Attracted by such great rewards, many come of their own accord to study under them, and others are sent by parents and kinsmen. They are said to learn a great number of verses there; accordingly some remain in their course of study for twenty years. They do not think it right to commit these things to writing, even though for virtually everything else -- for public and private business -- they use the Greek alphabet. They seem to have adopted this practice for two reasons: they do not wish the system to spread among the common people, and they do not want those who study to rely on writing and give less attention to memory, which is generally what happens when people have the resource of writing: they relax their effort and application in learning by heart. First and foremost, they wish to establish the conviction that souls do not perish but after death pass from one person to another, and they believe that by this teaching, with the fear of death set aside, valor is most powerfully encouraged. In addition, they hold many debates among their students about the stars and their motion, the size of the world and of our lands, the nature of things, and the power and might of the immortal gods.

**Analysis:**

This passage is a showcase of Caesar's ethnographic method at its most characteristic. The prose is measured and confident, the sentences mostly short to moderate in length, the vocabulary precise and administrative. Notice how chapter 13 opens with a near-definition: *genera sunt duo* -- there are two classes. Caesar the taxonomist, organizing Gaulish society the way a Roman magistrate would organize a provincial census.

The social picture Caesar paints is notable for its Roman frame. He maps Gaulish society onto Roman categories: the *plebes* who are *paene servorum loco* (almost like slaves), the *nobiles* who hold *iura* over them analogous to *dominus* over *servus*, the druids who handle *res divinae* and serve functions equivalent to Roman pontiffs and judges combined. This is not pure anthropology: it is translation, which always involves interpretation. Understanding Gaul in Roman terms makes it legible to Caesar's Roman audience, and makes it comprehensible as something Rome can and should administer.

The druids passage is substantively fascinating. Caesar notes that they function as judges and arbiters, that their highest penalty is excommunication (being forbidden from sacrifice, cut off from all civic interaction), and that their leader is elected -- or in disputed cases, the leadership is settled by armed contest. The detail about the druidic school in Britain is one of the earliest attestations of any kind of British-Continental cultural exchange, treated here as a given.

The chapter 14 passage on the oral tradition is particularly rich. Caesar observes -- as a trained Roman rhetorician himself, with apparent admiration -- that the druids memorize an enormous number of verses and deliberately refuse to write any of them down. His explanation of the two reasons for this is analytical and sympathetic. He reports their doctrine of the transmigration of souls without comment. He lists their curriculum -- astronomy, cosmology, natural philosophy, theology -- topics that would not have surprised a Roman Stoic. The paragraph functions, quietly, as a reminder that the people Caesar is conquering are not simple barbarians: they have an intellectual class with a sophisticated educational program. His enemies must be worthy of conquest, not trivial.

Syntactically, the passage is clean and instructional. The dominant constructions are the ablative absolute (*metu mortis neglecto*, "with the fear of death set aside") and the accusative and infinitive in indirect statement (*non interire animas, sed ab aliis post mortem transire ad alios*). These are exactly the constructions that appear in every first-year Latin grammar in the clearest possible contexts. Caesar did not design them as practice exercises; they are just how the constructions appear when someone writes correct, measured Latin prose.

### Excerpt 2: De Bello Civili I.7 -- Caesar Addresses the Thirteenth Legion

**Source:** Caesar, *Commentarii de Bello Civili*, Book I, chapter 7, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/caesar/bc1.shtml)

**Context:** January 49 BC. Caesar is at Ravenna, having just received word that his final diplomatic offers have been rejected, the tribunes who tried to veto the Senate's ultimatum have been physically expelled from Rome, and the *Senatus Consultum Ultimum* has been passed. He has the XIII Legion with him. Before crossing the Rubicon, Caesar addresses the troops. What follows is Caesar's account of Caesar's speech. Read that sentence again.

**Latin Text:**

[7] Quibus rebus cognitis Caesar apud milites contionatur. Omnium temporum iniurias inimicorum in se commemorat; a quibus deductum ac depravatum Pompeium queritur invidia atque obtrectatione laudis suae, cuius ipse honori et dignitati semper faverit adiutorque fuerit. Novum in re publica introductum exemplum queritur, ut tribunicia intercessio armis notaretur atque opprimeretur, quae superioribus annis armis esset restituta. Sullam nudata omnibus rebus tribunicia potestate tamen intercessionem liberam reliquisse. Pompeium, qui amissa restituisse videatur, dona etiam, quae ante habuerint, ademisse. Quotienscumque sit decretum, darent operam magistratus, ne quid res publica detrimenti caperet (qua voce et quo senatus consulto populus Romanus ad arma sit vocatus), factum in perniciosis legibus, in vi tribunicia, in secessione populi templis locisque editioribus occupatis: atque haec superioris aetatis exempla expiata Saturnini atque Gracchorum casibus docet; quarum rerum illo tempore nihil factum, ne cogitatum quidem. Nulla lex promulgata, non cum populo agi coeptum, nulla secessio facta. Hortatur, cuius imperatoris ductu VIIII annis rem publicam felicissime gesserint plurimaque proelia secunda fecerint, omnem Galliam Germaniamque pacaverint, ut eius existimationem dignitatemque ab inimicis defendant. Conclamant legionis XIII, quae aderat, milites -- hanc enim initio tumultus evocaverat, reliquae nondum convenerant -- sese paratos esse imperatoris sui tribunorumque plebis iniurias defendere.

**English Translation:**

Having learned of these developments, Caesar addressed his soldiers. He recalled the wrongs done to him by his enemies over all the years; he complained that Pompey had been led astray and corrupted by their envy and their constant efforts to undermine his reputation -- Pompey, whose own honor and position Caesar had always supported and promoted. He complained that a new precedent had been introduced in the state: that the tribunician veto should be silenced and crushed by armed force, the very right that had been restored by armed force in earlier years. He pointed out that Sulla, though he had stripped the tribunate of everything, had still left the right of veto free. Pompey, who had seemed to restore what was lost, had in fact taken away even the rights they previously held. Whenever the decree had been passed that magistrates should ensure the state suffer no harm -- the formula by which the Roman people had been summoned to arms by that voice and that senatorial resolution -- it had been used against dangerous legislation, against violence by the tribunes, against secessions of the people who had occupied the temples and the high ground; and he showed that examples of this kind from earlier generations had been atoned for by the fate of Saturninus and the Gracchi. Yet none of these things had happened in the present case -- not even been thought of. No law had been proposed, no action had been initiated with the people, no secession had occurred. He urged them -- in whose service, under his generalship over nine years, they had managed the affairs of the state with the greatest success, won very many battles, and brought all of Gaul and Germany to peace -- to defend his reputation and dignity from his enemies. The soldiers of the Thirteenth Legion, which was present -- for he had summoned it at the beginning of the crisis; the remaining legions had not yet assembled -- shouted out that they were ready to defend their general and the tribunes of the people from injustice.

**Analysis:**

Put this passage next to BG VI.13 and the difference is immediately visible. The fundamental machinery is the same -- short to medium sentences, coordinated or lightly subordinated, third-person narration -- but the texture is denser, the political assumptions thicker on the ground, the compression more aggressive.

Begin with the grammar. Most of the passage is in indirect discourse: not "he said that Pompey..." but the accusative and infinitive construction embedded throughout. *Deductum ac depravatum Pompeium* -- Pompey to have been led astray and corrupted. *Sullam intercessionem liberam reliquisse* -- Sulla to have left the veto free. *Pompeium dona ademisse* -- Pompey to have taken away the rights. Each is a compressed indirect statement, accusative subject plus perfect infinitive, running line after line. The reader must hold the reporting verb (*queritur*, *docet*, *hortatur*) in mind while parsing five or six consecutive accusations in the same construction. In the BG this structure appears briefly and in clear contexts. In the BC it is the dominant mode of much of the text.

Now consider what Caesar does not explain. He refers to the *tribunicia intercessio* -- the tribunician veto -- as a constitutional right that was suppressed by Sulla and later restored. For this to make sense, a reader must know: who the tribunes of the plebs were and what their powers included; what Sulla's dictatorship did to the tribunate in the 80s BC; what later reforms restored; and that in January 49 BC, two tribunes -- Mark Antony and Quintus Cassius -- attempted to veto the SCU and were expelled from the Senate. Caesar assumes all of this without explanation. Similarly, the references to Saturninus and the Gracchi assume the reader knows these were populist politicians killed by senatorial violence, and that in each case the SCU was invoked to justify the killing. The argument Caesar is making -- that the SCU was designed for genuine emergencies, not for use against a general who had done nothing illegal -- is sophisticated and potentially persuasive, but you have to know the history to follow it.

Finally, look at the propaganda structure. Caesar positions himself as (1) a victim of years of orchestrated enmity, (2) a defender of constitutional norms that his enemies have violated, and (3) a servant of his soldiers and the Roman people. Pompey is the villain -- led astray by envy, the man who took away rights he seemed to restore. The speech ends with the soldiers' response: they are ready to defend not just Caesar but "the tribunes of the people" -- meaning they are defending the Roman constitution, not just their general. The framing transforms a military revolt into an act of civic duty. Caesar wrote this. Keep that in mind every time you translate it.

## Legacy and Impact

Caesar's legacy is so pervasive that it has become almost invisible: it is the furniture of Western civilization. His name became a title. *Caesar* gave us *Kaiser* in German, *Czar* in Russian, and equivalents in a dozen other languages, used for centuries to designate supreme rulers who had nothing to do with Rome. The month of July is named after him. The Cesarean section probably is not, despite the popular belief, but the name stuck anyway.

In the history of Latin prose, the *Bellum Gallicum* occupies an almost paradoxical position: it is simultaneously the most widely read Latin text in the world and among the most underread, in the sense of being most frequently read without being seriously analyzed. It is the training text, the warm-up before the real authors -- and yet it is a masterwork of deliberate literary craft, every apparent simplicity a calculated effect. The military historian Adrian Goldsworthy called it one of the most brilliantly sustained pieces of political advocacy ever written in any language. He was right. Most first-year students have no idea.

The BC had less immediate literary influence -- it was incomplete, unrevised, and Pompey's faction lost, which reduced the readership of texts that made Pompey look bad -- but it established a template for politically engaged historical writing that Sallust, Tacitus, and many others would develop. Caesar's self-exculpatory narrative structure, the way he mobilizes constitutional argument in service of a predetermined conclusion, became a model for how the winning side writes civil war history. This tradition runs from the BC through Sallust's *Bellum Catilinae*, through Tacitus's careful management of the Tiberian record, and arguably all the way to political memoirs in the present day.

Caesar also mattered to the rhetorical tradition as an exemplar of the Atticist style -- spare, precise, unornamented. Cicero praised his Latin as pure to the point of elegance: *genus sermonis purus et inlustris*, a pure and brilliant manner of speaking (*Brutus* 261-262). Quintilian, writing a century later, said that if Caesar had devoted himself to oratory instead of military and political life, he would have been Cicero's only real rival.

Perhaps the most interesting aspect of Caesar's afterlife is the question of what he was trying to build. He did not call himself king. He did not abolish the Senate. He moved toward something -- a permanent personal supremacy, an end to the cycle of aristocratic competition that had been tearing Rome apart -- but the Ides of March came before he could define what it was. Augustus, in the end, did it for him: created the Principate, one-man rule disguised as a restored republic, and gave it Caesar's name.

One final detail, recorded by Suetonius: on a visit to a temple in Spain in his early thirties, Caesar wept at a statue of Alexander the Great. He said that Alexander at his age had already conquered the world, while he himself had done nothing of note. He was already praetor at the time. He seems to have recovered.

## Final Rating

**Tier: D (Good Exercise)**

The D rating reflects primarily the *Bellum Gallicum*, which is and remains the most pedagogically accessible extended prose text in classical Latin. Its vocabulary is controlled, its syntax clean, its sentences logically built. A student with two solid semesters of Latin can open BG Book I and move through it with reasonable confidence, dictionary in hand. This is not an accident; it is Caesar's Latin doing exactly what Caesar's Latin was designed to do.

The *Bellum Civili* is harder. The political background demands annotation, the indirect discourse is denser and more sustained, and the unfinished state of the text means there are passages that lack the smooth coherence of the BG. Taken alone, the BC would sit at a low C -- accessible with context, frustrating without it. But the aggregate of Caesar's output, in which the BG is both the larger and the more characteristic work, keeps the rating at D.

The caveat that always applies: reading Caesar *well* -- not just parsing him, but understanding what he is doing and why every stylistic choice is a political choice -- is harder than the tier suggests. A D for difficulty is not a D for complexity. The *Bellum Gallicum* rewards a second and third reading in a way that few "easy" texts do. The students who only read it to pass a test are missing the point, which is itself rather Caesarian.

# AULUS HIRTIUS (c. 90--43 BC)

## Biography

Aulus Hirtius is the rare figure in Roman literary history who owes his place in the canon entirely to finishing someone else's work -- and whose own life ended before he could finish his own. He is the literary world's eternal second act.

Born around 90 BC, Hirtius came from an equestrian family and attached himself early to Caesar's staff. He served as a legate in Gaul from approximately 54 BC onward and became one of Caesar's most trusted officers and diplomatic intermediaries. He handled negotiations with foreign powers, managed correspondence, and apparently spent enough time around Caesar's table to develop a reputation as a committed epicure. Cicero, who knew him well and corresponded with him regularly, mentions in his letters that they had an informal exchange arrangement: Cicero would give Hirtius rhetoric lessons, Hirtius would teach Cicero how to eat well. The surviving evidence suggests Hirtius held up his end of the bargain considerably better.

Cicero's letters paint a vivid portrait of Hirtius in the years after Caesar's death: politically moderate, genuinely loyal to the Republican institutions, distressed by the chaos following the Ides of March. He was not an ideologue, and he was not a Caesarian hardliner -- he was an officer who had served his general with competence and was now trying to figure out, like most of Rome, what happened next.

**The boss's work:** The anecdote that defines Hirtius's literary career is not really an anecdote at all -- it is the opening paragraph of his own preface to BG Book VIII. He writes that he was pestered (*coactus assiduis tuis vocibus*) by Cornelius Balbus, one of Caesar's secretaries and political fixers, into completing Caesar's unfinished commentaries. He had been refusing. He writes, with transparent anxiety, that he wishes readers could know how *unwillingly* he took on the task -- because that would make it easier for him to avoid the accusation of stupidity and arrogance for inserting himself between Caesar's texts. He admits that nothing written by anyone else can match the elegance of Caesar's commentaries. He notes that while others can judge how well and correctly Caesar wrote, he himself additionally knows how *fast* Caesar wrote.

This is a man who understood exactly what he was getting himself into and did it anyway because a powerful friend would not leave him alone. The result is Book VIII of the *Bellum Gallicum* and, almost certainly, the *Bellum Alexandrinum* -- a sequel covering the Alexandrian War of 47 BC that picks up exactly where Caesar's *Bellum Civili* breaks off. Whether Hirtius would have gone further, completing the historical record of Caesar's entire career, is a question that was never answered.

**Consul and soldier:** In 43 BC, Hirtius was elected consul alongside Gaius Vibius Pansa -- a remarkable piece of Cicero-backed positioning designed to create two reliably anti-Antonian consuls who would defend the Republic against Mark Antony's encroachments. They succeeded militarily and failed biologically. Both consuls raised armies, marched north against Antony, and defeated him at the battles of Forum Gallorum and Mutina in April 43 BC. Then both consuls died -- Hirtius killed in the fighting at Mutina, Pansa dying of his wounds a few days later. Mark Antony escaped with his army. The young Octavian, technically a subordinate in these campaigns, emerged as the only surviving Roman commander with troops. Ancient gossip -- unfounded, probably -- suggested Octavian had engineered the outcome. What is not in doubt is that within the year Octavian had allied with Antony, and Cicero (who had been the intellectual architect of the anti-Antonian strategy) was dead.

Hirtius was fifty-something, a competent general, a reliable ally, a decent writer, and someone who liked good food. He deserved a more dignified exit than dying at Mutina to set up the Second Triumvirate.

**A footnote on food:** Some later sources credit Hirtius with writing a cookbook. This is almost certainly apocryphal, but the image of Caesar's legate composing recipes between battles has a certain appeal. It would at least explain why the *Bellum Alexandrinum*'s prose, while military in content, occasionally pauses to describe things with rather more sensory precision than strictly necessary.

---

## Most Important Works

Hirtius's place in the *Corpus Caesarianum* rests on two attributions, one certain and one probable.

***De Bello Gallico, Book VIII*** is Hirtius's definite contribution. He says so himself in the preface, naming himself, his correspondent Balbus, and his sources (including Caesar's unfinished notes). The book covers the campaigns of 51-50 BC -- the mopping-up operations after the great Gallic revolt of Vercingetorix had been crushed. It is a competent continuation, sometimes quite good, but it reads differently from Books I-VII, as even a moderately experienced reader will notice within a few pages.

***De Bello Alexandrino*** ("On the Alexandrian War") picks up precisely where Caesar's *Bellum Civili* stops -- mid-sentence, essentially -- and covers the war Caesar fought in Alexandria (48-47 BC) after pursuing Pompey to Egypt, the subsequent campaign against Pharnaces II of Pontus (*veni vidi vici* territory), and operations in Illyricum and Spain. The attribution to Hirtius is widely accepted but not entirely settled. The Latin is more polished than the *Bellum Africum* and *Bellum Hispaniense*, which are generally thought to be by other, less skilled hands; it has thematic and stylistic continuities with BG VIII; and the only ancient reference that comes close to identifying an author points toward someone in Caesar's inner circle, which Hirtius certainly was. The majority scholarly opinion is that Hirtius wrote it, or at minimum supervised its compilation. For practical purposes, it is his.

The *Bellum Africum* and *Bellum Hispaniense* -- covering campaigns in Africa (47-46 BC) and Spain (45 BC) -- are NOT attributed to Hirtius by modern scholarship. They are rougher, more grammatically irregular texts, probably written by lower-ranking officers who were present for those campaigns. They are fascinating documents for exactly that reason, but they belong elsewhere in this project.

---

## Writing Style and Difficulty

The most instructive way to read Hirtius is directly against Caesar -- which is precisely what the *Bellum Gallicum*'s editorial structure forces you to do. Book VII ends with Caesar's crisp, measured account of Vercingetorix's surrender at Alesia. Book VIII opens with Hirtius talking about himself. The contrast is immediate.

**The first-person problem:** Caesar never writes "I." As established in the Caesar entry, the third-person narration is a deliberate political and rhetorical choice: Caesar = object of history, not subject of advocacy. Hirtius, from the very first word of his preface (*coactus* -- "having been compelled"), puts himself in the text. He addresses Balbus by name. He writes *me interposuerim* -- "I have inserted myself." He worries about his reputation. He admires his general. He is, in short, a person, not a camera. This is not worse than Caesar; it is simply a different register -- the register of a man writing literary Latin rather than political weaponry.

**Sentence structure:** Hirtius's sentences are longer and more hypotactically complex than Caesar's. Where Caesar packs information into compact coordinated clauses, Hirtius tends to subordinate more heavily, embedding relative clauses, participial phrases, and purpose constructions within each other. The praefatio is the most visible example, but the same pattern holds in the military narrative sections. This is not a radical difference -- Hirtius is still writing what is recognizably Caesarian Latin -- but the register is higher, the architecture denser.

**Vocabulary:** Hirtius allows himself words Caesar would not use. His descriptive passages in the *Bellum Alexandrinum* use technical military engineering vocabulary (*testudinines*, *musculi*, *arietes*) in greater density and with more explanatory detail than is typical of Caesar. He occasionally pauses to explain things -- the geography of Alexandria, the properties of the Nile flood -- in ways that recall Caesar's ethnographic digressions but are rather less efficiently executed.

**Psychological observation:** One of the more interesting features of the *Bellum Alexandrinum* is its occasional forays into the mentality of Caesar's opponents. The Alexandrians are portrayed with a complexity Caesar rarely extends to his enemies: they are clever, adaptable, aware of Roman intentions, and capable of rational political analysis. Chapter 3 gives the Alexandrians' own interpretation of the situation, and it is remarkably lucid as an account of imperial politics. Whether this reflects Hirtius's broader sensibility or simply the demands of the subject matter is difficult to say.

**Narrative distance:** Caesar's narration is impersonal even when describing events at which he was present. Hirtius, especially in the *Bellum Alexandrinum*, occasionally hedges -- *ut coniectura consequi possumus* ("as we can infer from conjecture," BA 23), a phrase that would be unthinkable in Caesar's text. Caesar knows. Hirtius sometimes only thinks he knows, and says so. This intellectual modesty is, in its way, more honest than Caesar's omniscient narration; it is also a measure of how differently the two men understood the genre.

**The bottom line:** BG VIII is close to Caesar in style and is approachable at roughly the same level, though students who move from BG I-VII to BG VIII will notice the gear-change. The *Bellum Alexandrinum* asks more: its subject matter is geographically and politically complex (Egyptian succession crises, Alexandrian urban warfare, naval operations in a city they have never seen), its sentence structure is more demanding, and its vocabulary includes technical and geographical terms not found in the earlier works. The BA rewards the reader who brings context; it frustrates the reader who does not.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: De Bello Gallico VIII, Praefatio 1-7 -- The General Finishes the Book

**Source:** Hirtius, *Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book VIII, Praefatio, sections 1-7, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/caesar/gall8.shtml)

**Context:** This is the preface Hirtius wrote for Book VIII, addressed to Lucius Cornelius Balbus, one of Caesar's most influential political secretaries. It is the only moment in the entire Corpus Caesarianum where an author writes in the first person, names himself, and discusses the project as a literary undertaking. Reading it after seven books of Caesar's impersonal third-person narration produces a mild vertigo: suddenly there is a *person* here, with anxieties and opinions and a named friend he is writing to.

**Latin Text:**

[Praefatio] 1 Coactus assiduis tuis vocibus, Balbe, cum cotidiana mea recusatio non difficultatis excusationem, sed inertiae videretur deprecationem habere, rem difficillimam suscepi. 2 Caesaris nostri commentarios rerum gestarum Galliae, non comparantibus superioribus atque insequentibus eius scriptis, contexui novissimumque imperfectum ab rebus gestis Alexandriae confeci usque ad exitum non quidem civilis dissensionis, cuius finem nullum videmus, sed vitae Caesaris. 3 Quos utinam qui legent scire possint quam invitus susceperim scribendos, qua facilius caream stultitiae atque arrogantiae crimine, qui me mediis interposuerim Caesaris scriptis. 4 Constat enim inter omnes nihil tam operose ab aliis esse perfectum, quod non horum elegantia commentariorum superetur: 5 qui sunt editi, ne scientia tantarum rerum scriptoribus deesset, adeoque probantur omnium iudicio ut praerepta, non praebita, facultas scriptoribus videatur. 6 Cuius tamen rei maior nostra quam reliquorum est admiratio: ceteri enim, quam bene atque emendate, nos etiam, quam facile atque celeriter eos perfecerit scimus. 7 Erat autem in Caesare cum facultas atque elegantia summa scribendi, tum verissima scientia suorum consiliorum explicandorum.

**English Translation:**

Compelled by your relentless insistence, Balbus -- since my daily refusals were beginning to look less like an excuse of difficulty and more like an apology for laziness -- I have undertaken a most difficult task. I have connected Caesar's commentaries on the campaigns in Gaul (since the books that precede and follow his account do not provide comparison), and I have completed the last unfinished one from the Alexandrian campaign up to the end -- not of the civil war, whose end we cannot yet see, but of Caesar's life. I only wish that those who read them could know how unwillingly I took on the writing of these books, so that I might more easily escape the charge of stupidity and arrogance for having inserted myself into the middle of Caesar's texts. For it is universally agreed that nothing worked over so carefully by others fails to be surpassed by the elegance of these commentaries, which were published so that writers should not lack knowledge of events so important -- and they meet with such universal approval that they seem to have robbed writers of an opportunity rather than provided one. My own admiration for this is, however, greater than that of others: while the rest can judge how well and correctly he wrote them, I also know how quickly and easily he finished them. Caesar possessed not only the highest ability and elegance in writing, but also the most precise understanding of setting out his own plans.

**Analysis:**

Open Caesar's BG Book I: *Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres*. Three words in, you know where you stand. It is an utterance, not a sentence; a fact, not a framing. Now open Book VIII: *Coactus assiduis tuis vocibus, Balbe*. The first word is a perfect passive participle modifying a subject still to be identified; the second is an adverb modifying an adjective modifying a noun (*assiduis vocibus*) in the ablative; and the whole thing is addressed to a named person. Seven words in, Hirtius has done more grammatical work than Caesar would do in a paragraph.

The stylistic differences are immediately apparent. Hirtius uses subordination where Caesar would coordinate. His sentence rhythms are longer and more periodic. He deploys the type of rhetorical hedging -- *utinam qui legent scire possint* ("I only wish those who read could know") -- that is entirely absent from Caesar's impersonal narration. He worries about how he will be perceived. Caesar never worried about how he would be perceived, at least not in the text; he simply arranged events so that the narration was self-evidently favorable.

The passage is also a remarkable piece of literary self-consciousness. Hirtius says outright that Caesar's commentaries have *robbed* other writers of the ability to write about these events -- not just surpassed them, but preempted them (*praerepta, non praebita, facultas scriptoribus videatur*: "the opportunity seems to have been taken from writers, not given to them"). This is Hirtius's elegant way of explaining why no other historian immediately tackled Caesar's Gallic campaigns: the commentaries were so comprehensive and so authoritative that there was nothing left to do. The observation is correct, and it has remained correct for two thousand years. Historians writing about the Gallic Wars still work around Caesar rather than independently of him.

The detail in paragraph 6 -- that Hirtius, unlike other readers, knows not only how *well* Caesar wrote but how *fast* -- is the intimate touch of someone who actually watched Caesar compose. Caesar, who wrote the *De Analogia* while crossing the Alps, was apparently as swift at the desk as he was on the march. Hirtius's admiration is not performed modesty; it is the testimony of a witness.

Syntactically, the passage is denser than Caesar but not forbidding. Students who have read BG I-VII will find themselves parsing slightly more carefully, holding subordinate clauses open slightly longer before the main verb resolves them. The vocabulary is standard; the difficulty is architectural.

### Excerpt 2: De Bello Alexandrino 1-2 -- Alexandria at War

**Source:** [Hirtius?], *De Bello Alexandrino*, chapters 1-2, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/caesar/alex.shtml)

**Context:** This is the opening of the *Bellum Alexandrinum*, which picks up exactly where Caesar's *Bellum Civili* breaks off. Caesar has pursued Pompey to Egypt, arrived to find him already dead, and become embroiled in the succession crisis between Ptolemy XIII and his sister Cleopatra. The work opens *in medias res* -- "with the Alexandrian War having broken out" (*bello Alexandrino conflato*) -- assuming the reader knows everything that happened in the BC and has followed along. There is no explanation, no orientation. You are already in Alexandria. Keep up.

**Latin Text:**

[1] Bello Alexandrino conflato Caesar Rhodo atque ex Syria Ciliciaque omnem classem arcessit; Creta sagittarios, equites ab rege Nabataeorum Malcho evocat; tormenta undique conquiri et frumentum mitti, auxilia adduci iubet. Interim munitiones cotidie operibus augentur atque omnes oppidi partes, quae minus esse firmae videntur, testudinibus ac musculis aptantur; ex aedificiis autem per foramina in proxima aedificia arietes immittuntur, quantumque aut ruinis deicitur aut per vim recipitur loci, in tantum munitiones proferuntur. Nam incendio fere tuta est Alexandrea, quod sine contignatione ac materia sunt aedificia et structuris ac fornicibus continentur tectaque sunt rudere aut pavimentis. Caesar maxime studebat ut, quam angustissimam partem oppidi palus a meridie interiecta efficiebat, hanc operibus vineisque agendis ab reliqua parte urbis excluderet, illud spectans primum ut, cum in duas partis esset urbis divisa acies, uno consilio atque imperio administraretur, deinde ut laborantibus succurri atque ex altera oppidi parte auxilium ferri posset, in primis vero ut aqua pabuloque abundaret, quarum alterius rei copiam exiguam, alterius nullam omnino facultatem habebat; quod utrumque large palus praebere poterat.

[2] Neque vero Alexandrinis in gerendis negotiis cunctatio ulla aut mora inferebatur. Nam in omnis partis, per quas fines Aegypti regnumque pertinet, legatos conquisitoresque dilectus habendi causa miserant magnumque numerum in oppidum telorum atque tormentorum convexerant et innumerabilem multitudinem adduxerant. Nec minus in urbe maximae armorum erant institutae officinae. Servos praeterea puberes armaverant; quibus domini locupletiores victum cotidianum stipendiumque praebebant. Hac multitudine disposita munitiones semotarum partium tuebantur; veteranas cohortis vacuas in celeberrimis urbis locis habebant, ut quacumque regione pugnaretur integris viribus ad auxilium ferendum opponi possent. Omnibus viis atque angiportis triplicem vallum obduxerant -- erat autem quadrato exstructus saxo neque minus XL pedes altitudinis habebat -- quaeque partes urbis inferiores erant, has altissimis turribus denorum tabulatorum munierant. Praeterea alias ambulatorias totidem tabulatorum confixerant subiectisque eas rotis funibus iumentisque obiectis derectis plateis in quamcumque erat visum partem movebant.

**English Translation:**

[1] With the Alexandrian War having broken out, Caesar summoned the entire fleet from Rhodes and from Syria and Cilicia; he called up archers from Crete and cavalry from Malchus, king of the Nabataeans; he ordered artillery to be gathered from everywhere, grain to be sent, and reinforcements to be brought up. In the meantime the fortifications were being strengthened daily by construction work, and all the parts of the city that appeared to be less than solid were being fitted with mantlets and protective sheds; battering rams were driven through openings in buildings into the adjacent buildings, and as much ground as was cleared by demolition or taken by force, by that much the fortifications were extended. For Alexandria is largely safe from fire, since its buildings lack timber framing and beams and are held together by courses of masonry and arches, and their roofs are of rubble or tile. Caesar was chiefly concerned to cut off, by means of siege-works and siege screens, the narrowest part of the city -- a strip created by a marsh lying to the south -- from the rest of the urban area, aiming first that, since his battle line was divided across two parts of the city, it should be directed by a single plan and command; second, that he could send help to hard-pressed sectors and bring support from one part of the city to another; and most of all that he might have ample supplies of water and fodder, of the first of which he had too little, and of the second none at all -- both of which the marsh could supply in abundance.

[2] Nor were the Alexandrians suffering any delay or slowdown in the management of their affairs. For they had sent envoys and recruiting officers to every part of the territory over which Egypt's borders and kingdom extended, and had brought into the city a great quantity of projectiles and artillery, and had assembled an innumerable multitude. No less great were the arms workshops established inside the city. They had additionally armed all their male slaves of fighting age, whose wealthier owners provided them with daily food and pay. This multitude disposed, they kept guard over the fortifications on the more distant parts of the perimeter; they held veteran cohorts on standby in the most frequented parts of the city, so that wherever in the city fighting broke out, they could be deployed at full strength to bring aid. In all the streets and alleyways they had built a triple rampart -- it was constructed of squared stone and stood no less than forty feet high -- and the lower parts of the city they had fortified with very tall towers ten stories high. In addition they had built other movable towers of the same height, and by placing them on wheels and hauling them with ropes and draft animals along the straight streets, they could move them to whatever position seemed necessary.

**Analysis: Caesar vs. Hirtius, head to head**

Compare the opening of BA chapter 1 with the opening of BG Book I. Caesar: *Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres.* Hirtius: *Bello Alexandrino conflato Caesar Rhodo atque ex Syria Ciliciaque omnem classem arcessit.* Both open with a statement of situation. But where Caesar's first sentence is a self-contained geographical orientation (five words, one main verb, complete), Hirtius opens with an ablative absolute (*bello Alexandrino conflato*) that presupposes you know what the Alexandrian War is, and then immediately launches into a list of troop-gathering activities across multiple provinces. The structural assumptions are different: Caesar orients the reader; Hirtius assumes the reader is already oriented.

The sentence at the end of chapter 1 -- from *Caesar maxime studebat* to *palus praebere poterat* -- is a good example of Hirtius at his most Hirtian. It is a single long sentence articulated through three nested purpose constructions (*ut*... *ut*... *ut*...), each explaining a different reason for Caesar's strategic objective, with an ablative absolute (*quarum alterius rei copiam exiguam, alterius nullam omnino facultatem habebat*) embedded inside the third. This is the sort of sentence Caesar would have broken into two or three shorter ones. Hirtius prefers to hold the whole argument in suspension until the final clause resolves it. The sentence is correct, elegant, and slightly harder to parse than anything in the first seven books.

Chapter 2's description of the Alexandrian defenses is the other side of the coin: Hirtius writing in a mode that is actually closer to Caesar's than the praefatio is. Short declarative statements, military precision, a preference for factual enumeration over elaboration. *Omnibus viis atque angiportis triplicem vallum obduxerant* -- "in all the streets and alleyways they had built a triple rampart" -- is very Caesarian in its compression. But the parenthetical explanation that follows (*erat autem quadrato exstructus saxo neque minus XL pedes altitudinis habebat*) -- describing exactly what kind of stone and exactly how tall -- is more than Caesar would typically supply. Caesar tells you what exists and what it does. Hirtius tells you what it is made of.

The technical vocabulary in these passages (*testudinibus*, *musculis*, *arietes* -- mantlets, protective sheds, battering rams; *ambulatorias turris* -- movable towers) is denser than anything in the standard BG text. Students who have only read BG I-VII will need a good dictionary for this material, or a good commentary. The concepts are straightforward once explained; the vocabulary itself is the obstacle.

The *in medias res* opening deserves a final note. Caesar's BC also begins abruptly, but it does provide some political context in its opening chapters. The BA provides none: it simply begins with the war already in progress, the reader presumably having read the BC, and the political situation around Ptolemy XIII and Cleopatra entirely unexplained. To understand what is happening, why Caesar is trapped in Alexandria, who the factions are, and what the stakes are, a reader needs either a good introduction or prior knowledge of Hellenistic Egypt. This contextual demand -- more than any grammatical feature -- is what makes the BA harder than BG.

---

## Legacy and Impact

Hirtius matters to the tradition primarily as a preserver and completer. Without BG Book VIII, we would lack the account of the final stages of Caesar's Gallic campaigns, including the mopping-up operations that permanently secured Roman control of the province. Without the *Bellum Alexandrinum* (if it is indeed his), we would have no coherent account of the war that kept Caesar pinned in Egypt for several months and produced the *veni vidi vici* episode. These are not small gaps.

As a writer, Hirtius served an important documentary function precisely because he was not quite as good as Caesar. The praefatio's self-conscious humility -- real or performed -- ensured that the Corpus Caesarianum was understood as a collaborative project: Caesar's design, Hirtius's completion. This framing preserved the commentaries' integrity as a literary corpus rather than allowing them to circulate as disconnected fragments.

The BA's authorship question has generated more scholarly ink than the text perhaps merits, but it has also produced some genuinely illuminating stylistic analysis of what distinguishes Caesarian from post-Caesarian military Latin. The answer, broadly, is this: Caesar wrote for a political audience that knew the players and needed to be persuaded. Hirtius wrote for a historical audience that needed the record completed.

One final irony: Hirtius died completing someone else's military campaign (the war against Antony) just as he had made his literary name completing someone else's literary campaign. He was a consistently excellent second-in-command who never got the chance to be first at anything. Given what happened to most of the people who tried to be first in 43 BC, this might not be entirely bad luck.

---

## Final Rating

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

BG Book VIII sits at the easier end of C, close to the Caesar D that surrounds it in the curriculum. A student who has successfully read BG I-VII will find Book VIII more demanding but not dramatically so; the main adjustment is the sentence architecture and the occasional shift in vocabulary register. With solid preparation it is thoroughly approachable.

The *Bellum Alexandrinum* earns the C more decisively. The technical vocabulary of urban siege warfare, the unfamiliar geography of Alexandria and the Nile Delta, the assumed knowledge of Hellenistic court politics, and the *in medias res* narrative structure all add up to a text that requires more preparation than any of Caesar's own books. It is not difficult in the way that Tacitus or Sallust are difficult -- the syntax is never gratuitously dense, the vocabulary never deliberately obscure -- but it asks more from the reader than it gives in return in terms of contextual support.

The tier is C rather than D because Hirtius, unlike Caesar, cannot be read cold. He assumes you know where you are.

# TITUS LUCRETIUS CARUS (c. 99--55 BC)

## Biography

Almost nothing is known about the life of Titus Lucretius Carus. His birth date is uncertain, his death date is uncertain, his social class is uncertain, his hometown is unknown, and he left no letters, no recorded friendships, and no portraits. He exists entirely in his poem. This is, for an Epicurean philosopher, a perfectly appropriate outcome. Epicurus's famous maxim was *lathe biosas* -- "live hidden." Lucretius followed it so thoroughly that two thousand years of scholarship have failed to locate him. He achieved in life exactly what he recommended in philosophy: radical, total, successful obscurity.

The one source that claims to know something specific about his life is also the one we should trust least. Jerome's *Chronicle* (written in the 380s AD, roughly four centuries after Lucretius's death) reports that Lucretius was driven mad by a love potion administered by his wife; that he wrote several books of poetry during intervals of lucidity; that Cicero "emended" them; and that he died by his own hand at the age of forty-three. This story has everything a hostile late-antique Christian writer would want from an Epicurean atheist: madness, a scheming woman, and a suicide. It has almost no corroborating evidence. Cicero does mention the *De Rerum Natura* in a letter to his brother Quintus in February 54 BC, writing that the poem shows "many flashes of genius, but also much art." This suggests he had read it and thought well of it. Whether he "emended" it in the editorial sense or merely discussed it with someone is entirely unclear. The love potion, the madness, and the suicide are almost certainly invented -- or at minimum colored by the tradition's hostility to Epicurean materialism. Jerome is not a disinterested source on a poet who argued that the soul is made of atoms and death is simply nothing.

What we can say: Lucretius lived sometime in the first century BC, probably died around 55-50 BC, and addressed his poem to Gaius Memmius, a Roman aristocrat, patron of letters, and sometime politician (also, incidentally, the dedicatee of Catullus's poem collection -- Memmius apparently attracted poets the way other Romans attracted clients). The poem itself, at approximately 7,400 lines across six books, shows signs of being unfinished: some books end abruptly, certain passages are marked as requiring revision, and the structural balance between the books is slightly off. It looks like a work that was still being polished when its author died. Whoever published it posthumously did the world a significant service.

The man remains invisible. The poem remains extraordinary. For once, this seems fitting.

---

## Most Important Works

Lucretius wrote, as far as anyone knows, exactly one thing: the *De Rerum Natura* ("On the Nature of Things"). It is a philosophical poem in six books of dactylic hexameter, presenting the complete Epicurean physical system in Latin verse. It is also one of the most ambitious literary projects in the history of the Latin language -- which is saying something in a century that also produced Caesar, Cicero, and Catullus.

The six books are organized as a sequence of interlocking arguments, each building on what came before:

**Book I** establishes the foundational principles: everything that exists is composed of *corpora* (bodies) and *inane* (void). Nothing comes from nothing (*nil posse creari de nihilo*), and nothing can be reduced to nothing. Matter is eternal, indestructible, and invisible at the fundamental level. The atoms exist; the void exists; everything else is a compound of the two.

**Book II** covers the motion of atoms -- they fall through the void, collide, and form compounds through an unpredictable swerve called the *clinamen*. The variety of atomic shapes accounts for the variety of things in the world. The world, and all worlds, are neither specially created nor uniquely permanent.

**Book III** is the book that should terrify the pious and comfort the wise: the soul (*anima* and *animus*) is a physical compound, made of especially fine and mobile atoms. When the body dies, the soul-compound dissolves too. There is no survival, no afterlife, no punishment -- and therefore nothing to fear from death. *Nil igitur mors est ad nos* -- "death, therefore, is nothing to us." When we exist, death is not present; when death is present, we no longer exist.

**Book IV** handles perception, imagination, and -- at considerable length -- the pathology of sexual love, which Lucretius regards as a particularly vicious form of self-delusion. He does not recommend celibacy, exactly; he recommends not falling in love. This is the Epicurean position, and Lucretius argues it with the enthusiasm of a man who has strong opinions on the matter.

**Book V** describes how the world came to be (not by divine creation but through the chance collision of atoms), how celestial phenomena work (in very un-modern ways, but rationally), and how human civilization developed from primitive beginnings to its current state. It is the closest thing in classical Latin literature to evolutionary social history.

**Book VI** tackles the natural phenomena most likely to inspire religious terror: thunder and lightning (not Jupiter's anger -- atmospheric electricity), earthquakes, volcanoes, and the plague. The book ends with a long description of the plague of Athens in 430 BC, adapted from Thucydides, presenting the ultimate natural catastrophe without a shred of divine explanation. It is one of the most powerful passages in all of Latin poetry and may also be the passage Lucretius never got to revise.

## Writing Style and Difficulty

### On choosing poetry, or: the honey gambit

Epicurus, the philosophical father of everything Lucretius is doing, distrusted poetry. Poetry deceives, distorts, and appeals to the emotions rather than to reason. For a system dedicated to clear-eyed understanding of reality, verse seemed counterproductive.

Lucretius disagreed. His argument, stated with characteristic candor at DRN I.926-950, is that Epicurean philosophy is genuinely hard to swallow -- dry, abstract, and counterintuitive. So he coats it in verse the way doctors coat bitter medicine in honey, to make it palatable:

> *sed vel uti pueris absinthia taetra medentes*
> *cum dare conantur, prius oras pocula circum*
> *contingunt mellis dulci flavoque liquore,*
> *ut puerorum aetas inprovida ludificetur*
> *labrorum tenus, interea perpotet amarum*
> *absinthi laticem deceptaque non capiatur,*
> *sed potius tali facto recreata valescat,*
> *sic ego nunc, quoniam haec ratio plerumque videtur*
> *tristior esse quibus non est tractata, retroque*
> *volgus abhorret ab hac, volui tibi suaviloquenti*
> *carmine Pierio rationem exponere nostram*
> *et quasi musaeo dulci contingere melle*

Just as doctors, when they try to give foul wormwood to children, first coat the rim of the cup around with sweet golden honey, so that the unsuspecting age of childhood is fooled at the lip -- meanwhile they drink down the bitter draught of wormwood and though deceived are not harmed, but rather by this treatment are restored to health; so I now, since this theory generally seems too grim to those not familiar with it, and the crowd shrinks back from it, wished to set out our philosophy for you in sweet-tongued Pierian song, and as it were to touch it with the sweet honey of the Muses.

The irony is glorious: Lucretius chose poetry to make Epicurus easier, and in doing so produced one of the most demanding poems in the Latin language. This was not entirely his fault.

### The vocabulary problem: inventing a language

Latin had no philosophical vocabulary when Lucretius sat down to write. Greek philosophy had spent four centuries developing precise technical terms. Latin had nothing equivalent. Lucretius solved this in two ways: by coining new Latin words, and by repurposing existing ones.

*Primordia* (primordial elements, i.e., atoms) -- Lucretius's coinage, combining *primus* + *oriri*. *Genitalia corpora* (generative bodies -- his most common periphrasis for atoms). *Inane* (the void -- a normal Latin adjective meaning "empty" promoted to a technical noun). *Clinamen* (the atomic swerve -- from *clinare*, "to bend" -- the word barely exists in Latin outside Lucretius). *Concilium* (assembly of atoms, from a word that usually means a political gathering). Even *rerum natura* itself, the title of the poem, is a deliberate translation of the Greek *physika*.

The result is that reading Lucretius requires not just a Latin dictionary but a willingness to follow how he has redefined common words into technical instruments, and to track coinages that appear nowhere else in the language.

### Archaic vocabulary: Ennius's ghost

Lucretius admired Quintus Ennius, the great second-century BC epic poet whose *Annales* established the tradition of Latin hexameter before Virgil swept the field. He says so explicitly, calling Ennius "our Ennius" and placing him among the philosophical visionaries. Stylistically, he imitates Ennius's archaic texture deliberately: genitive singular forms like *aulai* (for *aulae*), *materiai* (for *materiae*), *Iphianassai* (the Homeric form of Iphigenia's name, rather than the Latinized *Iphigenia*), old endings, old rhythms.

This archaism serves two purposes: it elevates the tone toward the heroic tradition, and it creates a deliberate distance from Ciceronian classical Latin. Lucretius is not writing the clean, calibrated prose of Caesar's era. He is reaching back toward an older, rougher, more muscular tradition. For a reader trained on Cicero or even Caesar, the lexical texture comes as a mild but persistent shock.

### The image sequences

Perhaps Lucretius's most distinctive technique is what we might call the philosophical snapshot: extended visual descriptions embedded in the argument to make an abstract point concrete and vivid. The most famous is the motes of dust in a sunbeam (II.114-128): "Often in sunny spots when sunbeams filter through the darkness of a shuttered room, you see a multitude of tiny bodies mingling in a multitude of ways through the empty space in the light of the beam..." He uses this image to make visible the invisible movement of atoms. He describes a flock of sheep on a distant hill -- all white, all motionless from a distance -- to explain why distant objects lose their individual properties (II.317-322). He describes the armor of a warrior abandoned on a battlefield, rusting into the earth (II.61-79), to illustrate atomic dissolution.

These passages are among the most beautiful in Latin poetry. They are also, for the translator and student, a significant additional challenge: Lucretius expects you to hold the philosophical argument in your head while processing an extended visual sequence that is doing philosophical work in disguise.

### The difficulty composite

The result of all this is a text that is difficult on almost every axis simultaneously: poetic form (hexameter, with all its metrical demands), archaic vocabulary (requiring a specialized glossary), philosophical neologisms (requiring systematic learning of the Epicurean system), dense arguments (requiring that you follow a six-book chain of reasoning), and elaborate image sequences (requiring that you see what Lucretius is describing and understand why he is describing it). It is a poem that rewards both the close reader and the patient one -- and that has very little patience for any other kind.

---

## The Lucretian Myth: On Pessimism

Before the excerpt, a necessary detour.

Lucretius is frequently described as a "pessimistic philosopher" -- someone who finds comfort in the bleakness of materialism, who tells us that we are just temporary configurations of atoms that will eventually dissolve, and that nothing ultimately matters. This reading is wrong. It is wrong in the way that matters most: it gets the emotional register backwards.

Lucretius is furious. He is furious on behalf of humanity, which has been terrorized for centuries by *religio* -- and it is crucial to understand what that word means in his usage. Not "religion" in any modern confessional sense. *Religio* for Lucretius is superstition: the irrational fear that the gods are watching, judging, and punishing human beings; that thunderstorms are divine anger; that plague is retribution; that death is followed by eternal torment. This fear, he argues, has caused incalculable suffering. It drove Agamemnon to slit his daughter's throat at Aulis. It keeps human beings in a state of chronic anxiety that prevents them from ever simply living.

Epicurean physics -- the atoms, the void, the soul's dissolution at death -- is not nihilism. It is liberation. If you understand that the gods do not intervene in human affairs, you cannot be terrorized by their supposed anger. If you understand that death is simply the dissolution of the atomic compound that is you, you cannot be paralyzed by the fear of punishment afterward. *Nil igitur mors est ad nos* -- "death is nothing to us." This is not resignation. It is the removal of the largest source of unnecessary suffering in human life.

The goal is *ataraxia* (tranquility of mind, the absence of anxiety) and *aponia* (freedom from bodily pain). Together they constitute the Epicurean ideal -- not pleasure in the hedonistic sense, but the peaceful condition of a person who has understood how the universe actually works and is no longer afraid of it. Living in harmony with the rhythm of the universe, not fighting shadows.

Lucretius is not telling Memmius that nothing matters. He is telling Memmius that the things he has been taught to fear do not exist, and that the things that do exist -- the beauty of the world, the fact of being alive at all, the capacity for calm rational thought -- are worth having precisely because they are finite and real. He writes about atoms with the intensity of a man who finds them beautiful.

This is not pessimism. This is the most committed form of optimism: the kind that refuses to flinch from the truth.

## Latin Excerpt

### De Rerum Natura I.80-101 -- The Sacrifice of Iphigenia

**Source:** Lucretius, *De Rerum Natura*, Book I, lines 80-101, from *The Latin Library* (thelatinlibrary.com/lucretius/lucretius1.shtml)

**Context:** This passage comes near the beginning of Book I. Lucretius has introduced his subject and addressed Memmius. He then anticipates an objection: won't this godless, materialist philosophy lead Memmius to impiety and wickedness? His answer is a pre-emptive reversal -- he inverts the accusation completely. It is not Epicurean physics that leads to *scelera* (crimes, impiety). It is *religio* -- superstitious religious fear -- that does. And to demonstrate what *religio* is capable of, he tells the story of the sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis.

The story, from Greek mythology: the Greek fleet was becalmed at Aulis, unable to sail to Troy. The seer Calchas declared that the goddess Diana (Artemis) was angry and could only be appeased by the sacrifice of Iphigenia, daughter of the commander Agamemnon. Agamemnon complied. The fleet sailed.

Note that Lucretius calls the girl *Iphianassa*, her archaic Homeric name rather than the usual Latin *Iphigenia* -- a deliberate choice that activates the heroic epic register. The altar is described as belonging to *Trivia* (Diana as goddess of crossroads), and the Greek commanders are the *ductores Danaum delecti, prima virorum* -- "chosen leaders of the Danaans, first among men." The framing elevates the scene to emphasize the full horror of what "first among men" are capable of, in the name of *religio*.

**Latin Text:**

> Illud in his rebus vereor, ne forte rearis
> impia te rationis inire elementa viamque
> indugredi sceleris. quod contra saepius illa
> religio peperit scelerosa atque impia facta.
> Aulide quo pacto Triviai virginis aram
> Iphianassai turparunt sanguine foede
> ductores Danaum delecti, prima virorum.
> cui simul infula virgineos circum data comptus
> ex utraque pari malarum parte profusast,
> et maestum simul ante aras adstare parentem
> sensit et hunc propter ferrum celare ministros
> aspectuque suo lacrimas effundere civis,
> muta metu terram genibus summissa petebat.
> nec miserae prodesse in tali tempore quibat,
> quod patrio princeps donarat nomine regem;
> nam sublata virum manibus tremibundaque ad aras
> deductast, non ut sollemni more sacrorum
> perfecto posset claro comitari Hymenaeo,
> sed casta inceste nubendi tempore in ipso
> hostia concideret mactatu maesta parentis,
> exitus ut classi felix faustusque daretur.
> tantum religio potuit suadere malorum.

*(DRN I.80-101)*

**English Translation:**

In these matters I fear lest perhaps you suppose that you are entering upon the first principles of impious reasoning and setting out on a path of wickedness. But on the contrary, it is religion that has more often given birth to deeds wicked and impious. Consider how at Aulis the chosen leaders of the Danaans, first among men, foully stained the altar of the Trivian virgin with the blood of Iphianassa. The moment the ribbon was placed around her girl's tresses, streaming equally down both sides of her cheeks, and she noticed her father standing grief-stricken before the altars, and the attendants nearby concealing the iron blade, and her fellow citizens pouring out tears at the sight of her -- struck mute with terror, she sank to her knees seeking the ground. It could not help the poor girl at that moment that she had been the first to give the king the name of father; for she was lifted in the hands of men and led trembling to the altars, not so that after the solemn rite of sacrifice was complete she might be escorted by the bright wedding hymn, but so that, chaste, in the very season of marriage, she might fall as a sorrowful victim in an unholy slaughter by her father's hand -- so that a fortunate and auspicious departure might be given to the fleet. Such great evils could religion persuade.

**Analysis:**

The passage works in two movements. The first (lines 80-84) is the logical setup: you fear that atomism leads to impiety? Let me show you what impiety actually looks like. The second (lines 84-101) is the narrative demonstration, and it is one of the most controlled pieces of emotional argumentation in Latin poetry.

Notice how Lucretius positions the girl. She is named in the archaic Greek form (*Iphianassa*), which removes her from the safe distance of myth into the heroic tradition's direct light. She is *prima virorum* -- she is surrounded by the best of men, the leaders of the Greek world. And she is, in the most brutal detail, a frightened child: the ribbon in her hair, the sight of her father's grief, the iron blade half-hidden by the attendants. *Muta metu terram genibus summissa petebat* -- "mute with fear, sinking to her knees, she sought the ground." Five words in Latin. There is no verbal elaboration, no pathetic fallacy, no rhetorical cushioning. Just the action, and the silence.

The detail *quod patrio princeps donarat nomine regem* ("because she had been the first to call the king by the name of father") is devastating. She was the first to call him *father*. She cannot help herself now, because this very relationship -- daughter to father, the relationship that should offer the most protection -- is what is killing her. Agamemnon is not a monster; he is a man who has weighed his daughter against his fleet, and the fleet won. That is exactly what religion asked of him, and he complied.

The grammatical turn in lines 95-100 is worth parsing carefully. *Non ut* ... *sed ut*: "not so that she might be escorted with the wedding hymn... but so that she might fall as a victim in unholy slaughter." The *non ut / sed ut* contrast introduces two purpose clauses, one imagined (the proper outcome of a girl at the appropriate age -- marriage, the *Hymenaeus*) and one real (sacrifice). The word *inceste* -- "impurely, in an unholy manner" -- modifies what is supposed to be a *casta* (pure, chaste) action. It is the virginal sacrifice of a virgin, sacred and obscene simultaneously.

Then: *tantum religio potuit suadere malorum.* "So much evil could religion persuade." The line is end-stopped, metrically clean, and comes after a long, syntactically complex narrative. The sudden simplicity is like a hammer blow. Eleven Latin words -- one sentence -- and the entire case against superstition is made.

The archaic vocabulary throughout is significant: *malarum* (cheeks, an archaism), *Aulide* and *Iphianassai* (archaic Greek-derived forms), *Triviai* (archaic genitive of *Trivia*). Lucretius is deliberately writing in the register of Ennius's epic, the tradition of Rome's oldest poets, to show that the critique of *religio* is not novelty -- it is as old as poetry itself.

## Legacy and Impact

After the DRN was apparently published posthumously -- by Cicero, by his brother Quintus, or by some unknown hand; no one knows for certain -- it entered the world to a somewhat muted reception. Cicero, as noted, valued it. Virgil read it with exceptional attention: the echoes run throughout the Georgics, which borrows Lucretius's hexameter register, his extended image sequences, and his philosophical ambition, and through the underworld scenes of the Aeneid. Ovid mentions Lucretius in a famous line that is also, inadvertently, almost accurate: *carmina sublimis tunc sunt peritura Lucreti / exitio terras cum dabit una dies* -- "the songs of lofty Lucretius will perish only when one day delivers the earth to its end." The DRN came genuinely close to perishing.

With the rise of Christianity, an Epicurean poem denying the afterlife, dismissing divine intervention, and treating death as simple annihilation was not a text that monasteries were eager to copy. Only a handful of manuscripts survived the medieval period. The critical moment came in January 1417, when the humanist Poggio Bracciolini, visiting a monastery in Germany -- probably Fulda -- found a manuscript of the DRN in the library and had it copied. This is one of the great intellectual rescues of the Renaissance: a text that was a few copying decisions away from complete disappearance found its way back into circulation. Stephen Greenblatt's popular history *The Swerve* (2011) dramatizes the event, though it somewhat overstates the poem's subsequent impact on Renaissance thought. The core story is accurate.

After Poggio's copy began to circulate, Lucretius became increasingly available and increasingly read. Montaigne read him closely and the materialism marked him. Enlightenment thinkers absorbed him; Thomas Jefferson owned several copies of the DRN and described himself in correspondence as an Epicurean, meaning substantially what Lucretius taught: material world, no afterlife, happiness through moderation and the removal of irrational fear.

Within Latin literary history, Lucretius's most important legacy is Virgil. The Georgics are the most Lucretian text after the DRN itself -- same meter, same didactic ambition, same philosophical register, same image sequences doing philosophical work. Virgil's debt was recognized in antiquity and is unmistakable on the page. He took Lucretius's hexameter and made it smoother; he took his philosophical reach and domesticated it within Roman civic ideology; he turned the Epicurean project of liberation from fear into a celebration of Italian land and labor. The relationship is not influence but transformation. Reading Lucretius after Virgil, you understand what Virgil was working with and working against simultaneously.

## Proposed Tier

**Tier: A (Very Difficult)**

Lucretius sits solidly in the A tier -- and firmly at the higher end of it.

**Medium:** Dactylic hexameter is harder than prose on principle, and Lucretius's hexameter is no exception. The verse imposes word order constraints that produce persistent hyperbaton, and the enjambment across lines means that sentence structure frequently does not coincide with line structure. Students trained on prose must recalibrate entirely.

**Lexicon:** The vocabulary burden is exceptional and operates on three levels simultaneously. First, archaic forms (*aulai*, *materiai*, *Iphianassai*, *Triviai*) that are not in standard dictionaries or, when they are, are marked as rare. Second, the standard Epicurean technical vocabulary (*primordia*, *inane*, *genitalia corpora*, *clinamen*) which must be learned as a system rather than word by word. Third, the image-sequence vocabulary, which is often concrete, visual, and from registers -- farming, navigation, metalwork -- not typically covered in introductory Latin courses.

**Syntactic complexity:** The hexameter line drives heavy use of hyperbaton (separation of words that belong together), which in prose would simply appear next to each other but in verse are spread across the line or across enjambment. Add to this that Lucretius's arguments are genuinely complex multi-step logical sequences that require the reader to track the thread of reasoning while simultaneously parsing difficult syntax.

**Allusion density:** Lucretius alludes to Ennius constantly, to Empedocles (his great philosophical predecessor-rival), to Homer, and to the entire Greek scientific tradition. Understanding the allusions requires either substantial background or a very good commentary.

**Why not S?** The S tier is for texts that combine maximum difficulty across all criteria simultaneously, including the kind of deliberate obscurity or compression that Tacitus, late Virgil, or Horace at his most Pindaric can produce. Lucretius is difficult but not obscure: his arguments, once the vocabulary is learned and the verse is parsed, move forward with reasonable clarity. He wants to be understood. He is not hiding. He is putting barriers of form and vocabulary between Memmius and the ideas, but the ideas themselves, once reached, are not designed to elude. For the reader who has done the work, Lucretius opens. For Tacitus or Persius, having done the work is no guarantee.

# GAIUS SALLUSTIUS CRISPUS -- SALLUST (86--35 BC)

## Biography

The greatest irony in Latin literary history is that the man who wrote most incisively about Roman corruption was himself one of the more spectacularly corrupt public figures of his generation. Gaius Sallustius Crispus was born in 86 BC in Amiternum, a small Sabine town, to an equestrian family -- good enough to open doors in Rome, not quite good enough to open all of them automatically. He entered political life as a populist tribune of the plebs in 52 BC, the year of Clodius's murder and the subsequent political chaos, and used the position with characteristic energy to attack Cicero and Milo. Then, in 50 BC, the censors expelled him from the Senate on charges of immorality -- the specific allegations are disputed, but the phrasing suggests something beyond political convenience: *probri gratia*, "for the sake of disgrace."

Enter Gaius Julius Caesar. Sallust attached himself to Caesar with the speed of a man who understood which way the political wind was blowing, served as a military tribune and later quaestor (which automatically restored his senatorial rank), and participated in Caesar's African campaigns with sufficient competence to be rewarded. In 46 BC, following the defeat of the Pompeians at Thapsus, Sallust was appointed governor of the new province of Africa Nova -- what had been Numidia, the kingdom whose history he would later write.

His governorship of Numidia was distinguished by the thoroughness of his plunder. The provincials accused him of extortion so systematic and brazen that charges were brought before the courts. The charges were, again, dropped -- Caesar saw to it -- but the scale of what Sallust had extracted from the province became something of an open scandal in Rome. He retired from public life entirely after Caesar's assassination in 44 BC, never seeking office again, and spent his remaining years -- he died around 35 BC -- in learned leisure funded by the proceeds of his time in Africa.

The monument to those proceeds is the *Horti Sallustiani*, the Gardens of Sallust, one of the most extravagant private landscape projects in the history of Rome. Sallust laid out his gardens on the slope between the Quirinal and Pincian hills, with artificial terraces, elaborate waterworks, sculptures, a triumphal arch of his own construction, and grounds that rivaled anything the old aristocracy could show. They were so magnificent that Augustus subsequently purchased them to use as imperial property. The man who wrote that avarice "subverts faith, honesty, and all other good qualities" and that wealth had corrupted the Roman character built himself the most ostentatious monument to wealth in the city.

Now open *De Coniuratione Catilinae*, chapters 1-4. Sallust explains why he has turned to writing history: in a republic so sunk in corruption and faction, honest political participation is impossible. Virtuous men are edged out by the unscrupulous. The only genuine service left to offer is historiography -- recording what happened, with surgical clarity, so that posterity may understand. He presents himself as a man who tried politics, found it intolerable, and withdrew to the higher calling of truth-telling.

Every sentence is technically accurate. It is also one of the most magnificent pieces of self-reinvention in Latin prose. The man who was expelled from the Senate for immorality, who enriched himself through provincial extortion, who survived three political crises by attaching himself to the right powerful patron, presents himself as a disappointed idealist. And then he proceeds to write some of the most penetrating moral analysis in Latin literature. The contradiction is not a bug. It may be the engine.

---

## Most Important Works

Sallust produced three historical works, of which two survive complete and one in fragments.

The *De Coniuratione Catilinae* (also called the *Bellum Catilinae*) covers the conspiracy of 63 BC, in which Lucius Sergius Catilina attempted to seize control of the Roman state through a coordinated armed uprising, only to be crushed by Cicero's consulship and the senatorial decree that led to the execution of his co-conspirators. At roughly 61 chapters, it is a short, tightly compressed monograph -- not an annalistic chronicle but a focused analytical narrative organized around a single crisis and the moral questions it raises. The characters (Catiline, Cicero, Caesar, Cato) are drawn with economic precision, the political analysis is unflinching, and the structure loops back constantly to the opening moral diagnosis: the conspiracy is a symptom, not a disease.

The *Bellum Iugurthinum* covers the Jugurthine War of 111-104 BC, in which the Numidian prince Jugurtha bribed, manipulated, and murdered his way to power while the Roman Senate -- corrupted by his gold -- repeatedly failed to act. At 113 chapters it is longer and more complex than the *Catilinae*, with a wider cast and a more elaborate narrative structure. It introduces one of Sallust's great counter-characters: Gaius Marius, the *novus homo* (new man, i.e. first of his family to reach the consulship) who ultimately defeats Jugurtha through competence and energy rather than aristocratic connection. The war is the diagnosis; Marius is the treatment; the treatment, as Sallust implicitly knows, only delays the disease.

The *Historiae*, covering Roman history from 78 BC onward, survives only in fragments -- speeches and letters extracted by later grammarians, plus scattered quotations. It is a major loss. What survives suggests it was Sallust's most ambitious work: broader in scope, more syntactically dense, and operating at a register of compression that even the *Catilinae* and *Iugurthinum* do not always reach.

Why history? Sallust explains this directly in the *Catilinae* preface: the *animus* -- the mind, the spirit -- must be put to its highest use, and in a republic corroded by faction and greed, the highest use of an honest mind is to record, analyze, and understand. Writing history is not a consolation prize for a failed political career. It is, Sallust argues, the only form of public service that the current state of Roman politics leaves open to a man who refuses to participate in its corruption. This argument is, as noted, somewhat awkward in light of his biography. It is also, within its own logic, completely coherent. Sallust genuinely believed that historiography mattered -- that the surgical analysis of how republics decay was itself a political act.

---

## Writing Style and Difficulty

### Brevitas: the politics of compression

Sallust's defining stylistic feature is *brevitas* -- concision taken to the point of aggression. Where Cicero constructs a periodic sentence that builds through multiple subordinate clauses toward a weighted close, Sallust stops before you expect him to. The sentence ends. A new one begins. Something essential has been left unsaid, and you are expected to supply it.

This is not laziness or carelessness. Sallust is a highly deliberate stylist who has read Thucydides closely enough to understand that compression carries moral weight. A long sentence performs persuasion; a short one performs diagnosis. Sallust is not trying to convince you. He is telling you what happened. The brevity is the tone.

The practical consequence for the reader is that Sallust's Latin frequently omits words, connectives, and transitions that classical Latin convention would ordinarily supply. Subject pronouns are dropped. Verbs are omitted and must be understood from context. The connective tissue between clauses -- the *enim*, the *autem*, the *igitur* -- is either absent or replaced with a blunt juxtaposition. You move from one statement to the next without being told how they relate. Working out the relationship is your job.

### Inconcinnitas: deliberate asymmetry

If brevitas is Sallust's rhythm, inconcinnitas is his key signature. Where classical Latin prose -- Cicero above all, but also Caesar -- builds on parallelism and structural symmetry (*et... et*, *aut... aut*, balanced ablative absolutes, paired accusative-infinitive constructions), Sallust systematically breaks the expected pattern. A clause with three elements is followed by a parallel clause with two. A phrase with an adjective is mirrored by one without. The list that should have four members has three; the antithesis that should be exact is slightly off.

This is not carelessness. It is one of the most sophisticated stylistic choices in Latin literature, and its effect is precisely calibrated: symmetry creates comfort, closure, the sense that the world can be organized into balanced categories. Sallust's asymmetry creates unease. It mimics the actual texture of political reality, where nothing quite balances, where the expected resolution never quite arrives. Reading Sallust, you are always slightly off-footing, always waiting for a structural resolution that is not coming.

The practical difficulty this creates is severe. A reader trained on Cicero or Caesar learns to use syntactic structure as a parsing guide: you know where you are in the sentence because you know what shape the sentence is supposed to have. Sallust removes that guide. The shape changes. You must parse from first principles, word by word, without the structural scaffolding that more symmetrical Latin provides.

### Archaisms and political vocabulary

Sallust writes in an ostentatiously archaic register -- not as archaic as Lucretius or Ennius, but deliberately older than his contemporaries. He uses *lubido* for *libido*, *voltus* for *vultus*, *maxumus* for *maximus*, *optumus* for *optimus*. He favors old political and legal vocabulary: *stipendium*, *imperium*, *magistratus*, *clientelae*, the whole apparatus of republican public life in its formal archaic dress. This is a political choice as much as a stylistic one: the archaic register invokes the authority of the old republic, the mos maiorum, the standards by which the present is being measured and found disastrously wanting.

For students, the archaisms compound the difficulty in a specific way: many of these forms appear in intermediate-level dictionaries only under their classical spellings, and recognizing *lubido* as *libido* requires either prior exposure or alert attention.

### Ellipsis and missing words

Sallust's ellipsis goes beyond the ordinary Latin tolerance for implicit subjects and objects. He omits verbs that must be understood from earlier in the passage; he drops the *esse* that would complete an indirect statement; he writes noun phrases that float grammatically until you work backward to find where they connect. This is not random -- every ellipsis is reconstructable, and working out what is missing is part of reading Sallust correctly. But it demands a higher level of grammatical attention than almost any other prose author in the canon.

### The speeches

Sallust embeds formal speeches throughout both works -- the speeches of Catiline, of Caesar and Cato at the senatorial debate over the conspirators' fate (the dramatic heart of the *Catilinae*), of Marius and Adherbal in the *Iugurthinum*. These speeches are not transcriptions. They are Sallustian compositions in which historical figures speak in Sallust's style, making the arguments Sallust wants them to make. The speech of Marius in BJ 85, in which a *novus homo* from the Italian countryside mocks the aristocrats who sneer at his lack of family portraits while being unable to match his record of service, is one of the most brilliant exercises in ironic ventriloquism in Latin prose -- written by a man who was himself a *novus homo*, who had enriched himself through the same provincial system he had Marius implicitly criticize, and who put these words in a general's mouth approximately forty years after the speech was supposedly delivered.

The Thucydidean model is never far away. Sallust's speeches have the same function as Thucydides's: not to record what was said but to illuminate what was at stake. Understanding this requires knowing Thucydides -- or at minimum understanding that the speeches are analytical instruments, not reportage.

## The Moralist and His Mirror: On Sallust's Anger

Before the excerpts, a word about what Sallust is actually doing when he describes Catiline.

The four opening chapters of the *De Coniuratione Catilinae* lay out Sallust's thesis: Rome has decayed from the virtuous republic of the ancestors into a state of moral catastrophe. The immediate cause is the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC, which eliminated Rome's external rival and with it the discipline that fear of external danger had imposed. Without an enemy to keep them honest, the Romans became corrupt. Corruption produced men like Catiline.

This is the analytical frame. But the portrait of Catiline himself (chapters 5 and 15) is something more complex than analytical framing. There is anger in it, and there is something that looks uncomfortably like fascination -- or recognition. Sallust describes Catiline's physical gifts with something close to admiration (*magna vi et animi et corporis*), acknowledges his endurance and his energy, notes his intelligence, and then pivots: *sed ingenio malo pravoque* -- "but with a character that was evil and perverse." The "but" carries enormous weight. Sallust is not describing a weakling or a fool. He is describing a genuinely formidable man whose gifts were entirely in the service of destruction.

The anger underneath the portrait is not the righteous indignation of a shocked moralist. It is the anger of a man who has looked at Rome's political class closely enough to understand how it worked, who has participated in it himself, and who is writing about its failure from inside the very failure he is describing. When Sallust writes about corruption, he is not writing from outside. He is writing from a position of thorough, personal familiarity. The surgical precision of his analysis is the precision of someone who knows where the organs are because he has operated on them himself.

This is what makes the hidden emotion in the Catiline portrait so hard to pin down: it is not simple disgust, not simple admiration, not simple moral condemnation. It is the portrait of a man who sees in Catiline a kind of dark perfection -- the ultimate product of the system Sallust is diagnosing -- and who cannot quite keep the recognition out of the prose.

## Latin Excerpts

### Excerpt 1: De Coniuratione Catilinae 5 -- The Portrait of Catiline

**Source:** Sallust, *De Coniuratione Catilinae*, chapter 5, from Lacus Curtius / penelope.uchicago.edu

**Context:** Chapter 5 is Sallust's formal *characterisatio* of Catiline -- the compressed moral and physical portrait that sets the reader's frame for everything that follows. It arrives immediately after the moral digression on Rome's decay, so the reader already knows what a corrupted republic produces before Sallust names his protagonist. The portrait does not disappoint: five sections, no wasted word, and a closing image that makes Catiline sound less like a conspirator and more like a force of nature gone badly wrong.

**Latin Text:**

> L. Catilina, nobili genere natus, fuit magna vi et animi et corporis, sed ingenio malo pravoque. Huic ab adulescentia bella intestina, caedes, rapinae, discordia civilis grata fuere, ibique iuventutem suam exercuit. Corpus patiens inediae, algoris, vigiliae supra quam cuiquam credibile est. Animus audax, subdolus, varius, cuius rei lubet simulator ac dissimulator, alieni appetens, sui profusus, ardens in cupiditatibus; satis eloquentiae, sapientiae parum. Vastus animus immoderata, incredibilia, nimis alta semper cupiebat.
>
> Hunc post dominationem L. Sullae lubido maxuma invaserat rei publicae capiundae; neque id quibus modis adsequeretur, dum sibi regnum pararet, quicquam pensi habebat. Agitabatur magis magisque in dies animus ferox inopia rei familiaris et conscientia scelerum, quae utraque iis artibus auxerat, quas supra memoravi. Incitabant praeterea corrupti civitatis mores, quos pessuma ac divorsa inter se mala, luxuria atque avaritia, vexabant.

*(DCC 5.1-8)*

**English Translation:**

Lucius Catiline, born of a noble family, possessed great force of both mind and body, but a character that was evil and perverse. From youth, civil war, murder, plunder, and political strife were welcome to him, and it was in these that he exercised his young years. His body could endure hunger, cold, and sleeplessness to a degree beyond what anyone would believe. His mind was bold, devious, and changeable -- capable of pretending to be anything or dissembling anything as occasion required. Greedy for others' property, wasteful of his own, burning in his desires; sufficiently eloquent, with very little wisdom. His vast spirit always craved things immoderate, unbelievable, impossibly high.

After the domination of Sulla, an overwhelming desire had seized him to seize control of the republic; and he had no scruple about what methods he used, provided he was securing power for himself. His fierce spirit was driven on more and more each day by poverty of private means and by awareness of his crimes -- both of which he had increased through the same practices I described above. He was further incited by the corrupted morals of the state, which were being torn apart by two equally ruinous but opposite vices: extravagance and greed.

**Analysis:**

The opening clause announces the structure of the entire portrait: *nobili genere natus* (noble birth), *magna vi et animi et corporis* (great force of mind and body), then the pivot: *sed ingenio malo pravoque*. Everything Sallust will say about Catiline flows from this "but." He is not a deficient man. He is a man whose genuine force has been put entirely in the service of the wrong ends.

What follows is a masterclass in Sallustian brevitas and inconcinnitas. *Corpus patiens inediae, algoris, vigiliae supra quam cuiquam credibile est.* Three nouns (hunger, cold, sleeplessness) in asyndeton, followed by a comparative clause that does not resolve into a main verb -- just the bare statement of endurance beyond belief, hanging in the air. Then: *Animus audax, subdolus, varius* -- three adjectives -- then a relative clause, then four participial phrases: *cuius rei lubet simulator ac dissimulator, alieni appetens, sui profusus, ardens in cupiditatibus.* The structural parallel with the *corpus* sentence never fully materializes -- the *corpus* sentence has three nouns and a clause; the *animus* sentence has three adjectives, a relative, and four participials. You keep waiting for the shape to balance. It does not.

*Satis eloquentiae, sapientiae parum.* Two nouns in the genitive of quality, no verb. "Enough eloquence, too little wisdom." This is not neutral reporting. It is contempt delivered in six words. And then *Vastus animus immoderata, incredibilia, nimis alta semper cupiebat.* Note the word *vastus*: in Latin it means "vast" but also "desolate," "ravaged," "barren" -- the adjective for deserts and ruins. A *vastus animus* is not just an ambitious soul. It is an empty one, hollowed out by its own limitless wanting.

Sections 6-8 shift the portrait from static description to dynamic causation -- Sallust moves from what Catiline is to why he acts when he does. Section 6 locates the decisive moment: *post dominationem L. Sullae.* Sulla's dictatorship (82-79 BC) is the hinge. It proved that one man could seize the Roman state by force, and Catiline took the lesson. *Lubido* (the archaic form of *libido*) -- desire, appetite, drive -- is the word Sallust gives to this ambition, and it is not a neutral word: throughout the *Catilinae* and *Iugurthinum*, *lubido* marks corrupted motivation, the irrational want that overrides reason and law. *Neque id quibus modis adsequeretur... quicquam pensi habebat*: he had "no scruple" about the means -- the phrase *quicquam pensi habere* (literally "to have anything weighed," hence "to care about anything") is a colloquial legal expression for moral accountability. He had none.

Section 7 names the two internal drivers with characteristic compression: *inopia rei familiaris* (poverty of private means) and *conscientia scelerum* (awareness of crimes). Neither is explained; neither needs to be. The *conscientia scelerum* is especially devastating: it refers back to crimes already committed (the ones the reader will not learn about until chapter 15 and beyond) and presents them not as a confession but as a structural force. The crimes drive the ambition further. The worse Catiline has become, the more he needs to win.

Section 8 broadens the frame to Rome itself: *corrupti civitatis mores* torn by *luxuria atque avaritia* -- two words paired as opposites (*divorsa inter se mala*). Extravagance spends; greed hoards. They seem antithetical, but Sallust argues they are equally destructive manifestations of the same underlying disease: the subordination of civic virtue to private appetite. Catiline is, in this reading, not the cause of Rome's crisis. He is its most perfect symptom.

### Excerpt 2: Bellum Iugurthinum 85 (selected) -- Marius Addresses the Roman People

**Source:** Sallust, *Bellum Iugurthinum*, chapter 85 (selected sections), from Splash Latino (splashlatino.it)

**Context:** It is 107 BC. Gaius Marius, newly elected consul despite having no family connections among the Roman nobility, is about to take command of the war in Numidia that the aristocratic commanders have been managing incompetently for years. Before leaving, he addresses the Roman people in a contio -- a public meeting -- justifying his election and attacking the senatorial class that resents it.

The speech is not a historical transcript. It is a Sallustian composition: Marius speaks in Sallust's style, makes Sallust's arguments, and serves Sallust's analytical purpose, which is to show that the Roman nobility's contempt for talent over lineage is itself a form of the corruption that is destroying the republic. The irony runs deep: Sallust, a *novus homo* from Amiternum who survived politics by attaching himself to powerful patrons and enriched himself through a province, is writing a *novus homo*'s attack on aristocratic privilege.

**Latin Text (selected):**

> Scio ego, Quirites, plerosque non isdem artibus imperium a vobis petere et, postquam adepti sunt, gerere: primo industrios supplices modicos esse, dein per ignaviam et superbiam aetatem agere. Sed mihi contra ea videtur.

> [...]

> Ad hoc, alii si deliquere, vetus nobilitas, maiorum fortia facta, cognatorum et affinium opes, multae clientelae, omnia haec praesidio assunt; mihi spes omnes in memet sitae, quas necesse est virtute et innocentia tutari; nam alia infirma sunt.

> Bellum me gerere cum Iugurtha iussistis, quam rem nobilitas aegerrime tulit. Quaeso, reputate cum animis vestris, num id mutare melius sit, si quem ex illo globo nobilitatis ad hoc aut aliud tale negotium mittatis, hominem veteris prosapiae ac multarum imaginum et nullius stipendi: scilicet ut in tanta re ignarus omnium trepidet, festinet, sumat aliquem ex populo monitorem offici sui. Ita plerumque evenit, ut, quem vos imperare iussistis, is sibi imperatorem alium quaerat.

> Atque ego scio, Quirites, qui, postquam consules facti sunt, et acta maiorum et Graecorum militaria praecepta legere coeperint: praeposteri homines, nam gerere quam fieri tempore posterius, re atque usu prius est.

> Comparate nunc, Quirites, cum illorum superbia me hominem novum. Quae illi audire aut legere solent, eorum partem vidi, alia egomet gessi: quae illi litteris, ea ego militando didici.

*(BJ 85, selected)*

**English Translation:**

I know, Romans, that most men use one set of qualities to seek power from you and another to wield it once they have it: at first they are industrious, deferential, restrained; then they spend their time in laziness and arrogance. But to me the opposite seems right.

[...]

On top of this, when others commit mistakes, they have ancient noble lineage, the brave deeds of their ancestors, the resources of their relatives and in-laws, many networks of clients -- all of these stand ready as protection. For me, all my hopes rest in myself alone, and I must defend them through virtue and integrity; for I have no other resources.

You ordered me to wage war against Jugurtha -- a commission the nobility took very badly. I ask you to consider in your own minds whether it would be better to change this, and send instead one of that cluster of the nobility: a man of ancient lineage and many ancestral portrait masks, and of zero military service. So that in so great a matter, ignorant of everything, he stumbles, rushes about, and takes some man from among the people as a tutor in his own duties. It usually turns out this way: the man you ordered to command goes looking for another commander for himself.

And I am fully aware, Romans, of those who, after being elected consuls, have begun reading the records of their ancestors and the military manuals of the Greeks. Men who do things in the wrong order -- for commanding comes later in time than being made a commander, but earlier in the reality and practice of the job.

Compare me now, Romans -- a new man -- with their arrogance. What they are accustomed to hear about or read, some of it I have seen, and the rest I have carried out myself. What they know from books, I have learned in the field.

**Analysis:**

The opening is a textbook demonstration of Sallust's inconcinnitas. The first sentence establishes a contrast: *non isdem artibus... petere et... gerere* -- "not with the same qualities to seek and to wield." The secondary clause -- *primo industrios supplices modicos esse, dein per ignaviam et superbiam aetatem agere* -- lists three adjectives in the first half (*industrios, supplices, modicos*) and two nouns in the second (*per ignaviam et superbiam*). Three vs. two, adjectives vs. abstract nouns, parallel but not equal. Then: *Sed mihi contra ea videtur.* Six words. No elaboration. The contrast is announced; the proof is the rest of the speech.

The passage on the nobility's safety net vs. Marius's total exposure is the analytical heart. The list of the nobility's protections -- *vetus nobilitas, maiorum fortia facta, cognatorum et affinium opes, multae clientelae* -- accumulates through four noun phrases in asyndeton, then lands on *omnia haec praesidio assunt*. The parallel for Marius: *mihi spes omnes in memet sitae.* One clause. One thing. Himself. Then: *quas necesse est virtute et innocentia tutari.* And the close: *nam alia infirma sunt.* Three words. This is inconcinnitas as argument: the structural imbalance between the two halves of the comparison is the argument.

The two central paragraphs move from defense to attack. *Bellum me gerere cum Iugurtha iussistis, quam rem nobilitas aegerrime tulit* -- the pivot from justifying himself to going after the nobility directly. The description that follows is devastating satire: a noble commander who is *ignarus omnium*, who *trepidat*, who *festinat*, and who has to hire a commoner to explain his own job to him. *Ita plerumque evenit, ut quem vos imperare iussistis, is sibi imperatorem alium quaerat* -- "the man you ordered to command goes looking for another commander." The sentence turns on itself with Sallustian elegance: *imperare iussistis* (you ordered him to command) against *imperatorem alium quaerat* (he looks for another to command him). Same root, inverted roles.

The *praeposteri homines* paragraph is a compressed gem. *Praeposteri* means "doing things in the wrong order" (literally "with the later part in front"), which is exactly the charge: they read the military manuals *after* getting the consulship. *Nam gerere quam fieri tempore posterius, re atque usu prius est* -- "commanding comes later in time than being made a commander, but earlier in the reality and practice." The chiasmus (*tempore posterius / re atque usu prius*) is one of those rare moments when Sallust allows himself a symmetrical structure -- but he loads it with *re atque usu* (two nouns) against the single *tempore*, so even here the balance is slightly off.

The finale (*Comparate nunc*) closes the argument. *Quae illi audire aut legere solent, eorum partem vidi, alia egomet gessi*: they passively receive information (hear or read); he has witnessed and he has acted -- two verbs against one gerund phrase, experience against text. Then the one-liner: *quae illi litteris, ea ego militando didici.* The verb (*didicerunt*) is dropped from the first clause; you supply it from *didici* at the end. Ten words. The entire argument in summary. Sallust at his most concentrated.

## Legacy and Impact

Sallust's immediate successors knew exactly what he had done. Livy, writing a generation later, engaged with him as a predecessor worth measuring against -- and occasionally disagreeing with on matters of fact. Quintilian, a century after that, placed him as the Latin equivalent of Thucydides, the highest compliment ancient literary criticism offered to a historical prose writer. The most consequential inheritor was Tacitus. In the late first and early second century AD, Tacitus took Sallust's methods and drove them to their logical extreme: Sallust's *brevitas* became Tacitean compression; Sallust's inconcinnitas became the unnerving asymmetry of the Annals; Sallust's moralizing digressions became the irony woven into every sentence of the Histories. Ancient critics noticed the connection explicitly. The phrase "Sallustianus Crispus" appears in ancient sources as shorthand for the compressed, adversarial, moralizing historical mode. Tacitus is its fullest realization, but Sallust built the template.

The *Horti Sallustiani*, the monument to his provincial extortion, outlasted him by centuries. Augustus purchased them and they became imperial property. They appear in ancient sources through the late empire and were probably destroyed in the Visigothic sack of Rome in 410 AD -- which would have given Sallust, had he been around, considerable material about the wages of *avaritia*.

His texts survived the Middle Ages in good shape, which is not a given. Medieval readers valued the DCC in particular as a source on late republican history and as a model of compressed Latin. Renaissance humanists found in him a compatible sensibility: sharp analytical vision, contempt for institutional corruption, willingness to write about power without flattering it. Machiavelli read him; the influence is not quotable in the direct sense, but the shared analytical method -- history as the study of how power is acquired, maintained, and lost through moral failure -- is unmistakable.

In Latin education, the DCC remains one of the most widely taught texts in the advanced curriculum, typically introduced after Caesar and Cicero when students are ready for something that will genuinely challenge them. Nothing in those earlier authors prepares a student for the first sentence of chapter 5, and the recalibration it forces -- parse without structural prediction, follow without symmetry, supply what is missing -- is itself a form of education. Students who have read Sallust know something about Latin that students who have not read Sallust do not know. The lesson is not comfortable. That is the point.

## Proposed Tier

**Tier: S (God Please Help)**

This decision requires explanation, because the case for high A is real and the user has framed it that way. Here is why S is correct.

The S tier is reserved for texts that combine maximum difficulty across multiple criteria simultaneously. Sallust qualifies on every axis -- and the axes compound each other in a way that is specific to his style.

**Lexicon:** Archaic forms (*lubido*, *voltus*, *maxumus*, *optumus*), specialized republican political vocabulary (*stipendium*, *clientelae*, *mos maiorum*), and Thucydidean calques (deliberate Latin translations of Greek analytical vocabulary). None of these are uniquely extreme, but they add up.

**Syntactic complexity -- inconcinnitas and brevitas:** This is where Sallust earns S. The combination of deliberate asymmetry with deliberate compression means that the two standard tools students use to parse Latin prose -- (1) predict the structure from its opening, (2) use symmetry to align parallel elements -- are both actively undermined. You cannot predict the structure because Sallust changes it. You cannot use symmetry to align elements because the elements are deliberately misaligned. Every sentence must be parsed from scratch, without scaffolding. This is syntactic difficulty of a qualitatively different kind from what authors like Caesar or even Cicero present.

**Stylistic figures:** Sallust uses ellipsis systematically, asyndeton relentlessly, and chiasmus only to break it. The effect is a prose texture that requires the reader to be both very fast (following the rhythm) and very slow (recovering the omitted elements). Doing both simultaneously is hard.

**Allusion density and political subtext:** Understanding Sallust fully requires knowing Thucydides (whose compressed, asymmetric Greek Sallust is consciously adapting), Cato the Elder (whose archaisms Sallust selectively imports), and the entire political landscape of the late republic (without which the ironies in the speeches and moral digressions are simply invisible). This is not ordinary allusion -- it is a sustained stylistic and intellectual conversation with specific predecessors that most students will not be equipped to follow.

**Why not just high A?** High A (the Lucretius position) is appropriate for texts that are very difficult on multiple criteria but that ultimately want to be understood -- that clear paths through their difficulty for the reader who has done the work. Sallust does not do this. His style is adversarial. The inconcinnitas is not just an obstacle; it is a message: reality is not symmetrical, and the reader who comes to Sallust expecting Ciceronian comfort will not get it. There is no reward at the end of the parsing process equivalent to Lucretius's beautiful arguments or Caesar's elegant clarity. There is just more Sallust.

Tacitus, who studied Sallust with enough devotion that later critics called him a student of his master, takes these techniques further -- more compression, more archaism, more brutal ellipsis. The Annals are S tier with no debate. Sallust in his extant works does not quite reach Tacitean density. But he operates in the same register, and the difficulty is the same in kind if slightly less extreme in degree. S is correct.

# GAIUS VALERIUS CATULLUS (c. 84--54 BC)

## Biography

Gaius Valerius Catullus was born around 84 BC in Verona, in Cisalpine Gaul -- technically not yet Roman Italy, but wealthy and connected enough that his father moved comfortably in Roman circles. Connected enough, in fact, to host Julius Caesar when the general passed through the region on official business. This must have made for interesting dinners, given what Catullus would later write about Caesar -- which was not, to put it diplomatically, a glowing endorsement.

At some point in his twenties Catullus moved to Rome, fell into its literary scene, and fell -- with the force of someone pushed off a cliff -- in love with a woman he called "Lesbia" in his poems. The pseudonym was not subtle: Lesbia = Sappho's island = the greatest lyric poet the ancient world had known. The identification behind the mask is almost certain: Clodia Metelli, wife of the consul Quintus Caecilius Metellus Celer, sister of the demagogue Publius Clodius Pulcher, and -- in Cicero's immortal description in the *Pro Caelio* -- "a Palatine Medea" and "a two-penny Clytemnestra." She was older, politically connected, brilliant, and apparently devastating. Catullus was finished from the start.

The relationship produced poetry the way a fire produces heat -- as a natural byproduct of something that could not help burning. Over what seems to have been several years, Catullus wrote about Lesbia in every emotional register: adoration (*da mi basia mille* -- give me a thousand kisses), jealousy, wounded pride, contempt, and the terrible lucidity of *odi et amo* ("I hate and I love") -- a whole philosophy of feeling compressed into three words and one pentameter.

A note on his temperament: Catullus is that friend who, if a mosquito brushes against him, does not swat it. He waits, picks up a stylus, and within the hour has set the mosquito on fire in eleven-syllable lines -- with mythological references, a formal dedication, and a curse on the mosquito's entire lineage. Carmina 16, 36, 37, 39, 40, 54 -- the list of people and things he publicly immolated in verse is long, and the quality of the invective is, despite (or because of) the occasions, often extraordinary.

There was grief alongside the fire. His brother died young, far from home, buried near the Troad in the shadow of Troy. This loss surfaces throughout the poems -- at the end of an elegy about Lesbia, in the middle of a learned mythological poem -- like a bruise you keep finding. In 57 BC, Catullus traveled to Bithynia (modern Turkey) on the staff of the governor Gaius Memmius -- yes, the same Memmius to whom Lucretius had recently dedicated the *De Rerum Natura*. Catullus went hoping to make money; Memmius gave him essentially nothing. He did visit his brother's grave and produced what may be the finest short elegy in Latin.

He died around 54 BC, probably before thirty. He left one book.

---

## Most Important Works

The *Liber Catulli* -- the Book of Catullus -- is a collection of 116 carmina organized in three broad groups, probably assembled by an editor working from Catullus's papers after his death.

**Carmina 1-60, the polymetric poems:** Short lyrics in varied meters, dominated by the Phalecian hendecasyllable. The range within this group is remarkable: from playful trifles (dinner invitations, affectionate jibes at friends) to savage invective to some of the most luminous love poetry in Latin. This is where most readers meet Catullus first, and rightly so.

**Carmina 61-68, the carmina docta (learned poems):** Longer, formally ambitious, metrically diverse, packed with literary allusion. Two epithalamia (wedding hymns), the great *epyllion* of carme 64 (the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, with its famous digression on Ariadne abandoned on Naxos), and the complex elegies of 65-68. This is the neoteric program operating at full scale.

**Carmina 69-116, the epigrammatic poems:** Elegiac couplets, mostly short, ranging from love poems to pointed social observation to outright abuse. The tone shifts markedly here toward the sharper, more compressed mode.

Recurring themes across all three groups: love (Lesbia above all), friendship (the beautiful carme 50 to Calvus), grief (the brother poems), literary rivalry, invective, wit, and the persistent project of giving small things the full weight they deserve.

---

## The Neoteroi: Poetry About Everything

The word *neoteroi* (νεώτεροι, "the newer ones") comes from Cicero, who applied it to a cohort of poets turning their backs on Roman epic ambitions and writing polished, personal, Alexandrian-influenced verse. Cicero did not mean it as a compliment. Catullus wore it as a badge.

The intellectual foundation was Callimachus, the great Hellenistic poet and chief librarian of Alexandria (c. 310-240 BC), who famously held that "a big book is a big evil" (μέγα βιβλίον μέγα κακόν). Against the vast gravity-laden epic in the Ennian tradition, Callimachus championed the short, refined, densely allusive poem -- technically perfect, personally voiced. The neoteroi adopted this program wholesale: brevity over grandeur, polish over volume, the individual over the civic.

In practice, this meant stepping away from political and celebratory poetry. Catullus was not interested in Caesar's Gallic campaigns (though he mentions Caesar -- dismissively). He was interested in a sparrow's death, in the pleasure of a dinner, in his brother's grave, in the exact texture of falling out of love.

Catullus makes his position explicit. In carme 36 he consigns Volusius's *Annales* to the flames, calling them *cacata carta* -- a phrase that translates roughly as "toilet-paper verse." In carme 95 he praises Cinna's *Zmyrna* (nine years in the making, Callimachean to the core) over Volusius's *Annales*, which will "serve to wrap mackerel." The joke is pointed: epic scope is not the measure of a poem's value.

But the deeper argument -- the one that makes the neoteroi a movement rather than a fashion -- is about dignity. When Catullus writes an epic-scale lament for a sparrow, he is not being playfully ironic. He is making an argument: love's objects deserve love's full attention, and a poet who brings the complete weight of his craft to a small subject is doing more serious work than one who applies lazy grandeur to a large one. The dignity of a poem is not in its subject; it is in what the poet does with it. Every subject is deserving of poetry. Poetry about everyday life deserves the same dignity as an epic about Troy.

---

## Writing Style and Difficulty

Reading Catullus is not one experience but three.

The **polymetric poems** (carmina 1-60) are among the most accessible entry points in all of classical Latin. The Phalecian hendecasyllable -- eleven syllables, with a predictable rhythmic pattern once internalized -- is one of the friendlier lyric meters to work with. Syntax is direct, vocabulary is mostly standard literary Latin leavened with colloquialisms, and the emotional content is immediate. A student who has finished Caesar can, with modest effort, read a large portion of carmina 1-60. This is not a slight against Catullus; it is an achievement. He makes the difficult look effortless.

The **epigrammatic poems** (69-116) are similarly navigable. Elegiac couplets (hexameter + pentameter) structure the reading, and the compression here comes from irony and implication rather than syntactic obscurity. The challenge is tonal -- Catullus can shift from bitter to tender to obscene within a single poem -- rather than grammatical.

The **carmina docta** (61-68) are a different matter. Carme 64 in particular -- 408 lines of dactylic hexameter -- is dense with Alexandrian allusion, extended periodic syntax, rare vocabulary, and a narrative structure that deliberately interrupts itself and resumes from unexpected angles. The difficulty here approaches the lower end of B tier: not because the Latin is hostile (it does not have Tacitus's venom or Horace's compression) but because navigating it fully requires both grammatical competence and a working knowledge of Hellenistic mythology and literary tradition.

Throughout the collection, Catullus's language is one of its most distinctive features. He ranges from elevated poetic diction to the warm softening of diminutives (*ocelli* for eyes, *miselle* for "poor little," *turgiduli* for "slightly swollen," the onomatopoeic *pipiabat* for a sparrow's chirp) to Greek-derived coinages (*basiationes*, "little kisses"; *venustiorum*, "of more refined persons") to outright obscenity. Carme 16 opens with a line so explicit it was expurgated from most printed editions until the mid-twentieth century -- and is simultaneously a poetic manifesto. Catullus argues that the poet himself must be decent, but his poems have no such obligation. This is not shock for its own sake; it is a defense of the right to write about everything, at any register, with full artistic force. The sparrow and the obscenity sit on the same spectrum: both assert that whatever the poet takes seriously deserves to be taken seriously by the poem.

### Excerpt 1: Carmina 3 -- The Death of Lesbia's Sparrow

**Source:** The Latin Library (thelatinlibrary.com/catullus.shtml)

**Latin Text:**

> Lugete, o Veneres Cupidinesque,
> et quantum est hominum venustiorum:
> passer mortuus est meae puellae,
> passer, deliciae meae puellae,
> quem plus illa oculis suis amabat.
> nam mellitus erat suamque norat
> ipsam tam bene quam puella matrem,
> nec sese a gremio illius movebat,
> sed circumsiliens modo huc modo illuc
> ad solam dominam usque pipiabat.
> qui nunc it per iter tenebricosum
> illuc, unde negant redire quemquam.
> at vobis male sit, malae tenebrae
> Orci, quae omnia bella devoratis:
> tam bellum mihi passerem abstulistis.
> o factum male! o miselle passer!
> tua nunc opera meae puellae
> flendo turgiduli rubent ocelli.

**English Translation:**

Mourn, all you Venuses and Cupids,
and everyone of any refinement:
the sparrow of my girl is dead --
the sparrow, darling of my girl,
whom she loved more than her own eyes.
He was sweet as honey and knew his mistress
as well as a girl knows her own mother;
he never stirred from her lap,
but hopping about now here, now there,
he chirped always to his mistress alone.
Now he travels the dark road
to where, they say, none return.
Curses on you, wicked shadows
of Orcus, who devour all lovely things:
you have taken from me so lovely a sparrow.
O dreadful deed! O poor little sparrow!
Because of you my girl's
little eyes are red and swollen from weeping.

**Analysis:**

The poem opens like an epic invocation: "Lugete, o Veneres Cupidinesque" -- mourn, divine powers of love. The same register in which a Roman poet might summon the Muses before a battle, Catullus deploys to summon the Venuses and Cupids before a bird's death. This is the neoteric manifesto in miniature: the scale of treatment is dictated not by the subject's importance in the cosmic order, but by its importance to the lover.

The first ten lines are almost playful in their tenderness. The sparrow "knew its mistress as well as a girl knows her own mother" (*norat ipsam tam bene quam puella matrem*); it hopped "now here, now there" (*modo huc modo illuc* -- the repeated *modo* mimicking quick darting motion); it "chirped always to its mistress alone" (*ad solam dominam usque pipiabat* -- the onomatopoeic *pipiabat* being one of the most charming verbs in Latin). Diminutives pile up and soften: *deliciae* (darling), *mellitus* (honey-sweet), and at the end *miselle* (poor little), *turgiduli* (slightly swollen), *ocelli* (little eyes). The language is deliberately small-scaled, tender, almost childlike.

Then line 11 turns: *qui nunc it per iter tenebricosum* -- "who now travels the dark road." The imagery is straight from epic death poetry; the underworld here is the same place from which no hero returns, described with the gravity appropriate to fallen warriors. The joke is not that this is funny. It is that it works. The sparrow's death earns the language, because the grief it causes is genuine.

The final two lines are the whole point: *flendo turgiduli rubent ocelli* -- "from weeping, her little eyes are swollen and red." Catullus is not grieving the sparrow. He is grieving because Lesbia is grieving. The sparrow matters because Lesbia matters. And Lesbia's tears are, to Catullus, a catastrophe of the first order. The epic machinery was never about the bird; it was always about her.

### Excerpt 2: Carmina 101 -- A Brother's Grave

**Source:** The Latin Library (thelatinlibrary.com/catullus.shtml)

**Latin Text:**

> Multas per gentes et multa per aequora vectus
> advenio has miseras, frater, ad inferias,
> ut te postremo donarem munere mortis
> et mutam nequiquam alloquerer cinerem,
> quandoquidem fortuna mihi tete abstulit ipsum,
> heu miser indigne frater adempte mihi,
> nunc tamen interea haec, prout ea consuetudo,
> tradita sunt tristi munere ad inferias,
> accipe fraterno multum manantia fletu,
> atque in perpetuum, frater, ave atque vale.

**English Translation:**

Carried through many peoples and many seas,
I arrive here, brother, at these wretched rites,
to give you the last gift that death allows
and speak in vain to your silent ashes.
Since fortune has taken you, yourself, from me --
O poor brother, torn from me undeservedly --
now at least these offerings, as old custom has it,
are given as a sad tribute at funeral rites.
Receive them, flowing with a brother's many tears,
and forever, brother: hail and farewell.

**Analysis:**

This is an entirely different Catullus -- no wit, no sparrows, no diminutives, no fire. The poem opens in the register of epic: *multas per gentes et multa per aequora vectus* scans almost as a dactylic hexameter, and the word *vectus* ("carried") immediately sets a passive, grief-laden tone. Catullus has been carried by circumstances, by fate, by the fact of his brother's death, to this distant grave. The word order places "many peoples" and "many seas" before the speaker arrives; the journey absorbs the first line before the act of arriving occupies the second.

The formal structure is ten lines of elegiac couplets -- entirely controlled, the emotion contained within strict form as grief tends to be in Roman poetry. The contrast between the scale of the journey (many peoples, many seas) and the smallness of the arrival (*has miseras inferias* -- "these wretched funeral offerings") is the poem's structural spine. He has come so far to give so little. The ashes cannot speak back: *mutam nequiquam alloquerer cinerem* -- the ashes are *mutam* (silent, feminine singular modifying *cinerem*), the speaking *nequiquam* (in vain). The whole poem is a necessary futility.

Line 5 has the emphatic *tete* -- the doubled form of *te*, used for stress -- "fortune has taken *you yourself* from me," underlining the irreplaceability of the loss. Line 6 adds *indigne*: not "my brother has died" but "my brother has been wronged by death," the passive participle *adempte* ("taken away") casting death as a theft.

The final line is the whole poem in ten syllables: *atque in perpetuum, frater, ave atque vale.* The Roman greeting (*ave*, said on meeting) and the Roman farewell (*vale*, said on parting) in the same breath -- because there will be no more meetings after this. This one farewell must stand for all time. The repetition of *frater* (brother) across the poem -- three times in ten lines -- is insistent, a word summoned against silence.

### Excerpt 3: Carmina 64.1--7 -- The Opening of the Peleus and Thetis Epyllion

**Source:** The Latin Library (thelatinlibrary.com/catullus.shtml)

**Latin Text:**

> Peliaco quondam prognatae vertice pinus
> dicuntur liquidas Neptuni nasse per undas
> Phasidos ad fluctus et fines Aeetaeos,
> cum lecti iuvenes, Argiuae robora pubis,
> auratam optantes Colchis avertere pellem
> ausi sunt vada salsa cita decurrere puppi,
> caerula verrentes abiegnis aequora palmis.

**English Translation:**

The pines once born on the summit of Pelion
are said to have swum through Neptune's clear waters
to the streams of Phasis and the Aeetaean bounds,
when the chosen young men, the strength of Argive youth,
hoping to carry off the golden fleece from Colchis,
dared to race across the salt shallows in their swift ship,
sweeping the blue waters with oars of silver fir.

**Analysis:**

Seven lines, one main verb. *Ausi sunt... decurrere* ("they dared to race") does not arrive until line 6; everything before it is subordination -- relative clause, indirect statement, purpose participle, temporal clause -- piling context on context before the act itself. This is the Alexandrian mode at its most characteristic: the interest is not in the event but in the texture and weight of the event. Every element of the Argo's launch matters: the timber (pines from Pelion's summit), the sea (Neptune's clear waters), the destination (Phasis, and "the Aeetaean bounds" -- Aeetes, king of Colchis, father of Medea, named obliquely and without explanation), the crew (chosen young men, the *robora* -- the oaken strength -- of Argive youth), the goal (the golden fleece), and finally the image of the oars themselves: *caerula verrentes abiegnis aequora palmis* -- "sweeping the blue waters with oars of silver fir," where *palmis* (literally "palms of the hand," hence oar-blades) is precisely the kind of precise, unexpected image that marks a Callimachean poet.

Compared to the short poems, the differences are immediate. The periodic syntax requires holding an unresolved sentence in mind for six lines before it closes. The mythological references require background (who is Aeetes? what is the Phasian shore?). The diction is elevated throughout, with none of the colloquial warmth of the polymetrics. This is why the carmina docta push toward B tier even as the bulk of Catullus sits comfortably at C: here he is writing in a different mode entirely, for a reader who has done the preparation.

## The Neoteric Circle: Brief Notes

The poets in Catullus's circle shared his Callimachean program but survive today largely as names and fragments -- which is precisely why they produce only one answer in a difficulty ranking.

**Gaius Licinius Calvus** (c. 82-47 BC): Catullus's closest friend and poetic alter ego. A respected orator (Cicero rated him highly as a rival at the bar) who also wrote personal verse, including love poems to a woman named Quintilia. Catullus writes warmly and admiringly to him in carme 14, 50, and 96. Almost nothing of his poetry survives -- a handful of fragments quoted by later grammarians for linguistic interest, enough to confirm the style but not to assess it. **Tier: NC.**

**Marcus Furius Bibaculus** (c. 103 -- after 27 BC): The senior figure in the neoteric set, known for satirical epigrams that apparently included pointed material about Julius Caesar. A few fragments survive, preserved mainly for their linguistic curiosity rather than literary completeness. **Tier: NC.**

**Helvius Cinna** (fl. 60s-44 BC): Author of the *Zmyrna*, the Callimachean ideal made flesh -- a brief, densely learned *epyllion* that took nine years to write and was praised extravagantly by Catullus in carme 95. Helvius Cinna was killed in the chaos following Caesar's assassination, mistaken by a mob for the conspirator Cinna the praetor. Shakespeare preserves the episode: *I am Cinna the poet, I am Cinna the poet -- yet they tear me for my bad verses.* Almost nothing of the *Zmyrna* survives. **Tier: NC.**

**Cornificius** (dates uncertain, probably died c. 41 BC): Addressed affectionately by Catullus (carme 38) and later a correspondent of Cicero. Wrote poetry, now essentially lost. **Tier: NC.**

## Legacy and Impact

Catullus's most direct heirs are the Latin elegists of the next generation. Tibullus, Propertius, and Ovid all absorbed his emotional register, his first-person love poetry, his Greek models, and his conviction that the personal is a legitimate subject for high art. Propertius opens his first book with an unmistakable nod: *Cynthia prima suis miserum me cepit ocellis* -- even the diminutive *ocellis* is Catullan. Ovid in the *Amores* and *Ars Amatoria* could not exist without Catullus's example. The entire tradition of Latin love elegy is, in a real sense, Catullus's aftermath.

The collection survived antiquity in a single manuscript, the *Codex Veronensis*, associated with Verona. This manuscript was apparently rediscovered around 1300, when it briefly resurfaced before being lost or dispersed again; all surviving copies of Catullus descend from copies made of it during that narrow window. The entire textual tradition hangs by one thread.

Around 1400, the humanist Guarino of Verona obtained a copy and helped circulate the text among scholars. But the most significant pre-Guarino reader was Francesco Petrarch, who encountered the Veronensis manuscript in the fourteenth century and studied it closely. The influence on the *Canzoniere* -- Petrarch's sequence of 366 poems addressed to Laura -- is not coincidental: the sustained lyric sequence organized around a single, idealized, largely unattainable beloved woman is Catullan to its core. Petrarch elevated the Catullan emotional architecture into the defining model of European love poetry for the next three centuries. Every Elizabethan sonnet sequence, every Renaissance canzoniere, every poet who ever wrote about unrequited love for a woman named after a laurel tree is, at some remove, downstream of Catullus's Lesbia.

Catullus quickly became central to Renaissance humanism more broadly: his combination of classical precision, personal directness, and emotional range made him immediately recognizable. The explicitness of several poems (carme 16 above all) caused predictable difficulties for early printers; expurgated editions were common until the mid-twentieth century, and some left explicit gaps where the offensive lines would have been. The modern scholarly consensus is that the explicit poems are among the most important in the corpus -- not despite their obscenity but because of what the obscenity argues: that a poet's full voice, including its least polite registers, is inseparable from his art.

## Proposed Tier

**Tier: C (Manageable)**

Catullus sits at C tier across the collection, with the carmina docta touching the lower edge of B.

**Medium and meter:** The polymetric poems use the Phalecian hendecasyllable, one of the more accessible lyric meters; the epigrammatic poems use elegiac couplets. The carmina docta add dactylic hexameter and rarer lyric meters. Averaged across the whole corpus, the metrical burden settles at C.

**Lexicon:** Standard literary Latin in the short poems, supplemented by colloquialisms, diminutives, Greek coinages, and occasional obscenity. The carmina docta add rarer vocabulary and denser allusion to Hellenistic sources. Averaged: C to low B.

**Syntactic complexity:** The short poems are syntactically clear -- Catullus at his most direct is almost conversational. The carmina docta use extended periodic structures (see the carme 64 opening above). Averaged: C.

**Allusion density:** Low in the polymetrics, high in the carmina docta (dense Alexandrian mythological apparatus requiring familiarity with Hellenistic models). Averaged: C.

**Why not B?** B tier requires sustained difficulty across multiple criteria. Catullus delivers that only in the carmina docta, a portion of the whole. A student reading primarily the polymetrics or epigrams will not encounter B-tier resistance. The entry point is too generous.

**Why not D?** Because the carmina docta exist and cannot be ignored in any honest assessment. Carme 64 is not D-tier reading. And even the short poems reward close attention in ways that D-tier texts do not.

C tier is the right call. Come for carme 3. Stay for carme 64.
